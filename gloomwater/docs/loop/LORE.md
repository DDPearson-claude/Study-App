**The rules this story keeps, in the user's words.** "Art, atmosphere and fishing those are our key drivers." "There's no way to fail." "For now park the creature attacks." Buying a map is "the only way of discovering a new place". The waters grow "progressively more and more lush and green" and "physically morph bit by bit into a more natural greener environment and landscape". Original work only. The story explains the fishing loop; it changes none of it. Nothing in it is fought, and nothing in it can be lost.

**The story in five sentences.**
1. The world has been stuck at evening since long before anyone remembers, so the people of the warm south carried a flame from their first hearth north, water by water, lighting a lamp at each, and hung it in a great lantern over the Mossmere to give themselves a day.
2. One night the lantern's old chain parted; nothing broke it, it was old; the lantern fell, its weight let the great sluice drop shut, the lake rose over the capital, and in the centuries after, every unkept weir, dam, lock and sea-wall of the kingdom let the water back in.
3. A pale fish older than the kingdom caught the ember before the water could put it out; the window candles of the drowning city floated down to the Landing; some of them woke, and they are the Wicks.
4. The people went home the way the fire had come, down the Lamp Road to the green forest where they began, and lived out their days there until the last of them banked their hearth "for morning"; the water kept it, and it grew fins.
5. The Wick, buying Old Murk's charts one at a time, follows the people's road home from the drowned stone capital to the forest where its own wax was made, and the first time it comes home from the Inkwood, the Landing has something like morning.

## 1. The user's questions, answered

**"Draw out a huge continent shaped map. This map is the whole world and it shows how each lake and river is actually connected to one another."**
The world is one continent, the Evenland, about 6,000 km from its cold north coast to its warm south cape, with sea on every side (`WORLD.md`). Every one of the 16 waters lies on one old water road, the Lamp Road, and each is joined to the one before it by a real river, canal, lock stair or stretch of coast that you can trace with a finger: up the kingdom's river into the stone highland, out through a mountain gap to a fjord and the sea, south down the long cold coast, up a great escarpment and down to a sea-gate, then through a swamp canal onto a great brown river and into the forest at its heart. `WORLD.md` gives every water its coordinates on the 1000 × 1400 canvas and the leg that joins it to the next.

**"So It feels like we're actually journeying through a world when we unlock new maps etc."**
Each map you buy is pinned onto the Chart at its true place, and it brings the river or coast that joins it to the last water, so the continent draws itself in the order you travel. The land goes from cold stone to forest because the route really runs from a cold coast through a dry one into the wet tropics. And the story is a road: you are following the people's way home, and each water shows one more chapter of it.

**"Make it feel natural and real and that it follows our story arc."**
The geography obeys real rules: rivers run downhill and cross ranges only by gaps, canals cross the low divides, the west coast is dry because of a cold current and a rain shadow, the fjords are wet under the westerlies, the coral grows where warm water meets a clear coast, and the forest at the heart is a blackwater basin under trade-wind rain. The drowning is real too: the kingdom held its water back with sluices, weirs, dams, locks and sea-walls, and when nobody was left to keep them, the water came back in. The arc follows the land: the dead stone north is where the light fell, and the green south is where it began.

**"Why actually is our universe were in?"**
The sun went down long ago and has not come back; nobody knows why, and the game never says. The world has been at evening ever since: the violet-dusk sky, the lavender moon and the clear night air are the same on every water because they are the only sky there is. A kingdom of people made its own day with a great lantern lit from their first hearth. When it fell, their capital drowned, their works failed, and the water took the land back. The water keeps what it takes, and what it keeps grows fins: that is why fish glow, why the great fish each keep a lost light or a lost job, and why a candle can wake.

**"What is our story arc?"**
A candle's walk home, told in four acts of four waters. Act I, *What Fell*: the drowned capital and what the lantern held up. Act II, *The Road of Farewells*: the cold road the kingdom sent its dead down, and the lamps still lit along it. Act III, *Where They Turned*: the working coast, the open sea-gate and the fleet the people began and did not finish, because they chose to go home. Act IV, *The Way Home*: the canal and the barge road to the forest where the kingdom began and ended, where the last of the people banked a fire for morning. The ending is not a victory: the Wick brings that fire's warmth home, and the Landing has something like morning. Then the evenings go on (§8).

**"Are there humans?"**
Not any more. There are none anywhere on the Chart, and the game never shows one.

**"We're there ever humans in this universe?"**
Yes. The people of the Lamplit Kingdom were people like us: tall, warm-handed folk (a Wick stands about knee-high to them) who planted orchards and kept bees, made candles, and built every moat, garden, lock, dam, lighthouse, harbour, canal and barge road the Wick sails through. They never carved their own faces, because they thought it unlucky to be remembered by a face, so the game shows them only by their works, their cloth-veiled statues, their names on the Long Pier and what the fish remember. Nothing killed them: some drowned when the capital went under, the rest went home to the forest where they began and, over many lifetimes of cold evening, grew fewer and ended, the way a candle does.

**"Where are all the other wicks or people or frogs or whatever"**
- **Other Wicks:** four more candles stand by the Landing hearth (Taper, Stub, Tallow, Ashwick), cold until each catches, then dozing lit there. About a dozen more sit in the far lamps along the road, keeping them lit; you only ever see their lamps greet you once. The candles that went out lie cold under the water. One, the Wayfarer, went farther than any chart and has not come back.
- **People:** gone (above). Their works stand in every water.
- **Frogs:** ordinary frogs, the south's voice. None in the stone north, a few in Sporewood's warmth, a chorus by the Siltway, a roar in the Inkwood.
- **Toads:** Old Murk is a toad of the warm south, centuries old, the last trader on the road. A few of his kin live quietly in Lanternroot Lagoon's stilt village. You never meet them: you see their lanterns, and one green one among the amber. "My lot live there. We don't talk."

## 2. The world, and why it is like this

**2.1 The long evening.** One evening, long before the kingdom, the sun went down over the Lampless Sea and did not come back up. Nobody knows why. Since then the world has stood at evening: the violet-dusk sky, the lavender moon and the clear night air. This is why every trip is an evening, why the big fish "come up at dusk. They always did.", why Sporewood is "A Garden That Forgot the Sun", why the Remembered Sun is "like a sun it has only heard about", and why the Banked Fire "waits for morning". What the loop calls late day is only the palest hour of the long evening, never a warm sun. A few things are older than the kingdom and were alive before the evening: the Long Winter ("It was old when the kingdom was built"), the Lantern-Swallower ("Older than the kingdom above") and the Well of Ledgers ("Older than the Vault. Waiting for the sky.").

**2.2 The first hearth and the Lamp Road.** The people lived first in the Green Heart, the low, warm, wet basin at the middle of the southern lobe, where the evening stayed warmest. They kept orchards and bees, and their first hearth burned there. They would not sit in the dusk. So they took a flame from that hearth, shut it in a lantern, and carried it north by water, the only roads they had: up the barge river, across the swamp gap, along the coast, up the great escarpment, up the cold coast and in through a mountain gap to the stone highland. At every water they stopped, they lit a lamp from it and left it burning. That chain of lamps is **the Lamp Road**, and it is the Wick's route, in order, from its far end back to its start. On the highland's north lip, over the lake in front of the Sunken Gate, they hung the flame in **the great lantern**. That lake is the Mossmere, and that city is the capital, the kingdom's seat. The forest where the fire began is its heart. (The capital is where the kingdom ended; the green heart is where it began. Both are true.)

**2.3 The kingdom.** The Lamplit Kingdom held the whole continent along that road. It was a kingdom that held its water back: the capital's lake kept low by a great sluice in the outer wall, gardens and a crystal vault in the highland basins, a summit canal, a royal weir on a glacier lake, a lighthouse on the outer rock, pyre stairs in the snow, a harbour town on land won from the sea behind a mole, a lock stair down the escarpment, a great dam, a sea-gate, a straight canal, a barge road with mile-posts, and orchards on dikes in the forest. Every lamp in it, every window candle, and every Wick was lit from the one flame.

**2.4 The fall.** The great lantern hung on chains over the Gate's towers, and its weight held the great sluice open, so the lake drained through the outer wall and the lower city stood dry. One evening the chain's last link wore through. There was no enemy, no curse and no crime: "Nothing broke it. It was old." The lantern fell into its own reflection. With its weight gone, the sluice gate dropped shut. The Gate's windows guttered out row by row while their reflections stayed lit a beat longer, the lake's memory. Over that night and the days after, the Mossmere rose over the capital to the arches, until it spilled over the outer wall and fell into the cove as the Sluice Run, which is how it falls still. The film shows all of it in one moment. On the Toppled Bridge the chain still hangs, ending in one worn-thin link.

**2.5 The drowning, slowly.** "The lantern falls. The kingdom follows." The capital went in a night; the rest went over centuries. With the capital drowned and its keepers gone, nobody kept the works. The royal weir broke and the Thin Water spread over its sand. The lock stair broke and the river fell down it. The mole gave way and the sea took the harbour town back, and coral took its roofs. The dikes in the forest went and the river took the orchards. The delta's edge sank with nobody to bank it, and the orchard by the sea became a mangrove lagoon. The fall was about four hundred years ago (coral over a harbour town and a forest through an orchard take that long). The folk say it another way: "The water rose to look for it." That is a saying, never a rule of the game.

**2.6 The pale fish.** The Lantern-Swallower lived under the kingdom all its life, in the Bottomless of the Pallid Deep. On the night of the fall it came down the King's Cut and the Kingsflow and caught the ember as it sank, before the water could drown it, and carried it home to the Bottomless. It is not a thief. It has kept the ember warm ever since: "The great lantern went down. Still going." The old saying "it fell into her the way a coin falls into a well" was about the Bottomless; the name "Pale Leviathan" survives only in that saying and never ships (§10).

**2.7 The water keeps what it takes.** This is the world's one rule, and it is kind. The lights that went under did not go out; the water kept them, and what the water keeps grows fins. That is why fish glow, why the Glass Sovereign keeps the Vault's last light, why the Jetsam Shark has kept "half the kingdom's lost things", why the Belled Matriarch tolls the drowned bell, and why the creatures still do the kingdom's jobs: the Last Sentry, the Old Shipwright, the Last Lock, the Barge-Shadow. Murk's run line "The water keeps what it takes" sounds like a warning at the start and reads as a kindness at the end.

**2.8 The dark.** The Gloom, which is lore only, is just the cold of the long evening once the great lantern was gone: "only the drowned, missing warmth". It is patient, not cruel. There is nothing in it to fight, and nothing out there hunts the Wick.

**2.9 Why the land greens the farther you go.** The plain reason is the land: the route runs from a cold northern coast and a stone highland, through rain-shadow steppe and a cold-current desert coast greened only by rivers and spray, into the wet tropics, and every drowned work is overgrown at the speed of its climate: bare in the north, furred in the dry west, swallowed in the wet south (`WORLD.md` §3). The story's reason sits on top of it: the north is where the light fell, and the land there still grieves bare; the farther from the fall, the more kindly the world has forgotten, and green is how it forgets kindly ("moss has kept the vigil the candles could not"). The kingdom stopped; the world did not. And the Wick is walking back toward the forest where its fire was first lit. Only things turn green: the sky stays the same evening sky on every water.

**2.10 The sky.** It is a real sky everywhere. The four highland lakes lie in deep basins that the kingdom roofed with stone vaults to hold the lantern's light in; most of the vaulting has fallen, so what old canon calls stalactites and hanging ruins against the stars are broken vaults and hanging roots with the real sky beyond, and the waterfall "from a ceiling crack" falls through a gap in a vault. The Rime Sound is "the first water outside the walls", the first with no roof at all. The Landing alone is a true sea cave with a real ceiling, which is why its warm shaft can open there (§8).

**2.11 The mist and the maps.** Forgetting looks like mist. A water nobody remembers sinks into standing lavender mist and cannot be found. A chart is a remembering: somebody who knew the way drew it. When a living flame holds that chart, the water is reminded, and the mist lets go for good. That is why buying a map is the only way to find a new place, and why only Murk can sell them: his charts are the last memory of those waters. Murk: "A water nobody remembers is hard to find. I remember. That's what you're paying for." (That line is lore for the docs, not on screen.)

**2.12 How an evening reaches a water four thousand kilometres away.** Out past the Landing's ferry gate, the channel runs into the mist, and in the mist every remembered water lies one evening from home. A lit Waystone remembers the way. So the raft sails out of the cove, through the low lavender bank, and comes out at the dock of the water it was sent to, and it comes home the same way. The Chart shows the real road and how far it really is; the mist is how a small candle travels it in an evening. This is the only magic in the world's geography, and it is never explained on screen: the player just sails into the mist and out onto the water.

## 3. The story arc, by acts, over the 16 waters

The arc is a road, walked in unlock order. Each water shows one chapter of the people's life, readable at a glance, and gives one **clue you cannot miss**: a landmark the camera frames during the 3-second mist lift on the first evening there (SPEC §5.1 rule 7). Nothing in the arc needs a legend, a catch or a count; every reveal comes with a map and its landmark, and the legends add beats but open nothing (SPEC §5.1 rule 4).

### Prologue: One Warm Light (the film, then the Landing)

The film plays as it is (its line 3 is rewritten in §12). The lantern falls; the Gate goes dark; a pale flank takes the ember; a Wick's flame catches on a raft with its line already out. The Wick wakes by the hearth among cold candles at the Landing, "where the last Wicks came ashore". It is not the first Wick to wake there, and the tally board says so: Murk's old figures, other Wicks' catches, centuries of them. Murk: "Awake, little Wick? Good. Fish sell. Clams buy. That's the whole of it." It is not the whole of it, and he knows. Once, far down the channel, a single warm point pulses: the ember (canon). The First Outing goes to the Mossmere because it is the nearest water to home and the door the last candles came out of.

*How the player learns it:* the film only, plus three things in the Landing with no words: the cold candles at the hearth, the old figures on the tally board, the warm point far down the channel.

## 4. Humans, then and now

**Then.** The people of the Lamplit Kingdom were humans: tall people of the warm south. A Wick (0.6 m) stands about knee-high to them, and their doors are four Wicks tall. They planted orchards and kept bees in the Green Heart, made candles from the wax, carried their first fire north, and built the whole road. They fished at evening, by the basketful, laughing. They burned their dead on the pyre stairs in the snow and sent them west with a candle on each brow, and they carved their names on the Long Pier ("Every name on the pier was somebody's."). They never carved their own faces, because they thought it unlucky to be remembered by a face; they carved cloth instead (the Two Who Kneel, "no faces at all, only cloth"; the stone hands with the empty bowl). That is why the game never shows a human face or figure, and it never should. On screen they are only ever "the kingdom", "the people", "somebody", "the gardeners", "the dead". The word "human" never appears.

**What happened to them.** No catastrophe killed them and nothing hunted them. Some drowned in the night the capital went under. The rest, with no lantern, no capital and no day, went home the way their fire had come: down the Lamp Road, past the lighthouse and down the long coast, up the escarpment and down to the sea-gate. There they began a fleet to follow the sun and their dead west, and stopped. They turned inland by the canal and went down the barge road with everything they could carry. The last barge, which Murk poled, sank at the great bend; they walked the rest of the way. In the Green Heart, over many lifetimes of cold evening, they grew fewer and ended, slowly, the way a candle does. Before the last of them were gone, they banked their hearth-fire for morning. There are no graves to find: the forest has them.

**What is left of them.** Everything they built, in all sixteen waters. Their lamps, still burning. The creatures still doing their jobs (the Last Sentry, the Old Shipwright, the Last Lock, the Barge-Shadow, the Lampwarden Angler "still waiting for someone to come home"). The things that miss them (the Rushlight common, the Paper-Lantern Jelly, Emberbelly under the Kitchen Stair, the lantern-gourds still ripening). Murk's pack: empty birdcages, dead lanterns, a crooked umbrella, "Everything here was somebody's last hope once." The Banked Fire. And the Wicks themselves: their bees' wax, lit from their fire.

**Now.** None. None on any sheet, and none will ever appear. The blank east of the Chart and the world beyond the far isle are room for later sheets, not a promise that anyone is there. The game never says whether anyone lives beyond the map, and it should not.

## 5. The Wicks, and where the others are

**5.1 What a Wick is.** In the kingdom every house set a small, fat candle in its window at evening, lit from the great lantern, so that whoever was out on the water could find the way home. The candles were made of orchard-bee wax from the Green Heart, so every Wick is a small piece of the forest at the end of the road, and going south is going back to where it was made. On the night of the fall the capital's window candles went out row by row (the film); some were carried out of the windows on the rising water, down the Sluice Run, and washed up in the old ferry cove under the outer wall: the Landing. Most went cold. Some did not. A candle lit for a goodbye goes out (the funeral tapers at Pyrefall, the candle-rafts on the Pallid Deep). A candle lit for someone coming home keeps waiting. These waited so long, with nobody left to come home, that one evening they took the ferrymen's rafts and rods and went out on the water themselves. "No one ever told it that it couldn't."

**5.2 Why a Wick stays lit.** A window candle's whole life is someone going out on the water at evening and coming home. A Wick keeps that going by doing both. While it has a reason to go out and something to bring home, it burns. When it stops, it does not die: it goes cold and dozes by the hearth, and a cold Wick can catch again from a warm one ("It went out once. It came back angry."). That is the story's way of saying there is no way to fail. Nothing runs out, nothing needs feeding, and no trip ends because a flame runs low.

**5.3 Where the other Wicks are.**
1. **You.** The latest candle to catch at the Landing, and very likely not the first time this candle has burned. Murk's "Fished it off a Wick like you. Near enough like you." is allowed to mean exactly that, and is never explained.
2. **The four at the hearth: Taper, Stub, Tallow and Ashwick.** The other last candles who washed ashore and went cold by the fire. They catch one at a time, as yours did, on SPEC §10.1's triggers (12 species in the Journal, the Sporewood map, 5 Trophies, a giant landed). The reason, never written on screen: a candle catches from a candle that has come home with something to tell, and each trigger is something worth telling (a full log, a new water and its warm glowcap keepsake, trophies on the wall, a giant). Each catch is shown, not explained: the flame catching as the player's did. The ones not out on the water doze lit by the hearth.
3. **The lamplighters.** About a dozen Wicks sit in the far lamps along the road, keeping them lit: some since the kingdom sent them out to light the far lamps from the one fire, and some who fished from the Landing long ago, went farther than their charts and sat down in a lamp that had gone dark (that is where the Old Ledger's other Wicks went). The Sunken Gate's lit slits, the Lantern Hole's lantern, the sluice lamp, the lighthouse, the pyres, the two tower lamps, the dam lamp, the harbour-light stump, the Sunk Ferry's lamp under the water. They never wake, speak, move or ask for anything. In close views a small candle shape shows in the flame, and on the first evening at some waters a lamp greets the raft once (§3). That is all they ever do.
4. **The cold ones in the water.** The candles that went out on the night of the fall, and the dead's candles that went out long before it, lie cold in the Pallid Deep and the drowned towns. The 30 drifting candle-rafts (the Pallid Deep's Waystone keepsake) are these. If the parked walking build returns, its sister-candles are these, and the parked Shrine of Drowned Lanterns is where their keepsakes would go.
5. **The Wayfarer (parked with walking).** The Wick who went farthest down the road and has not come back. "It never found the bottom. It found every road on the way." Its place by the hearth holds only an old wax drip. (Data change: §10.)
6. **The First Wick** (the relic "Stub of the First Wick") is the first window candle ever lit, in the Inkwood's stilt village, from the first hearth. It is a story the others tell, never a character. The Inkwood's wax-ringed niches are where it stood.

## 6. The folk of the world (none of them enemies)

Nobody in the world is an enemy. Everyone is kind, busy or asleep. Only Murk speaks.

**Old Murk.** An enormous umber toad of the warm south, centuries old ("I've had centuries. Fewer than I'd like."). The old toads are a long-lived people who were the kingdom's neighbours and traders, never its subjects. Murk was the barge-trader who plied the whole Lamp Road, from the orchards up the barge road, through the canal and the sea-gate, up the coast and the cold road to the capital. He saw the lantern fall and the people's long leaving, and he poled their last barge south; it sank under him at the great bend, he swam, and they walked on. When they were gone he drew the last chart himself, then came back up the road alone, to the cove where the candles had washed ashore, because somebody had to keep the hearth. He has kept it ever since, and the Ledger with it: every Wick's catch for four hundred years in his own hand. He is the last trader on the road, so "I'm the only shop left" is plainly true.

- **His charts** are the kingdom's own sheets, collected in trade over centuries, in four hands: court surveyors' (reach I), funeral priests' (reach II), harbour-masters' (reach III) and barge pilots' (reach IV); the Inkwood's is his own clumsy drawing. He seals each with candle wax pressed into the shape of the water's legend, because he names waters for whoever lives there.
- **He buys fish** because fish are what the water gives back, and in a world where the water keeps everything, that is the only trade left. He renders some for the oil that keeps the Landing's lamps and hearth lit (lore only: no meter, ever), and smokes the rest. "First oil's on me. The rest you fish for." is the hearth's oil, a gift.
- **Clams** are the old toads' shell money, dug from the Siltway's beds, and the only money left: the kingdom's coins drowned with it (they lie in the Coin Run, thrown into the Pyre for the dead).
- **He lets the great ones go.** He counts each legend twice, pays for her, and that night lets her slip back into the water at the gate; only her print stays. That is why a legend can be met again on a later evening (SPEC's repeat landings). The sale, the price and the hold do not change (§7.4).
- **His green lamp** is a southern swamp-lamp, the one green light in the game: the south's colour carried north.
- **He never says any of this outright.** He is wry and tender, and his kindness is never stated: "Murk is not a charity. Murk is barely a toad." The prices that are always just reachable, the free first oil, the legends let go.

**The old toads (Murk's kin).** A few very old toads live quietly in Lanternroot Lagoon's stilt village. They are never seen whole and never speak. Their signs: lanterns lit at every dusk, one green among the amber; scraps on the offering posts ("The stilt village feeds it and asks nothing."); a moored barge; a slow smudge of pipe smoke; a porch bell answering the Wick's rod bell once. They do not trade, so Murk is still the only shop. In the Inkwood they still drop scraps from the Kitchen Stair for Emberbelly, out of old habit. They are not a community to visit, and nothing human is ever drawn there.

**Frogs.** Ordinary small frogs, animals, not folk: the south's voice. None in the stone north; a first few in Sporewood's warmth; reed frogs piping in Rushlight Fen; the first chorus at Lampmoss Slough, heard, not seen; the Siltway's floating meadows "loud with frogs", the loudest place in the game so far; and the Inkwood a full chorus. The world's sound greens with its colour.

**Other living things.** Fireflies from the Thin Water south (kept 6 m from the raft), synchronous at Lanternroot, where some have moved into the paper lanterns. Moths at the Wick's flame. Grey herons from the reach III coast onward, night birds from the Siltway south (Murk's empty cages are a joke that lands late), bats under the Inkwood canopy. Bees: carved over a garden door in Sporewood, remembered on the Siltway ("Somebody down here still remembers bees."), and a real hum in the Inkwood. Bees made the Wicks' wax.

**The keepers: the great fish.** The legends and giants are the world's elders, many older than the kingdom, and each keeps a light or a job: the Belled Matriarch tolls for the drowned; the Glass Sovereign keeps the Vault's last light; the Lantern-Swallower keeps the ember; the Long Winter remembers before the kingdom; the Jetsam Shark keeps its lost things; the Ashen Guest attends every funeral; the Last Lock guards the stair; the Waxen Abbot keeps his cellar; the Old Shipwright cuts ribs; the Last Sentry stands his post; the Barge-Shadow follows the last barge; the Silver Regent sighs for the lagoon; the Banked Fire keeps the people's hearth. The wry small ones round them out: the Stone Lodger "has rented the same rock for a hundred years and never paid", the Potlid Turtle, Little Logjaw's amber eyes at the edge of the light.

**Who you meet along the road.** Murk at home. Out on the water, no voices: only the lamps, the keepers, the frogs and, at the very end, the Banked Fire's breath. The north is empty; the world fills up as it greens.

## 7. Fish, light and clams

**7.1 Why the Wicks fish.** A window candle was lit to wait for a fisher coming home. With no fishers left, it became the fisher. Going out in the evening and coming home again is what keeps a Wick lit (§5.2). The film shows it: the Wick whose flame catches is the one with its line already out. Murk's "Fish sell. Clams buy. That's the whole of it." is wry and also true, and the road corrects it one map at a time.

**7.2 Why a fish is worth anything.** The water keeps what it takes, and what it keeps grows fins. The kingdom's lights and lost things went under on the night of the fall, and the glowing fish (the Candlefin Koi, the glowcap-fed pool fish, the lantern-eyed deep fish) carry a little of that kept light. Every fish brought up is a small piece of the drowned world given back to the air. That is why Murk buys them, why the Landing grows warmer as the Journal fills, and why a cold candle can catch from a Wick with stories to tell.

**7.3 Why fish sell for clams, and maps cost clams.** Murk is the last trader on the road; clams are the only money left; the charts are what he has to sell, and a chart is a memory, the one thing he will not give away. Clams stay the only key: nothing needs a legend, a catch or a count to open a water (SPEC §5.1 rule 4), and no reveal in the story waits on luck.

**7.4 Why the giants and legends matter.** Each legend is the water's memory of one thing the kingdom lost, a light or a job: "That one had a name before you met it." Landing one is meeting a keeper, not taking a treasure. Her lore line is told, her Waystone burns gold, her ink print hangs under the gate's arch, so the Landing slowly fills with the kingdom's kept things, and Murk lets her go home (§6). The two legends that frame the story are both optional: the Lantern-Swallower (water 4) keeps the great lantern's ember, the kingdom's last fire; the Banked Fire (water 16) keeps the people's hearth, its first. Their two prints hang facing each other under the arch: last fire on the lintel, first fire at the keystone. The giants and the ones that got away ("It took the line and the hook and the prayer.") are what make each water deep and worth coming back to.

**7.5 The light, as a theme.** The Wick follows a chain of lights that are all one fire: from the first hearth, to the great lantern, kept alive in the Lantern-Swallower's belly, along the lamps the far Wicks keep, to the Banked Fire breathing in the forest where the fire began. The light never went out. It was spread thin across a long evening, waiting.

## 8. The ending

**8.1 At the Inkwood.** The last chart, drawn in Murk's own hand, lifts the mist on the blackwater forest. On the first evening there, after the mist lift at the Kitchen Stair has framed the ash-covered hearth, the Banked Fire comes up to breathe once, far off in the Breathing Mere's mist: a long warm glow rising through the lavender, its back turning, never the whole fish (SPEC §5.1 rule 6's giant in the mist, made warm). The Wick's flame leans toward it and burns gold for the rest of the evening. Nothing is caught, fought or won; the player fishes the evening as on any other.

**8.2 Coming home: the closing film, "Something Like Morning".** It plays once, on the first trip home from the Inkwood, with any catch or none. It is triggered by the road, not by a fish, so no legend opens anything and there is no way to miss it once the last map is bought. About 18 s, the opening's grammar, no Skip word:
- At the tally Murk counts in silence.
- The Wick sets its flame to the hearth, and the hearth catches gold from it: the people's first fire, home.
- Any candle at the hearth that is still cold catches too, one by one, as the player's did, so every Wick is lit.
- If the Lantern-Swallower has been landed, her pale flank passes far down the channel and the ember answers once, warm.
- A warm shaft opens through the cove ceiling, the game's one warm daylight, and every lamp in the Landing burns gold.
- The Chart opens by itself, and the lamps along the road light gold one by one, from the Inkwood back up the road to the Landing.
- Four lines, one at a time, present tense: "The last of them banked a fire for morning." / "You brought it home." / "Far above, something like morning." / "You are still very small. You are still burning." (The last two are canon.)
- The title card changes to the Landing in its warm shaft. One button, canon: **Keep Drifting**.

**8.3 Why it is only "something like" morning.** The sun does not come back. The sky over the sixteen waters stays the same violet evening, as the art rule demands (SPEC §5.3). Morning exists in one place, the Landing, where the last candles came ashore. Whether it is the long evening at last beginning to turn, or only the people's fire shining up through the cove, the game never says. It is a quiet, true ending: the Wick did not save the world; it kept a promise somebody made before it was lit.

**8.4 Landing the Banked Fire.** The last legend (325 cm) stays the final dream after the film. When she is landed, Murk counts her twice and lets her go; her Waystone burns gold and her print hangs at the keystone facing the Lantern-Swallower's; the tier-3 note on the Breathing Mere's sheet keeps the canon line, "It must come up for air, like any fire. It burns low and waits for morning.", which now reads as a promise kept.

**8.5 After.** The game never ends, because nothing can be lost. Every trip is still an evening ("The big ones come up at dusk. They always did."). Every fish, giant, legend and record stays to be fished; the Wicks take turns; the hearth stays warm and the shaft stays open. Murk's rotating lines gain one: "Morning. Hm. I'd forgotten the colour." The east of the Chart (the Eastern Downs, the Morning Gulf, the Morning Isle) stays blank paper: room for later sheets, and for the oldest question, where the sun went, which no later content should answer cheaply.

**8.6 The old ending.** "The Light Comes Up" (landing the Pale Leviathan or Lantern-Swallower as victory, "Return to the Surface", the title change at water 4) is retired under the loop. Landing the Lantern-Swallower is Act I's quiet beat (§3).

## 9. How the game tells it, with almost no words

The story is carried by things the game already has. The only new on-screen text is one Murk line per lake map, three sheet notes, two Murk lines at home and the closing film's two new lines: about a hundred and fifty words in all, each for sign-off.

1. **The opening film.** "One Warm Light", unchanged but for line 3 (§12).
2. **Landmarks.** Each reach is one chapter of the people's life, readable at a glance: reach I the capital's rooms (gate, garden, treasury of light, burial water); reach II the funeral road (bell-buoys, sluice lamp, lighthouse, pyre stairs); reach III the working coast (harbour, lock stair, dam, sea-gate and unfinished fleet); reach IV the road home (canal, barge road, lit stilt village, kitchen in the forest). Each water has one clue the mist-lift camera frames (§3); these are small asks of the art lanes and must fit `WATERS.md`'s looks and steps. The lamps that still burn are the thread: on every water a warm amber point you can sail toward.
3. **One-time greetings, no text, first evening only.** The sluice lamp brightens; the lighthouse beam pauses; the tower lamps blink together; the stilt lanterns lower and a bell answers. Never twice, never interactive.
4. **Map cards (tier 1).** One line each, the canon lines, at most 8 words.
5. **Murk (tier 2, and home).** One line when each lake map is bought (§3's tables). All canon lines stay. At home, two more: after the ending, "Morning. Hm. I'd forgotten the colour."; on a legend's second landing (optional), "Her again? She likes you."
6. **Sheet notes (tier 3).** After the named fish is landed: the Bottomless, "She caught it before the water could."; the Slipway, "He is not sad. He is busy."; the Long Hole, in Murk's hand, "Sank under me at the bend. I swam. They walked."
7. **Legends.** The catch-card beat (the Wick's flame leaning toward a kept light) and their canon lore lines. Their prints under the arch make the Landing a wall of kept things.
8. **The Waystone sill.** 16 stones in four arcs under the Landing's gate, carved with each water's glyph in road order, so the sill reads as the journey: lit when bought, gold when its legend is landed.
9. **Sound.** Silence and bells in the north; wind and surf on the cold road; spray and heat lightning on the dry coast; frogs, insects and bees growing into a chorus in the south. Murk's hand bell is the sound of home.
10. **The Chart, sheet by sheet** (`WORLD.md` §8):
    - The Chart is the continent, assembled from Murk's sheets on the Map Table. It starts as one small sheet: the Landing, the outer wall, a strip of the Cold Coast and the Sluice Run going up to the Mossmere. Everything else is bare paper under a lavender wash: no coastline, no names. A water you do not own is not in the world (SPEC §5.1 rule 5).
    - Each lake map bought is pinned in place on the continent. It brings its water (the landmark drawn small, its places named), its lamp (an amber dot), its Waystone glyph, and the road that joins it to the water before it (river, canal, lock stair or sea passage, drawn as a spine with flow arrows), plus the coast and relief that water can see. So the continent's shape grows out of the player's own road: the stone highland after reach I; the Spine, the fjord coast and the sea to the far isle after reach II; the dry west coast and the Great Step after reach III; the whole southern basin and the green heart after reach IV.
    - Where an owned road runs on into a water not yet bought, it fades into a small lavender wisp, with no name and no shape (the mist at the Pallid Deep's head that turns out to be the Pyre; the black water at the Seam that turns out to be the Inkwood).
    - Each reach's sheets are in a different hand: court surveyors' fine ink with a little Pilgrim Salmon in the margins, always swimming down the road; funeral priests' route charts with small pyre and candle marks; harbour-masters' sounding charts, numbers in the water; barge pilots' long strip maps; and the Inkwood, Murk's own clumsy sheet. The history shows in the drawing.
    - When the fourth lake map of a reach is bought, a small wordless ink picture appears in the Chart's border, so by the end the border is a frieze of the story: (I) a lantern falling from its chain; (II) a candle in a window beside a pyre; (III) a gate left open and a ship's ribs; (IV) a barge going south under orchard trees. The ending adds a fifth: a shaft of light into a cove.
    - At the end the whole road is there, except the blank east and the margins. At the ending, the lamps light gold one by one along the road.

**The word budget is the main risk.** The myth above is rich; almost all of it stays in this document. If it is not a landmark, a line in §3's tables or a line listed above, it does not go on screen.

"The long way home" points at the journey and at the story at once: every evening the Wick fishes and comes home to the Landing, and across sixteen waters it follows the people's road home to the forest where its wax was made.

The film then reads: "A kingdom hangs a great lantern over its lake." / "The lantern falls. The kingdom follows." / "Its last candles fish the long way home." / "You are one of them."

**Alternates**
- **"Its last candles fish from lamp to lamp."** (40 characters): it names the Lamp Road, the chain of lights the player will follow water by water, and it is the most concrete of the three.
- **"Its last candles fish their way home."** (37 characters): the plainest and shortest, nearest to the old line's sound; it says journey and homecoming but loses the hint of distance.
