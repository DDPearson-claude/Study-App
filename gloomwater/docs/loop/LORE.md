## 1. The user's questions, answered

**"Draw out a huge continent shaped map. This map is the whole world and it shows how each lake and river is actually connected to one another."**
The world is one continent, the Waxwold (in the people's old word, "the wax forest", for the south where the bees were), about 6,000 km from its cold north coast to its warm south cape, with sea on every side (`WORLD.md`). Every one of the 16 waters lies on one old water road, the Lamp Road, and each is joined to the one before it by a real river, canal, lock stair or stretch of coast you can trace with a finger: up the Lockwater into the stone highland, out through a mountain gap and down a fjord to the sea, south along the fjord coast to a snow gorge where the mountains meet the sea, south down the long cold coast, up a great escarpment and down to a sea-gate, then through a swamp canal onto a great brown river, down it to a lagoon on the eastern sea, and through the lagoon into the forest beyond. The road only ever goes forward: every water lies farther along it than the one before. `WORLD.md` gives every water its coordinates on the 1000 × 1400 canvas and the leg that joins it to the next.

**"So It feels like we're actually journeying through a world when we unlock new maps etc."**
Each map you buy is pinned onto the Chart at its true place, and it brings the river or coast that joins it to the last water, so the continent draws itself in the order you travel. The first time you go to a new water you tow your home there, down its leg of the road (§2.12). The land goes from cold stone to forest because the route really runs from a cold coast through a dry one into the wet tropics. And the story is a road: you follow the people's way back to where they began, and each water shows one more chapter of it.

**"Make it feel natural and real and that it follows our story arc."**
The geography obeys real rules: rivers run downhill and cross ranges only by gaps, canals cross the low divides, the west coast is dry because of a cold current and a rain shadow, the fjords are wet under the westerlies, the coral grows where warm water meets a clear coast, and the forest is a blackwater basin under trade-wind rain. The sun rises and sets as on any world. The drowning is real too: the kingdom held its water back with sluices, weirs, dams, locks and sea-walls, and when nobody was left to keep them, the water came back in. The arc follows the land: the bare stone north is where the lantern fell, and the green south is where the people began.

**"Why actually is our universe were in?"**
It is an ordinary world with days and nights. Two things make it what it is. First, a kingdom of people once lived all along one water road, and its capital hung a great lantern over its lake; the lantern fell, the capital drowned, the people went back south, and in the four hundred years since, every work they left unkept has let the water back in. Second, the Wicks are window candles, and a window candle lives at evening: it is lit at dusk and burns until first light. That is why every trip runs from the last of the day through dusk and night and home at dawn, why the big fish matter ("The big ones come up at dusk. They always did."), and why the folk call the age since the fall "the long evening". The world has a few small wonders nobody explains (§2.13); it has no curse and no villain.

**"What is our story arc?"**
The two lines at the top. In acts: *What Fell*: the drowned capital and the fall. *The Cold Road*: the kingdom's sea road and its lamps, still burning. *Where They Turned*: the warm working coast and the open sea-gate where the people turned inland. *The Way Back*: the canal and the barge road to the orchard forest where the kingdom began and ended. The ending is not a victory: the Wick comes home from the last water to the barge it has towed the whole road, and the old toad who has kept his fire banked for four hundred years rakes it open, because somebody has finally brought the whole road home (§8).

**"Are there humans?"**
None that you will ever see. There are none on any water of the Chart, and the game never shows one.

**"We're there ever humans in this universe?"**
Yes. The people of the Lamplit Kingdom were people like us: tall, warm-handed folk (a Wick stands about knee-high to them) who planted orchards and kept bees, made candles, fished at evening by the basketful, and built every moat, garden, lock, dam, lighthouse, harbour, canal and barge road the Wick sails through. The game shows them only by what they made and left: their doors, their tools, their baskets, their names on the Long Pier, their worn statues. When the lantern fell they chose not to hang another (§2.4), and they went back the way they had come, to the orchard country where they began. The kingdom grew small there, as kingdoms do once they stop, and its story stops at a banked hearth in the forest. Where the last of them went after that, or whether any are still somewhere beyond the map, the world does not say.

**"Where are all the other wicks or people or frogs or whatever"**
- **Other Wicks:** four more candles doze by the banked stove aboard the home barge (Taper, Stub, Tallow, Ashwick) and wake one at a time (SPEC §10.1). A few more woke over the centuries and went out on the far waters and stayed. You find their signs (the stool and the seven knots by the Lantern Hole), and (v2.3, the user's choice of 15 November 2026) you meet four of them now and then out on the water, one to each reach: Dip, Glim, Lean and Farthing (§5.3). Others still lie cold in the drowned towns, dozing as candles do.
- **People:** gone from the Chart (above). Their works stand in every water.
- **Frogs:** ordinary frogs, the south's voice. None in the stone north, a few in Sporewood's warmth, a chorus by the Siltway, a roar in the Inkwood.
- **Others met on the road** (v2.4, the user's document of 15 November 2026; `CHARACTERS.md`): five creatures with something to say, who come to you now and then: Tiller, a snail gardening the meadow on the Drifting Grove's back, sure it is an island; the Velvets, a family of moths flying north to the great lantern; Dart, a kingfisher after the Long Thunder; Supper, a shipyard night bird that says the people's everyday words, who moves into one of Murk's empty birdcages; and Flinch, a young fruit bat afraid of the dark, who rides your lantern home to the Inkwood (§6).
- **Toads:** Old Murk is a toad of the warm south, centuries old, the last trader on the road. A few of his kin live quietly in Lanternroot Lagoon's stilt village. You never meet them: you see their lanterns, and one green one among the amber. "My lot live there. We don't talk."

## 2. The world, and why it is like this

**2.1 Days, evenings and the long evening.** The sun rises and sets; the reefs, the rainforest, the papyrus, the orchards and the bees live on it as anywhere. The game is played at evening for two plain reasons. A Wick is a window candle, and a window candle is lit at dusk and burns until first light, so a Wick wakes in the last of the day, fishes through dusk and night, and comes home at first light to doze through the day (SPEC §3.4's late day, dusk, night and the sail home at dawn). And the big fish come up at dusk. "The long evening" is the folk name for the four hundred years since the lantern fell, the years the kingdom spent without its light. It is a saying, not a sky. A few things are older than the kingdom: the Long Winter ("It was old when the kingdom was built"), the Lantern-Swallower ("Older than the kingdom above") and the Well of Ledgers ("Older than the Vault. Waiting for the sky.").

**2.2 The people and the Lamp Road.** The people began in the Orchard Basin, the low, warm, wet forest at the middle of the south lobe, where they kept orchards and bees and made candles from the wax. Over many generations they went north along the water roads, the only roads they had: fishers and traders following the big fish and the long dusks. The farther north, the longer a summer evening lasts, and these were people who loved the hour the big fish rise; so they kept going until the land ran out, and on the highland's north lip, over the last lake before the sea cliffs, they built their capital: the Sunken Gate and its city on the Mossmere. At every water they settled along the way, the people of that water built a lamp at the landing for whoever was out on the water at evening, and kept it themselves. Read in order, those lamps are **the Lamp Road**, and it is the Wick's route, walked from its north end back to its south. The lamps are each water's own; nothing links them but the road.

**2.3 The kingdom and its lantern.** The Lamplit Kingdom held the whole continent along that road. It held its water back: the capital's lake kept low by a great sluice in the outer wall, gardens and a crystal vault in the highland basins, a summit canal, a weir on a glacier lake, a lighthouse on the outer rock, temple stairs in the snow, a harbour town on land won from the sea behind a mole, a lock stair down the escarpment, a great dam, a sea-gate, a straight canal, a barge road with mile-posts, and orchards on dikes in the forest. Over the capital's lake hung **the great lantern**: the kingdom's oath that someone would always be watching for whoever was out on the water. Every house set a small window candle burning for the same reason (§5.1).

**2.4 The fall.** The great lantern hung on chains over the open lake in front of the Gate, and its weight, through the chains and a counterweight, held the great sluice open, so the lake drained through the outer wall and the lower city stood dry. One evening the chain's last link wore through. There was no enemy, no curse and no crime: it was old. The lantern fell into its own reflection. With its weight gone, the sluice gate dropped shut. The Gate's windows guttered out row by row while their reflections stayed lit a beat longer. Over that night and the days after, the Mossmere rose over the capital to the arches, until it spilled over the outer wall and fell into the cove as the Sluice Run, which is how it falls still. The film shows the fall in one moment. On the Toppled Bridge the chain still hangs, ending in one worn-thin link. **Why they did not hang another:** the lantern was the kingdom's oath, and the people took its fall as the kingdom's time ending. They kept their word by letting it end: they hung no second lantern, and they went back south the way they had come.

**2.5 The drowning, slowly.** "The lantern falls. The kingdom follows." The film's line, and the old premise's "the kingdom followed it into the water", mean the kingdom's works: the capital went under in a night and the rest went over centuries, as nobody was left to keep them. The weir on the glacier lake broke and the Thin Water spread over its sand. The lock stair broke and the river fell down it. The mole gave way and the sea took the harbour town back, and coral took its roofs. The dikes in the forest went and the river took the orchards. The delta's edge sank with nobody to bank it, and the orchard by the sea became a mangrove lagoon. The fall was about four hundred years ago (coral over a harbour town and a forest through an orchard take that long). The folk say it another way: "The water rose to look for it." That is a saying, never a rule of the game.

**2.6 The pale fish.** The Lantern-Swallower is a deep-water shark older than the kingdom. In the kingdom's time she came up under the lantern at dusk, as the big ones do, and the capital's fishers knew her. On the night of the fall she took the ember as it sank (the film). In the years after, while the Summit Cut's gates still stood open, she went up the waters to the Bottomless of the Pallid Deep, the deepest water in the kingdom, and lives there now; the ember still shows through her six gills (ROSTER). She is not a thief and not a keeper of anything. She is a big old fish people tell stories about: "The great lantern went down. Still going." The old saying "it fell into her the way a coin falls into a well" survives in these docs only; the name "Pale Leviathan" never ships (§10).

**2.7 Fish are fish.** The fish glow because deep water, glowcaps and the dark make fish glow; the giants are just very old; the legends are fish with habits the people noticed and named (the Belled Matriarch rings the drowned bell as she passes; the Barge-Shadow follows the barge's track; the Old Shipwright works the slipway's timbers). Nothing in the water is made of what was lost, and no fish carries the plot. Murk's line "The water keeps what it takes" means only what it says: what sinks stays sunk.

**2.8 The dark.** The Gloom, which is lore only, is just the dark of night on cold water: patient, not cruel. There is nothing in it to fight, and nothing out there hunts the Wick.

**2.9 Why the land greens the farther you go.** The route runs from a cold northern coast and a stone highland, through a rain-shadow steppe and a cold-current desert coast greened only by rivers and spray, into the wet tropics, and every drowned work is overgrown at the speed of its climate: bare in the north, furred in the dry west, swallowed in the wet south (`WORLD.md` §3). The story sits lightly on top: the north is where the light fell, and the farther from the fall, the more the world has grown over it ("moss has kept the vigil the candles could not"). The kingdom stopped; the world did not. Only things turn green: the sky over each water is the same violet-dusk sky at the same hour.

**2.10 The sky.** It is a real sky everywhere. The four highland lakes lie in deep basins that the kingdom roofed with stone vaults to hold the lantern's light in; most of the vaulting has fallen, so what old canon calls stalactites and hanging ruins against the stars are broken vaults and hanging roots with the real sky beyond, and the waterfall "from a ceiling crack" falls through a gap in a vault. The Rime Sound is the first water outside the walls (a new line for these docs, not a canon quote), the first with no roof at all. The Landing alone is a true sea cave with a real ceiling.

**2.11 The mist and the maps.** Forgetting looks like mist. A water nobody remembers sinks into standing lavender mist and cannot be found. A chart is a remembering: somebody who knew the way drew it. When a living flame carries that chart, the mist lets go of that water for good. That is why buying a map is the only way to find a new water, and the main way to find a new place (see below), and why only Murk can sell them: he holds the last of the kingdom's charts. Murk, for the docs only: "A water nobody remembers is hard to find. I remember. That's what you're paying for."

**(v2.3) A tale is a remembering too.** A water lifts for any flame that carries its chart, or has fished it; the charts are the household's, kept aboard, so all five of the barge's Wicks share them. The far-water Wicks went out while the waters were still remembered and have fished theirs ever since, so they fish where your raft still sees only mist. A far-water Wick's tale is a remembering told rather than drawn: told the way to a small corner of a water it has fished, a flame finds it, as a chart lets a water go (the same small wonder, a forgotten water remembered; §2.13 stays four). Two limits hold. **No tale ever tells the way to a water**: a water's way is too long to tell, and only a chart holds it, so buying a map is still the only way to find a new water. And a tale can show only a corner no chart-maker drew, one the drowning or the long years since made after their last sheet: four in all, one to each reach (`FARWICKS.md` §4.3).

**2.12 How the raft, and home, get there.** The road is real and long, and the game shows each leg once. **The tow**: the first time the Wick goes to a newly opened water it takes its home with it, the old barge-house on a line behind the raft and Murk's barge alongside, down that water's leg of the road, and the Chart shows the leg go by (the Long Coast's cliffs, the lock stair's towers, the canal's lock, the Seam's black water) to the new sheet, inked since its map was bought (the first sheet, the Mossmere's, inks in as the first tow arrives); then, one ink dip later, the raft is seen on the water towing the barge in to that water's own old landing (SPEC §6.5). The barge moors there, under its lamp, facing back down its leg. **A kept lamp remembers the way.** The people of every water kept a lamp at its landing for whoever was out on the water at evening (§2.2), and the ones that still burn remember the way for whoever is: that is how a raft towing a house makes a long leg between one dawn and the next evening, and why, once the home has lain under a water's lamp, going back to it is no voyage at all (one ink dip). How is never explained; it is the only magic in how anyone travels. The Chart shows the real road and how far it really is. (v2.1's Waystones carried this wonder; v2.2 moves it onto the road's own lamps, and the stones become the home barge's carved sill, §9.)

**2.13 The small wonders.** Four things in the world are never explained and never need to be: some lamps keep burning with nobody to feed them; a window candle can wake; a forgotten water hides in mist until it is remembered; a kept lamp remembers the way. Nothing else is magic.

## 3. The story arc, by acts, over the 16 waters

The arc is a road, walked in unlock order. Each water shows one chapter, readable at a glance, and gives one **clue you cannot miss**: a landmark the camera frames during the 3-second mist lift on the first evening there (SPEC §5.1 rule 7). Nothing in the arc needs a legend, a catch or a count; every reveal comes with a map and its landmark, and the legends add beats but open nothing (SPEC §5.1 rule 4).

### Prologue: One Warm Light (the film, then the Landing)

**Story order, in one line:** the Wick wakes at the Landing, aboard an old barge-house moored in the cove since the fall, by the fire Murk banked in its stove; it takes the barge's line and tows it up the Sluice Run to the Mossmere (the first tow), and its first evening there is the film's last two beats; the film shows the kingdom's end and then jumps to now, to you on that first evening, and the opening then shows how you got there (`OPENING.md` §6.2). (Before M1 step 5 the film hands straight to the Jetty at dawn.)

The film plays as it is, with its line 3 rewritten (§12). The lantern falls; the Gate goes dark; a pale flank takes the ember; centuries later, a Wick out on the Mossmere with its line already out: its flame gutters in the cold of its first water and catches again. Then the wake: aboard the barge-house by its banked stove, among dozing candles, at the Landing, "where the last Wicks came ashore". Murk: "Awake, little Wick? Good. Fish sell. Clams buy. That's the whole of it." He means it; he is wry, not secretive. Once, far down the channel, a single warm point pulses (canon). The first tow goes to the Mossmere because it is the first water up the road and the door the last candles came out of; the barge has been home ever since, and the Landing is where it set out from.

*How the player learns it:* the film, plus three things at the Landing with no words (drawn on the Chart in the opening): the dozing candles by the stove, the barge-house itself with its banked glow in the window, the warm point far down the channel; then the tow.

## 4. Humans, then and now

**Then.** The people of the Lamplit Kingdom were humans: tall people of the warm south. A Wick (0.6 m) stands about knee-high to them, and their doors are four Wicks tall. They planted orchards and kept bees in the Orchard Basin, made candles from the wax, and built the whole road. They fished at evening, by the basketful, laughing. They remembered their dead at the Pallid Deep and carved their names on the Long Pier ("Every name on the pier was somebody's."). The game never shows a human face or figure, and it never should; its statues are ordinary statues worn smooth by the water. On screen the people are only ever "the kingdom", "the people", "somebody", "the gardeners". The word "human" never appears.

**What happened to them.** No catastrophe killed them and nothing hunted them. When the lantern fell they let the kingdom end (§2.4) and went back the way they had come: down the cold road to the sea, south past the snow temple, down the long coast, up the escarpment and down to the sea-gate, inland by the canal, down the barge road to the lagoon and through it, up the Monsoon Cut into the orchard forest beyond, carrying what they could. Murk poled the last barge of their things; it sank under him at the great bend, empty of people, and he swam. In the orchard country the kingdom grew small, a village, then a few houses, and the last of the households banked its hearth one night and did not come back to stir it. That is where the kingdom's story stops. The docs do not decide whether any of the people are still somewhere beyond the map, and the game never answers it.

**What is left of them.** Everything they built, in all sixteen waters. Some of their lamps, still burning. The creatures still keeping to the habits the people named (the Last Sentry, the Old Shipwright, the Last Lock, the Barge-Shadow, the Lampwarden Angler "still waiting for someone to come home"). The things that miss them (the Rushlight common, the Paper-Lantern Jelly, Emberbelly under the Kitchen Stair, the lantern-gourds still ripening). Murk's pack: empty birdcages (from the Salt Gate on, one of them Supper's, v2.4), dead lanterns, a crooked umbrella, "Everything here was somebody's last hope once." And the Wicks themselves: their bees' wax. (v2.4) And the habits they left in animals: the shipyard's birds still saying the people's everyday words at the sounds that came with them, four hundred years on (Supper); a snail planting straight rows because the gardens it drifts past were planted in rows (Tiller); moths still flying north, generation after generation, to a lantern that fell (the Velvets).

**Now.** None on any sheet, and none will appear. The blank east of the Chart and the world beyond the far isle are room for later sheets, not a promise and not a denial.

## 5. The Wicks, and where the others are

**5.1 What a Wick is.** In the kingdom every house set a small, fat candle in its window at evening, so that whoever was out on the water could find the way home; it was lit at dusk and burned until first light. The candles were made of orchard-bee wax from the south, so every Wick is a small piece of the forest at the end of the road. On the night of the fall the capital's window candles went out row by row (the film); some were carried out of the windows on the rising water, down the Sluice Run, and washed up in the cove under the outer wall: the Landing. Murk carried them aboard the old barge-house moored in the cove and laid them by the fire he kept banked in its stove, and there they lay cold. Now and then, over the centuries, one woke. A candle lit to see someone off burns down (the travellers' tapers at Pyrefall). A candle lit for someone coming home keeps waiting, and these waited so long, with nobody left to come home, that the ones that woke took the old rafts and rods and went out on the water themselves. "No one ever told it that it couldn't."

**5.2 Why a Wick goes out.** Nothing makes it. A Wick goes out at evening and comes home at first light because that is the shape of a window candle's life: someone out on the water at evening, home by morning. Wicks doze and wake as candles do; a dozing Wick is asleep, not lost, and nothing a Wick does or fails to do puts it out. Nothing runs out, nothing needs feeding, and no trip ends because a flame runs low. (Ashwick's canon "It went out once. It came back angry." is a candle's joke about being blown out, nothing more.)

**5.3 Where the other Wicks are.**
1. **You.** The latest candle at the Landing to wake, and the first to fish the whole road through. Not chosen and not special: stubborn. Most Wicks that woke fished the near waters and dozed again; a few went far and stayed out. None kept buying Murk's charts to the last one.
2. **The four by the stove: Taper, Stub, Tallow and Ashwick.** Other candles that washed ashore. They wake one at a time on SPEC §10.1's triggers (12 species in the Journal, the Sporewood map, 5 Trophies, a giant landed). The doc gives no rule for it and the screen gives no reason: each wake is shown as the player's was, a flame catching. The ones not out on the water doze by the stove aboard, and travel with it.
3. **The four far-water Wicks** (v2.3; the user, 15 November 2026: "Far-water Wicks"; `FARWICKS.md` §2). A few Wicks woke long ago, went out on the water and stayed. Four of them are met, now and then, one to each reach: **Dip** in the old kingdom, which went down with its window into the Mossmere on the night of the fall, lay cold on the bottom a long while and caught again where it lay, and so never knew there was a cove or a stove; **Glim** on the cold rim, whose stool, lantern and seven knots are by the Lantern Hole (a knot for each time the Long Winter has passed under its hole; this stays in these docs; the lantern on the ice is Glim's own, left burning by its hole as a fisher leaves a rod, and on an evening Glim is met at the Sound it is on Glim's crook and the hole is dark); **Lean** on the falls and the gate, the warm coast, among the people's baskets, which fishes for the joy of it and laughs at every fish, landed or lost; and **Farthing** in the green heart, the cheapest candle in the kingdom (made from the dregs of the orchard wax, remelted, brown-yellow and uneven: bee wax like every Wick) and the one that went farthest, its cloak grown over with lichen. Glim, Lean and Farthing woke by Murk's banked stove in his first years in the cove, while the waters were still remembered, and kept going down the road until each found the water it would rather be on. They never came home because nothing called them: a fisher stays out while the fish rise, and home had no one awake in it. They are not sad. **Why they tell you things**: a window candle was set in a window so that whoever was out on the water could find the way, and telling another small fisher where the fish lie is the same light. **What they are not**: keepers, guides, quest-givers, traders or rivals. They tend nothing, lead nowhere, ask nothing, sell nothing and never take a fish you could catch. **What changed in v2.3**: they were "never met, never heard, never sought"; the user has chosen to meet them, so they are **met** (a lantern across the water at dusk, and you pull alongside) and **heard** (a line or two of fisher's talk), and still **never sought**: no quest, no marker and no call; one comes when it has something to tell. Other Wicks fished the near waters and dozed again; of the Wicks, only these four are met.
4. **The cold ones in the water.** Candles that sank on the night of the fall lie in the drowned towns and the Pallid Deep, dozing as candles do; a cold candle can catch again some day, and nothing says when. The 30 drifting candle-rafts (the Pallid Deep's Waystone keepsake) are the kingdom's remembrance rafts. (v2.3: Dip is one that caught again, entry 3; no other is shown waking.) If the parked walking build returns, its sister-candles are these, and the parked Shrine of Drowned Lanterns is where their keepsakes would go.
5. **The Wayfarer (parked with walking).** Its place by the stove holds only an old wax drip. No lore line goes with it.
6. **The First Wick** (the relic "Stub of the First Wick") is the first window candle ever lit, in the Inkwood's stilt village. It is a story the others tell, never a character. The Inkwood's wax-ringed niches are where it stood.

## 6. The folk of the world (none of them enemies)

Nobody in the world is an enemy. Everyone is kind, busy or asleep. Who speaks is the user's call, character by character. **(v2.4) Named characters talk; every other creature stays an ordinary animal**: Murk at home; out on the water the four far-water Wicks, a line or two when you pull alongside one (v2.3, §5.3 (3)); and the five met on the road (below), who come to you. Each line fits two short rows on a phone, one row wherever it can, one line at a time, never during a bite (SPEC v8.6 §8.10). The old toads stay unseen and silent, and the Wick never speaks.

**Old Murk.** An enormous umber toad of the warm south, centuries old ("I've had centuries. Fewer than I'd like."). The old toads are a long-lived people who were the kingdom's neighbours and traders, never its subjects. Murk was the barge-trader who plied the whole Lamp Road, from the orchards out through the lagoon and up the barge road, through the canal and the sea-gate, up the long coast past the snow temple and up the cold road to the capital. He was in the capital the night the lantern fell, and he poled the last barge of the people's things south; it sank under him at the great bend, and he swam. When they were gone he came back up the road alone, to the cove where the candles had washed ashore, because a trader goes where his last customers are, and these were the last of the kingdom. In the cove lay an old barge-house of the barge road, the **Slow Hearth**: a barge family's floating home that had come north with a barge train and lay waiting in the sluice cove when the lantern fell. He carried the candles aboard and banked his fire in its stove, low, as the toads and the people both did for a household that was away, to be stirred when someone came home with news of the road (§8). The household is the barge and its candles, and a hearth goes where the household goes (in these docs only: "A hearth goes where the household goes."), so when the Wick takes the barge's line, Murk's fire goes up the road with it, and so does Murk. **His own barge**, the lily-pad trading barge with his towering pack, moors alongside the Slow Hearth wherever it lies: the shop goes where the household goes. He is the last trader on the road, so "I'm the only shop left" is plainly true. He is wry and tender, never secretive: what he knows he would tell you if you asked, and you never need to.

- **His charts** are the kingdom's own sheets, collected in trade over centuries, in four hands: court surveyors' (reach I), the sea-road wardens' (reach II), harbour-masters' (reach III) and barge pilots' (reach IV, waters 13–15). The Inkwood's is the oldest sheet of all, a plain orchard plan from the people's first villages, which Murk traded for long ago. He seals each with candle wax pressed into the shape of the water's legend, because he names waters for whoever lives there.
- **He buys fish for their stories.** At the tally he counts and weighs every fish, writes it in his Ledger and lets it go at the gate before dawn; what he is buying is the count and the news of the waters, which is how he keeps his charts honest ("Bring me something with a story."). That is why any fish, giant or legend can be met again on a later evening. The sale, the price and the hold do not change.
- **Clams** are the old toads' shell money, dug from the Siltway's beds, and the only money left: the kingdom's coins drowned with it (they lie in the Coin Run, left on the water for luck).
- **His green lamp** is a southern swamp-lamp, the one green light in the game: the south's colour carried north.
- **He never says any of this outright.** His kindness is never stated: "Murk is not a charity. Murk is barely a toad." The prices that are always just reachable, the fish let go, the banked fire.

**The old toads (Murk's kin).** A few very old toads live quietly in Lanternroot Lagoon's stilt village. They are never seen whole and never speak. Their signs: lanterns lit at every dusk, one green among the amber; scraps on the offering posts ("The stilt village feeds it and asks nothing."); a moored barge; a slow smudge of pipe smoke; a porch bell answering the Wick's rod bell once. They do not trade, so Murk is still the only shop. Up the Monsoon Cut they still drop scraps from the Kitchen Stair for Emberbelly, out of old habit. They are not a community to visit, and nothing human is ever drawn there.

**The far-water Wicks** (v2.3). The four of §5.3 (3), met out on the water at dusk, one to each reach. Murk knows three of them (they woke by his stove; Dip he never met) and is glad they are still out there: "Still out there, that lot? Good.", said once, at the first tally after the player first meets Glim, the first of them he knew. They never come aboard, never trade (Murk stays the only shop), never give anything but a tale, and never speak of the story: they talk of fish, water and themselves.

**The five met on the road** (v2.4; the user's document of 15 November 2026 and its four answers; `CHARACTERS.md`). Small personal stories inside the big loss, each a habit that outlived its reason or a belief out of step with the world, each with a story that moves on between visits and a payoff the player causes. They come to you now and then (never two in an evening, and never on the evening a far-water lantern lifts), say a line or two, and go; they never trade, keep, guide, ask, rival or take; nothing of them is on the Chart or in the Journal. Their names are for these docs.
- **Tiller** (Sporewood Pools). A pond snail with an ochre, moss-furred shell and a row of seedlings along its ridge, who climbed onto a meadow as a hatchling and has never got off. He waters his straight rows each night with an old spore-cap because the drowned gardens he drifts past were planted in rows and their cans hung in rows; he has never met a gardener. His island is the Drifting Grove, Sporewood's legend, who at night, when she bites nowhere, rises and drifts through the Pools with her meadow; by day she lies on the floor of the Grove Well, "where no one sees", and Tiller blames the tides. He never twigs. When she is landed he is on her back at the tally, furious ("Who moved my island?"), and Murk lets her go with him still aboard. Murk knows: "Snail's happy. Leave him be."
- **The Velvets** (Pyrefall Gorge, then back north). In the kingdom's time moths came from all over the south to the great lantern; the habit outlived it. A moth lives a few weeks, so the flight takes generations, and every daughter is called Velvet; the ones setting out from the south have never heard that it fell. The first comes to your lantern at the temple fires ("Is this the great lantern?"); she stays there ("Mother stopped at the gorge. Liked the fires."): **no Velvet dies; each settles at a small light along the way** (the user's answer), the temple fires, the Rime Sound's and Glasswater's kept lamps, and her daughter flies on north. The last reaches the Mossmere, finds only the chain and the stars where the lantern hung ("…Oh."), and settles in one of the Gate's lit windows: "Smaller than they said. Warm, though." Murk's tongue does not flick: "I wasn't going to."
- **Dart** (Rushlight Fen). A small kingfisher, blue-violet and ochre, who touched the Long Thunder's back when he was young and has dived at her all his life since; he knows he could never lift her, and that was never the point. He turned the fen, where fishing was a joy, into a grudge match; when the Wick lands her he wants every detail, and then he fishes the fen's tiny Basket Fry by the basketful, laughing, letting every one go, as the people did: "Forgot this was fun."
- **Supper** (the Salt Gate, then aboard). A small bark-coloured night bird of the shipyard. A young fruit bat of the Inkwood's colony who followed the canal north on its first flight, got lost, and has hidden from the night at Lampmoss ever since. It lands on your lantern pole and swings underneath it ("Don't turn it off."), rides your lantern home through the swamp, the barge road and the lagoon, a little braver each evening, and sees the hearth stirred; on the next Inkwood evening it flies off into the dark with the others: "It's not so bad. It's just night." The dark is only night, patient and not cruel (§2.8), and the Wick is, for once, what a window candle is for: a light for someone coming home.

**Frogs.** Ordinary small frogs, animals, not folk: the south's voice. None in the stone north; a first few in Sporewood's warmth; reed frogs piping in Rushlight Fen; the first chorus at Lampmoss Slough, heard, not seen; the Siltway's floating meadows "loud with frogs", the loudest place in the game so far; and the Inkwood a full chorus. The world's sound greens with its colour.

Bees: carved over a garden door in Sporewood, remembered on the Siltway ("Somebody down here still remembers bees."), and a real hum in the Inkwood. Bees made the Wicks' wax.

**The great fish.** The legends and giants are the world's oldest fish, many older than the kingdom, each with a habit the people noticed and a name they gave it: the Belled Matriarch and her bell; the Glass Sovereign in the Vault; the Lantern-Swallower in the Bottomless; the Long Winter, unhurried; the Jetsam Shark among the wreckage; the Ashen Guest by the temple steps; the Last Lock in the stair; the Waxen Abbot in his cellar; the Old Shipwright on the slipway; the Last Sentry at his post; the Barge-Shadow in the barge track; the Silver Regent in the lagoon; the Banked Fire breathing in the creeks. The north is quiet, not empty (v2.4, the user's answer: it keeps Tiller's and the Velvets' voices, beside Dip's and Glim's); the world fills up as it greens.

## 7. Fish, light and clams

**7.1 Why the Wicks fish.** A window candle was lit to wait for a fisher coming home. With no fishers left, the ones that woke became the fishers. The film shows it: the Wick on the Mossmere has its line already out. Murk's "Fish sell. Clams buy. That's the whole of it." is wry and true, and the road adds to it one map at a time.

**7.2 Why a fish is worth anything.** To Murk, a fish is news: where it was, how big, at what hour. He pays for the count and the story, and lets the fish go (§6). That is all the economy needs; nothing about a fish is magic.

**7.3 Why fish sell for clams, and maps cost clams.** Murk is the last trader on the road; clams are the only money left; the charts are what he has to sell. Clams stay the only key: nothing needs a legend, a catch or a count to open a water (SPEC §5.1 rule 4), and no reveal in the story waits on luck.

**7.4 Why the giants and legends matter.** Each legend is a fish the people knew by name: "That one had a name before you met it." Landing one is meeting a very old fish, not taking a treasure. Her lore line is told, her stone on the barge's sill is gilded, her ink print hangs along the home barge's cabin eaves, so home slowly fills with the road's great fish, and Murk lets her go home. The two legends at the ends of the road are both optional: the Lantern-Swallower (water 4), who was under the lantern when it fell, and the Banked Fire (water 16), named for the people's hearth. Their two prints hang facing each other along the cabin's two eaves. The giants and the ones that got away ("It took the line and the hook and the prayer.") are what make each water deep and worth coming back to.

**7.5 The light, as a theme.** Small lights kept by habit and kindness: a window candle for whoever is out on the water, a lamp at each landing kept by that water's own people, a toad's banked fire, carried aboard. None of them is the same fire as another, and none needs to be. The kingdom's great lantern went out; the small lights did not. (v2.4) The Velvets carry it to its end: generations of moths fly north to the great lantern, each settles at a small light along the road (the temple fires, two kept lamps, a lit window of the Gate), and the last, finding only the chain, settles in a window: "Smaller than they said. Warm, though."

## 9. How the game tells it, with almost no words

The story is carried by things the game already has. The only new on-screen text is one Murk line per lake map, three sheet notes, one Murk line at home and the closing film's three new lines: about a hundred and fifty words in all, each for sign-off. (v2.3, after M1: the far-water Wicks add about seventy short lines over the whole road, about four hundred and fifty words, at most three a meeting, each said once; fisher's talk, never the story; `FARWICKS.md` §8. v2.4, after M1: the five met on the road add sixty short lines, about two hundred and seventy words, seven of them Murk's, at most one beat an evening; `CHARACTERS.md` §9.)

1. **The opening film.** "One Warm Light", unchanged but for line 3 (§12).
2. **Landmarks.** Each reach is one chapter, readable at a glance: reach I the capital's rooms (gate, garden, treasury of light, the place of remembrance); reach II the cold road (bell-buoys, sluice lamp, lighthouse, snow temple); reach III the working coast (harbour, lock stair, dam, sea-gate and its idle shipyard); reach IV the way back (canal, barge road, lit stilt village, kitchen in the forest).
