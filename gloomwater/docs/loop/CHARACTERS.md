## 0. The user's document, the four answers, and what binds

**The document.** Five characters to meet on the road, each built from one recipe: an odd small want, a habit or belief out of step with the world, a story that moves on between visits, and a payoff the player causes; every line short, like Murk's; no enemies, no quests, no human figure, no new warm light on the water, Murk still the only shop. Its craft, described here without the games it cites: the best small characters are personal stories inside a big loss, telling you what happened to the world without being about it, by six means: **a habit that outlived its reason**, **one small want**, **a story that moves on while you are away**, **heard before seen**, **one of you knows something the other does not**, and **a payoff the player causes**. The five: **Tiller**, a snail gardening the meadow on the Drifting Grove's back, sure it is an island (Sporewood, water 2); **Velvet**, a family of moths flying north over generations to the great lantern (met at Pyrefall Gorge, 8, her daughters at the Rime Sound, 5, Glasswater, 3, and the Mossmere, 1); **Dart**, a kingfisher who has dived at the Long Thunder all his life (Rushlight Fen, 11); **Supper**, a shipyard night bird that says the people's old phrases at the sounds that once came with them, moves into one of Murk's empty birdcages and travels with home (the Salt Gate, 12); **Flinch**, a young fruit bat afraid of the dark who rides the lantern home to the Inkwood (13–16).

## 1. In one breath

Five creatures of the road have something to say. On Sporewood's warm water at night, a patch of meadow drifts past the raft, and a snail with a spore-cap is watering it; it is sure it lives on an island, and it does not. At the snow temple a moth comes to your flame and asks if it is the great lantern; her daughters fly on north, each stopping at a small light along the way, until the last of them reaches the Mossmere and finds only the chain. On the fen a kingfisher lands on your lantern pole to look at your rod; he has dived at the Long Thunder all his life and touched her twice. In the shipyard at the sea-gate a bark-coloured night bird calls "Mind your fingers." when a fish flaps on your planks, and a few dawns later it is sitting in one of Murk's empty birdcages with the door open. At the canal's summit a young fruit bat lands on your lantern pole, swings underneath and shakes: "Don't put it out." It rides your lantern home to the forest, a little braver each evening. They come to you; they say a line or two; they never take, block, sell or ask anything of you; and on the water there is never more than one beat a night.

## 3. The rules all five keep

### 3.1 Who talks

**Named characters talk; every other creature stays an ordinary animal.** The speakers are Murk, the four far-water Wicks (Dip, Glim, Lean and Farthing) and the five met on the road (Tiller, the Velvets, Dart, Supper and Flinch). Everyone else is an animal behaving as one and never speaks: every fish (the giants and legends included: fish are fish), the frogs, the herons, the fireflies, the ordinary moths at the flame, the bees, the bats of Flinch's colony, the ordinary night birds, Farthing's small frog. The old toads stay unseen and silent. The Wick never speaks, and neither do the four other playable Wicks: their one lines are card lines under New Descent, not speech. A new speaker is the user's call, character by character.

The five never talk to the far-water Wicks or to each other, except the one exchange the barge makes natural (Supper's "Home before dark." and Flinch's answer, §8). Murk talks about them, once each, at home.

### 3.2 The one line budget (for every speaker)

The lead ruled one budget for Murk, the far-water Wicks and the five. **The budget:** a line a character says sets in **at most two rows**, word-wrapped, and **a row holds at most 36 characters and 8 words**: one row at 18 px on a 358 px phone, the measure the far-water design and the passives already use. So a line is at most 72 characters and 16 words, and **one row wherever it can be** (the writing target, above all on the water, where the ink pool sits beside a raft). It shows one line at a time on the feathered ink pool beside the speaker, held at least 2.4 s and 0.05 s more a character, or until a tap, 0.3 s apart; never during a bite, a play, the show or a card; read aloud by the built live region. **At most three lines a beat**, from one speaker or two in turn.

**Why two rows, and not one.** One row (36 characters, 8 words) for everyone would break sixteen of Murk's lines (`SPEC.md` §8.5–§8.6), nearly all of them M1's and the opening's wake line among them ("Awake, little Wick? Good. Fish sell. Clams buy. That's the whole of it.", in the opening's words the user ruled on 14 November), and M1 changes in nothing. The document's 60 characters would also fail the wake line (71) and is not measured on the screen. Two rows of the far-water row hold every line already written, Murk's on the water too (his lore toasts after a card, his sailing-out lines), with no word changed, and they keep the far-water Wicks' reason: a row is what one glance reads on a phone. One row stays the target, and a line is never cut below its meaning or voice to reach it.

**What each speaker's lines must also do is its own rule**, unchanged and stated here on purpose so the one budget does not blur them:

| speaker | may say | never says |
|---|---|---|
| a far-water Wick | a fisher's observation of fish, water and itself | an instruction; an uncaught fish's name; **any number, word or digit** (so no tale hints at an odd); the fall, the people, the lantern, Murk or the road's end (`FARWICKS.md` §8) |
| Murk | his own lines as written (§8.6), number words included ("Three slots in that hold.") and an errand ("Go again.") | a numeral; an odd |
| the five | talk: frets, questions, boasts, their own small world, the great lantern | an instruction to the player (a fret such as "Don't rock the beans." or a question such as "What did she look like?" is talk, never a task: nothing changes whatever the player does); a fish's name; a numeral; a fishing figure (a size, rate, share, price or count). A number word as talk is allowed ("Touched her twice.", "Eleven today.") |

**The scan** (`validate-data --loop`, the characters' slice): every `TEXT_LOOP` line of every speaker sets in at most two rows of at most 8 words, **measured with Drowned Filigree's own advance widths at 18 px in a 358 px column** (the browser phone test's measured row count is the gate; 36 characters is the writing target, and a line whose row reaches 36 is flagged to be looked at on the phone); no numeral (no digit) in any line; the guards' word list; and the table's speaker rules: for a far-water line, no number word; for the five, no fish name and no imperative opening (the far-water list: "Go", "Look", "Listen", "Let", "Try", "Wait", "Cast", "Follow"; "Don't" is allowed: it is a fret, never a task).

### 3.3 They come to you: a visit on the water

A **visitor** (Tiller, a Velvet, Dart, Supper before it moves aboard, Flinch before it boards) is never sought: there is no lantern to tap, no target, no marker, no ring and no count. On its evening (§3.4), within its window, it waits for **a quiet moment**: nothing is biting, playing, showing or on the card: the float waiting on the water, the moment just after a card closes, or the raft gliding between places (it stops for the visit, as alongside a far-water raft). Then:

| moment | what happens | time |
|---|---|---|
| it comes | it arrives at the raft: a bird or a bat to the lantern pole, a moth to the lantern's cap, the meadow drifting up alongside on the side away from the float; **the clock and the bites wait** from its arrival (as alongside a far-water raft); the camera eases a little closer to the raft (10%, 0.6 s) | about 1.2 s |
| it speaks | its line or lines, one at a time, on the ink pool beside it, no name and no glyph | ≥ 2.4 s each, or a tap |
| it goes | it leaves the way it came; the camera eases back; the clock and the bites run on | about 1 s |

About 5–9 s of the player's time, none of it the evening's: the clock is stopped throughout, so the sim charges nothing (§12.4).

**Its window.** A visitor's hour is a window that runs until the turn for home, as a fish's hour runs into the next phase (SPEC §3.4): a **dusk** visitor (Dart, Supper's first meeting, Flinch's first landing) comes at the first quiet moment from the start of dusk until you turn for home; a **night** visitor (Tiller, the Velvets) at the first quiet moment from the start of night. Dusk is only 20 s of water time, so a dusk spent sailing between places or with a fish on the line no longer loses the visit: it comes in the night instead. **If you turn for home first**, it does not come that evening: its beat stays due, nothing is lost, and the next evening on which a far-water tale is also due goes to the tale (§3.4), so a visitor that keeps missing you never holds up a far-water Wick.

**Reduced motion**: no camera ease and no camera move inside a beat; the visitor fades in at its perch and out; a beat's own camera move (V3's glance under the water, V4's tilt) becomes a cut to a still and a cut back, held as long as the line.

### 3.4 One beat an evening: the far-water Wicks and the five, each on its own clock

The lead ruled "never two meetings in one evening". The far-water Wicks' lanterns and the five share exactly that, and nothing more: **each kind keeps its own clock**, so neither slows the other.

| rule | value | why |
|---|---|---|
| the save has ended at least 5 evenings, and the tutorial is over | `fromEvening: 6` (shared, `LOOP.farwicks.meet`) | the first trips are the game's own |
| never on a water's first evening, a told place's lift evening, the opening, the tutorial or the tow | — | a first evening is the mist lift's |
| **at most one beat on the water an evening**: a lantern, a visit, or an aboard character's line. | **the five's own clock**: a gapped visit is due at this water (its trigger met, §4–§8); at least **4 evenings** ended since the last visit by any of the five came (`folk.last`); tonight's roll 1 in 2 on the first and second eligible evenings, sure on the third (`folk.wait`) | `LOOP.folk.meet`: `gap: 4` (4–6), `chance: 0.5`, `sureOn: 3` | rare, never two close together, never more than three evenings late |

**Who comes among the five** never needs a rule: no water has two of them (Tiller is Sporewood's, each Velvet her own water's, Dart the fen's, Supper the Salt Gate's), and an aboard character's lines are aboard lines, not visits, except Flinch's last (a gap-free follow-on at the Inkwood, §8.2).

A visit counts for its gap only once it has come. **Beats in their moment.** Two payoffs play where the player makes them, whatever else the evening held, because the player caused them: Tiller's (TT) and Dart's (DP), at the first landing of their legend, at the tally or (when she is let go) in the quiet moment just after her card closes (§4.2, §6.2). Over the road: about **16 gapped visits, 5 gap-free beats and 2 payoffs in their moment**, beside the far-water Wicks' 48, each 5–9 s with the clock stopped. At the five's nine waters a beat on the water comes about one evening in three, and a tale comes about half an evening later on average than without them (at most a few evenings, on the evenings a visit wins), not the whole meeting interval a shared gap would have cost (§16, B2).

### 3.6 What none of them ever is

- **Not a trader, keeper, guide, quest-giver or rival.** None sells, gives or takes anything; none tends a lamp or a fire (Velvet only rests at one); **none leads the raft anywhere or asks it to go anywhere** (the two aboard simply ride along with home); **none asks anything the player must do**; none takes a fish the player could catch, or stops one being landed. Murk is the only shop.
- **Never an instruction.** A line of the five is talk, never a thing to do: "Don't rock the beans." and "Don't catch her." are frets, "Then tell me everything." and "What did she look like?" are a bird's delight; nothing changes whatever the player does.
- **Never a marker, a number or a sign of its own.** A character's fishing help is a sight on the water that the water was already making (§4.4, §6.4): no sign's spot, rate, share, size or count changes, and nothing is drawn on the HUD, the Chart or the catalogue.
- **Never during a bite**, a play, the show, a card, the opening, the tutorial, the tow, a water's first evening, or the closing film (except Supper's one line, the user's answer).
- **Never on the Chart, the catalogue, the shelf or the Well.** No page, glyph, knot, count or log of them anywhere; Tiller never goes down the hatch.
- **Never harmed and never hunting.** Nothing eats a Velvet (Murk "wasn't going to"); Dart lets every fish drop back; Flinch ignores the moths at your flame.
- **Never human, never a story-teller.** No human voice (Supper's calls are a bird's); none explains the fall, the people, Murk's past or the road's end. What they know of the world is what an animal would know; the player knows more.
- **Their light** is cold or a pinpoint that lights nothing; their reflections are threads.

## 4. Tiller (Sporewood Pools)

### 4.1 Who

**Look.** About the size of the Wick's head. An autumn-ochre shell furred with moss, a neat row of seedlings planted along its ridge; two pale lavender eyes on stalks, faintly glowing at the tips, and nothing else for a face; a soft grey-violet foot. He carries water in an empty spore-cap, a small cap from Sporewood's mushrooms, tipped like a can. Living green only, on the moss and the seedlings; nothing on him glows warm.

**Who he is.** A pond snail who climbed onto the meadow as a hatchling and has never got off. He plants in straight rows because the drowned gardens he drifts past are laid out in rows, and he waters his rows each night because the gardeners hung their watering cans in rows on the caps: he has never met a gardener; he copies what they left, and believes someone gardened his island before him. He blames its wandering on the weather, then the tides, and in the end on its temper. **His two gaps**: he keeps the gardeners' habit without knowing it was theirs, and the player slowly works out what he never does: his island is the Drifting Grove, Sporewood's legend.

**What she is** (ROSTER, unchanged): a round giant ray nearly as wide as the raft, plum-backed, a whole meadow growing on her back whose small blooms glow amber (pinpoints that light nothing); she lives on the floor of the Grove Well, in the Mother's Cup, and bites there only, by day and dusk, on her bait ("A whole meadow rides on her back. It blooms where no one sees."; the crown's card: "The Mother breathes. Big things breathe with her."). **New here, a sight only:** at night, off her hours, she rises and drifts through Sporewood's open water, her meadow just at the surface, and by the next late day she is back on the Well's floor. She comes again after every landing, kept, placed or let go, as every legend does (Murk lets every fish go at the gate before dawn, `LORE.md` §6).

### 7.1 Who

**Look.** A small night bird, about the size of the Wick's head: a wide flat head and a broad gape, grey-brown and mottled like bark, so it vanishes against the unfinished hulls; huge dark eyes with a glint of pale starlight. Its voice is far too big for it, and always its own bird voice, never a person's.

**Who it is.** In the kingdom's time birds nested in the shipyard and picked up what the people said, sound by sound, and taught their chicks; four hundred years on, the words are still being passed down, and nobody has answered them in all that time. **Why it matters**: the purest habit that outlived its reason. The people are gone, but their everyday words still go off at the right sound and the wrong moment: funny most of the time, and once, at the very end, exactly right.

### 7.2 How it works: a moment, a sound, then a phrase

Each phrase is keyed to a **named moment** of the game (the build drives it from that event, never from the sound itself), and each moment has its sound:

| the moment | its sound | it says |
|---|---|---|
| home, arrival: the raft knocks against the barge's stern and the Wick steps aboard | the stern's creak (new) | "Mind the step." |
| home, arrival: Murk's hand bell as you come home | the bell (built) | "Is that you?" |
| the tally: a fish set on Murk's scale (or, at S1 only, a fish flapping on the raft's planks as its card closes) | the scale's clink (built); the planks' flap (built) | "Mind your fingers." |
| the tally opens: the hold's lid, whichever hold it is by then (creel, hamper or chest) | the hold's lid (new) | "Supper's on." |
| the sail out: the raft's line slipped from the stern | the cast-off (new) | "Home before dark." |
| home, arrival, the visit after a barge part is brought aboard: Murk's mallet as the part is seen fitted | the mallet's knock (new) | it knocks back, two taps (no words) |
| the closing film: Murk's rake | the rake's scrape (built, in the film) | "Welcome home." |

Each phrase is a bird call whose rhythm is the phrase's syllables (whistled, warbled; never a person's timbre), with the words floating beside it as any line does. The four new sounds (the stern's creak, the hold's lid, the cast-off, the mallet) and its calls are rendered with `audio-render --set=folk`.

## 8. Flinch (Lampmoss Slough to the Inkwood, aboard)

### 8.1 Who

**Look.** A young fruit bat about the size of the Wick's fist, with wings it wraps round itself like a cloak, a little like the Wick's: a soft dark shape with a violet rim light and two small amber pinpoint eyes. It hangs upside down under the raft's iron lantern, as close to the glass as it can get.

**Who it is.** Flinch's colony lives under the Inkwood's canopy. On its first flight it followed the canal north, got lost, and ended up alone at Lampmoss Slough, where it has been hiding from the night ever since. **Why it matters**: a bat afraid of the dark is a joke that turns into the game's own point: the dark is only night, patient and not cruel. And it lets the Wick be what a window candle is for, a light for someone coming home.

## 10. How they fit together

### 10.1 With the far-water Wicks

Each reach now has its far-water Wick and its own of the five, and the one beat an evening (§3.4) gives each evening at most one of them on the water:

| reach | far-water Wick | the five met there |
|---|---|---|
| I, the old kingdom | Dip | Tiller (Sporewood); Velvet III (Glasswater) and Velvet IV (the Mossmere), late in the road |
| II, the cold rim | Glim | Velvet I (Pyrefall) and Velvet II (the Rime Sound) |
| III, the falls and the gate | Lean | Dart (the fen); Supper (the Salt Gate), then aboard |
| IV, the green heart | Farthing | Flinch, aboard from Lampmoss to the Inkwood |

**Tells beside the tips.** The far-water Wicks and the five help the fishing in two different ways that never overlap:

| | a far-water Wick's tip | Tiller's or Dart's tell |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | knowledge, told once | a sight on the water, shown again and again |
| what it changes | that fish's bites and Hold at that water (`FARWICKS.md` §4.1) | nothing: a sign's spot, rate, kind and share are as they were; the meadow drifts to a sign that has risen, Dart dives at one |
| where the player sees it | the knot; the number in the player's own logs | the water itself |
| a number anywhere | in the logs only | none, anywhere |

A tipped fish's sign may well be the one the meadow drifts to, or the one Dart dives at: the tip makes it come more often, the tell shows where it rose. Neither ever marks a fish.

**What they share and do not.** Both kinds speak in the one budget, never during a bite, at most three lines a beat; both are never sought; neither is ever at the tally but by Murk's mention, Tiller's and Dart's payoffs, and Supper's phrases. The far-water Wicks are met by tapping their lantern at dusk and you sail to them; the five come to you, from their hour until you turn for home (night for Tiller and the Velvets; dusk for Dart, Supper and Flinch). Each kind keeps its own clock and they share one beat an evening. The far-water Wicks speak only of fish, water and themselves, with no number at all; the five may speak of the great lantern and their own small world, because that is what they are. Murk knows of both, and says so once for each.

### 10.2 Aboard: the barge's rules

- **The barge never sinks, drifts or needs keeping, and nothing aboard needs anything.** Supper and Flinch need no feeding, cleaning, calling or care; nothing about them can go wrong; there is no button, menu, item, count, page or slot for either; they never enter the hold, the Well or the catalogue.
- **Where they live.** Supper in one of the empty birdcages in Murk's pack, on Murk's barge alongside the Slow Hearth, door open: out at night (a night bird), home by first light, roosting by day. Flinch under the Slow Hearth's awning by the Chart's table by day, under the raft's lantern each evening, from Lampmoss Slough until it leaves at the Inkwood.
- **They travel with home.** On every tow (§6.5 of the SPEC) the glide-in shows Supper's cage on Murk's barge and, while aboard, Flinch under the awning; the ink dip back to an earlier water takes them too.
- **Their words at home** follow §3.5's order: at most one character beat a visit, never over a line of Murk's.
- **Draw cost.** At home: Supper ≤ 2 calls, Flinch ≤ 2 calls, inside the barge's budget; on the raft: Flinch ≤ 2 calls.

### 10.3 Velvet's way back north, and the kept lamps

Going back to an earlier water is one ink dip, because a kept lamp remembers the way (`LORE.md` §2.12): following the moths north costs a tap on the Chart and an evening at a cheaper water, nothing more, and it is never needed. Two of the four Velvets settle on the road's own kept lamps (the Rime Sound's and Glasswater's, over the barge's mooring), so a player who goes back sees her from home before she comes to the lantern; the first rests by the temple fires that nobody feeds, the last in a lit window of the Gate. The kept lamps themselves change in nothing: a moth rests on one; it is no brighter and no one keeps it.

### 10.4 The empty birdcage

Murk's pack has held empty birdcages since the opening ("Everything here was somebody's last hope once."), and the lore called them "a joke that lands late". This is where it lands, by the lead's ruling: at the Salt Gate a night bird lets itself into one, door open, by choice; a toad who flicks his tongue at moths insists he had nothing to do with it ("Don't look at me. It let itself in."); and he names it after its favourite phrase, which is "Supper's on." ("Supper's on, is it? That's your name, then."). The other cages stay empty.

```
meta.loop.folk = { seed: n, last: evening, wait: n, missed: bool,
                   seen: { beatId: evening }, aboard: [whoId], boarded: { whoId: evening }, gone: [whoId],

run.meet = { kind: 'tale', wick, tale, spot } | { kind: 'folk', beat } | null   // tonight's one beat, chosen at the evening's start, restored by Continue
```

- `seed`: drawn once by `migrateLoop` or `freshLoop`. `last`: the evening the last visit came (the five's gap). `wait`: eligible evenings since the gap passed with a gapped visit due (the sure third). `missed`: the last visit chosen did not come (the next tie goes to the tale). `seen`: every beat that played, with its evening, written in the same save as its first line (a reload mid-beat keeps it seen and never replays it). `aboard` and `boarded`: Supper from S2, Flinch from boarding; `gone`: Flinch after F7. `velvet`: the evening V1 or its fallback played (the family's clock). `phrase`: Supper's last phrase (never twice running). `hearth`: if the ending's slice names its flag otherwise, the five read that one.

The tales' block is untouched (`FARWICKS.md` §9 keeps its own `seed`, `last` and `wait`). A save already past a character's water loses nothing: its beats wait for the next evening there, and an aboard character whose water the barge has already left boards at the next visit home (its "at the latest").
- **The boot rule** (the flag off): unchanged; a saved trip's `run.meet` is dropped with the run. The flag-off game ignores `folk`.
