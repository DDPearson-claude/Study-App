# Learning-science evidence for structuring a pre-built, phone-first, self-study course with interactives

Scope note for the report writer: evidence-strength labels are given in square brackets on each finding — [meta] = meta-analysis or systematic review, [RCT] = randomised (lab or field), [lab] = controlled lab experiment(s), [obs] = observational/log data, [review] = narrative review or expert synthesis. Most primary papers were reachable only as abstracts, publisher pages or secondary summaries (see the tool-access note in the final Gaps section), so exact numbers from full texts are flagged where unverified.

## Key question 1: In what order should explanation, worked example, interactive exploration, practice and recap come in a pre-built lesson, and does it differ for a novice vs someone with partial knowledge?

### Takeaway
For a true novice the strongest evidence favours: short orienting question or prediction -> explicit explanation with a fully worked example -> guided interactive exploration with a specific goal -> faded practice (completion problems, then full problems) -> retrieval-based recap. For a learner with partial knowledge the order flips at the middle: a short problem-solving or invention attempt (with contrasting cases) before the explanation, worked examples thinned or dropped in favour of problems, and lighter guidance around the interactive. Prior knowledge, not age or "style", is the variable the evidence says to branch on.

### Cited Findings

Guidance vs discovery (the frame for the whole sequence)
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) argue from cognitive architecture and "a half-century" of studies that minimally guided instruction is less effective and less efficient than strongly guided instruction, and that "the advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge to provide 'internal' guidance." [review] — [ERIC record of KSC 2006](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ736299)
- The main rebuttal (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn 2007) does not defend unguided discovery; it argues KSC conflated problem-based and inquiry learning with discovery learning, and that PBL/IL "employ scaffolding extensively thereby reducing the cognitive load." [review] — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00461520701263368)
- Mayer (2004) reviewed three literatures and concluded "there is sufficient research evidence to make any reasonable person skeptical about the benefits of discovery" (pure, unguided), favouring guided discovery. [review] — [PubMed 14736316](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14736316)
- Alfieri et al. (2011), 164 studies: explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (580 comparisons, d = -0.38 for discovery, 95% CI [-0.44, -0.31]); enhanced/assisted discovery beat other instruction (360 comparisons, d = 0.30, CI [0.23, 0.36]). "Unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations do." [meta] — [APA PsycNet record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2010-23599-001)

Worked examples first, then fade (novices)
- Barbieri et al. (2023) meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics (55 studies, 181 effect sizes): g = 0.48; the effect was medium whether examples were used for initial acquisition or practice; correct-only examples outperformed incorrect or mixed sets; adding self-explanation prompts to examples moderated the effect negatively relative to examples without prompts. [meta] — [Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [conference poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf)
- Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen & Sweller (2001), trade apprentices: inexperienced trainees "benefited most from the worked examples condition ... with lower ratings of mental load"; "with more experience in the domain, worked examples became redundant and problem solving proved superior." [lab, within a trade-training setting] — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/); the pattern was named the expertise reversal effect in Kalyuga et al. 2003 — [UNSW special-issue intro](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/22fc7cfc-938b-4de4-a98c-22c5e8e353e4)
- Reisslein et al. (2006), computer-based electrical circuit analysis: low-prior-knowledge learners benefited most from example-problem pairs (example first), high-prior-knowledge learners from problem-example pairs (problem first). [lab, electrical domain] — [ASU-hosted PDF](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- Renkl, Atkinson, Maier & Staley: fading (complete example -> increasingly incomplete examples -> problem) beat example-problem pairs in a field and a lab experiment, "at least when near transfer performance is considered"; the effect was mediated by fewer errors. Far-transfer gains were unreliable unless fading was combined with prompts to name the principle behind each step, which then gave medium-to-large near and far transfer effects without extra time. [lab + field] — [PsychArchives record](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196); [Atkinson et al. 2003 record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/transitioning-from-studying-examples-to-solving-problems-effects-/)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect exists (Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod, Learning and Instruction 98, 102142, open access) but only its citation was reachable. [meta, numbers not retrieved] — [listing](https://hubbs.schule/mediathek/tetzlaff-et-al-2025-instruktion-versus-selbstorganisation)

Problem-first works for learners with some knowledge, and only with specific design
- Sinha & Kapur (2021), 53 studies/166 comparisons: problem-solving-then-instruction (PS-I) beat instruction-then-problem-solving with g = 0.36 (CI 0.20-0.51); designs faithful to Productive Failure principles gave g = 0.37-0.58; the advantage reversed for younger learners (grades 2-5) and domain-general skills, and for procedural skills instruction-first was about as effective. Subject areas: maths, physics, chemistry, biology, medicine. [meta] — [DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105) (abstract read via a university mirror; verify figures against the SAGE version)
- Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017): "PS-I fosters learning only if specific design features, namely contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions, are implemented." [review] — [record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/towards-a-theory-of-when-and-how-problem-solving-followed-by-inst/); a later Loibl & Leuders conceptual replication in Bayesian reasoning found neither a PS-I main effect nor a benefit of building on typical student solutions. [lab, failed replication] — [FORRT atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.08.004/)
- Schwartz & Bransford (1998) "A Time for Telling": students who first invented graphs for contrasting data sets learned and transferred more from a later lecture than students who summarised a chapter then heard the same lecture; lectures/readings help "if students have sufficiently differentiated domain knowledge to use the expository materials in a generative manner." [lab] — [PDF](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf); Schwartz et al. (2011): tell-first groups matched invention groups on formula use but learned deep structure less well. [lab] — [PDF](https://ece.northeastern.edu/edsnu/mcgruer/USC/PracticingVersusInventing2011-edu-103-4-759.pdf)

Where the interactive goes and what wraps it
- Moreno & Mayer (2007) review of interactive multimodal environments supports five design principles: guided activity, reflection, feedback, control/pacing, and pretraining, and notes that "interactivity and the need for learners to make inferences is often not as effective as direct instruction," so one must identify when interactivity helps. [review] — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9047-2); [principle summaries](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0053-2/tables/1)
- Mayer's pre-training principle (teach names/characteristics of key components before the main lesson) has a reported effect of d = 0.46 in Mayer 2017 (other Mayer chapters report a median of 0.75); segmenting d = 0.70 (0.79 elsewhere). [lab syntheses, values differ across editions] — [metatoc abstract copy](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning)
- Crouch, Fagen, Callan & Mazur (2004): students who predicted a demonstration's outcome before seeing it understood significantly more; predicting then discussing was strongest; passive observers "understood the underlying concepts no better than students who never saw the demonstration." [classroom quasi-experiment] — [Mazur group page](https://mazur.harvard.edu/presentations/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment); [ComPADRE record](https://www.compadre.org/portal/items/detail.cfm?ID=2329)
- Chi & Wylie (2014) ICAP: learning increases from passive -> active -> constructive -> interactive engagement; evidence drawn from note-taking, concept-mapping and self-explanation studies. Manipulating a slider without generating an inference is "active"; predicting or explaining is "constructive." [review/framework] — [ASU record](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-wylie-r-2014-icap-framework-linking-cognitive-engagement-active-learning)

Concreteness fading and representation sequence
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014) recommend "beginning with concrete materials and then explicitly and gradually fading to the more abstract," arguing this grounds symbols, provides embodied experiences, builds memorable images and strips extraneous properties. [review] — [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036777)
- A meta-analytic review of the concrete-representational-abstract (CRA) approach reports it effective for maths concepts "from place value and basic addition to fractions and advanced math." [meta, mostly school/special-education samples] — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/385223643_A_Meta-Analytic_Review_of_the_Concrete-Representational-Abstract_Math_Approach)
- Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013) meta-analysed 57 experiments (336 tests) of learning by comparing cases; comparison of cases is the mechanism contrasting-case tasks rely on. [meta, pooled effect size not retrieved] — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712)

Analogies to prior knowledge
- Richland & Simms propose relational (analogical) reasoning as the cognitive basis of higher-order thinking and apply it to maths, science and history instruction. [review] — [UChicago PDF](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Publications_files/Richland%26Simms_WIREs.pdf)
- Evidence on analogy instruction is mixed: a practitioner summary notes analogies are linked with conceptual understanding and transfer mainly in STEM and that designers must weigh presentation, support, prior knowledge and "opportunities for generation" [review] — [Psychology in Action](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/?p=5793); Skopeliti & Vosniadou (2022) found readers with more sophisticated prior knowledge understood counter-intuitive science analogies better and gained more. [lab] — [journal record](https://ejournals.epublishing.ekt.gr/index.php/psychology/article/view/30684)
- Reverse-direction caution from the electricity literature: the water-flow analogy is a "consensus model" but with "pedagogical constraints"; some researchers "contend that flow analogies are inappropriate" [review/preprint] — [arXiv 1910.02762](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.02762); Taber's case study shows a learner misreading the water analogy to conclude current runs freely after a resistor, "a teaching model meant to support learning, which actually misleads the learner" [case study] — [science-education-research.com](https://science-education-research.com/?p=1474); engineering-technology students "continuously applied incorrect 'water-analogy' to the concepts of current flow" from freshman to senior year. [interview study] — [i-JEP](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jep/article/view/5146)

### Inferences
- The evidence converges on a two-track lesson template keyed to prior knowledge. Novice track: predict/pre-question (Q2) -> pre-train key terms -> explain with a fully worked example -> guided interactive with a stated goal and a prediction step -> completion problems -> full problems -> retrieval recap. Partial-knowledge track: brief invention/contrasting-cases attempt -> explanation that references the learner's attempt -> interactive with lighter guidance -> problems (examples optional) -> retrieval recap. Kalyuga 2001 and Reisslein 2006 are the most directly relevant studies because both used trade/electrical content.
- Because Dan is an electrician, electrical systems are an unusually strong "base" for analogies into other domains (flow, pressure, resistance, load, feedback loops), but the water-analogy literature shows that mappings must be made explicit, with the non-matching features named, or they create durable misconceptions. The same literature suggests running the analogy in the direction of strong prior knowledge (electricity -> new domain), not the reverse.
- Barbieri's finding that self-explanation prompts attached to worked examples reduced the worked-example effect, against Bisra's positive self-explanation meta (Q2), suggests the two should not be stacked on the same step for novices: study the example cleanly, then self-explain on the next (faded) step.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests the full five-part sequence (explain -> example -> interactive -> practice -> recap) as a unit; the ordering above is assembled from pairwise comparisons.
- Tetzlaff et al. 2025 expertise-reversal meta-analysis numbers and moderators were not retrieved.
- Alfieri 2013 pooled effect size for case comparison, and the Loibl 2017 mechanism labels, could not be verified from full text.
- No evidence was found on how to cheaply and reliably classify a self-study learner as "novice" vs "partial knowledge" in a pre-built course (Kalyuga's rapid dynamic assessment was not reachable).

## Key question 2: How much should a learner struggle before being told, and how should the telling be done?

### Takeaway
Short, bounded struggle helps: attempting an answer before being told (pretesting/prediction) reliably improves later learning even when the attempt fails, provided the correct answer follows and is processed. Long, unguided struggle does not help and can harm. Telling should be explicit, explain why (not just what), be delivered immediately for hard new material and may be delayed for easy or transfer-oriented material, avoid normative comparison, and focus on the task.

### Cited Findings
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009): unsuccessful retrieval attempts before being shown the answer enhanced learning for fictional facts and weak associates; "taking challenging tests-instead of avoiding errors-may be one key to effective learning." [lab] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265)
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009): asking about concepts before reading an essay beat extended reading time in all five experiments, even analysing only items not retrieved on the pretest. [lab] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751074)
- Pan & Carpenter (2023) review: the pretesting effect is demonstrated "with a variety of learning materials, despite many erroneous responses ... and in conjunction with text materials, videos, lectures, and/or correct answer feedback"; effects persist at 24 h+ retention intervals; with paired associates, only immediate feedback produced the effect, while for richer material (trivia, text) delayed feedback still worked; in authentic classrooms the effect was about 7% at end of lecture but not significant a week later in one study; in a low-dosage form it neither added to nor detracted from retrieval practice; pretesting beat posttesting by d = 0.30 and 0.14 across experiments in one comparison, though in another retrieval practice was larger. [review of lab/online/classroom] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)
- Pan & Sana (2021): "Pretesting is highly competitive with posttesting." [lab] — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2021-32127-001)
- Brod (2021): predicting differs from mere guessing; it "boosts surprise about unexpected answers, which leads to enhanced attention to the correct answer and strengthens its encoding." [review] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33768503)
- Metcalfe (2017): "errorful learning, followed by corrective feedback, helps learning"; feedback "should include analysis of the reasoning leading up to the mistake," and benefits are strongest when the learner was confident in the error (hypercorrection). [review] — [Wesleyan PDF](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf); [hypercorrection summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercorrection_(psychology))
- Self-explanation prompts: Bisra et al. (2018), 64 reports/69 effects, g = 0.55 (CI 0.45-0.65); prompts that ask learners to explain concepts beat metacognitive prompts; by format, interrogative prompts g = 0.56 (36 studies), "conceptualize" inducements g = 0.87 (13). [meta] — [BPS digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [TUM clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate elaborative interrogation ("why would this be true?") and self-explanation as moderate utility: benefits generalise across some variables but evidence is limited. [review] — [APS Observer summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade-2)
- Learning by teaching / "say it back": Kobayashi (2019), 28 studies, g about 0.35 for preparing to teach and 0.56 for teaching after preparing, vs studying without expectation of teaching; gains covered deep learning and persisted after a delay; interactive teaching gave larger gains. [meta] — [metatoc abstract](https://metatoc.com/papers/112234-learning-by-preparing-to-teach-and-teaching-a-meta-analysis)
- Feedback timing: Kulik & Kulik (1988), 53 studies: "Applied studies using actual classroom quizzes and real learning materials have usually found immediate feedback to be more effective than delayed. Experimental studies of acquisition of test content have usually produced the opposite result." [meta] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543058001079)
- Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007): after a multiple-choice initial test, delayed feedback led to superior delayed cued-recall than immediate feedback; answer-until-correct vs standard feedback made no difference; the authors attribute the benefit to spacing. [lab] — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-type-and-timing-of-feedback-on-learning-from-multip/)
- Shute (2008) guidelines (Tables 2-5): focus feedback on the task not the learner; provide elaborated feedback in manageable units; do not give normative comparisons; be cautious with overall grades; "For difficult tasks, use immediate feedback. For relatively simple tasks, use delayed feedback. For retention of procedural or conceptual knowledge, use immediate feedback"; "To promote transfer of learning, consider using delayed feedback"; low-achieving/novice learners should get immediate, directive feedback and scaffolding, high-achieving learners can take delayed or verification-only feedback; "hints may not be as helpful as more explicit, directive feedback" for novices. [review with prescriptive tables] — [FSU PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- Adesope et al. (2017): testing effects were robust whether or not feedback was given (g = 0.63 with, 0.60 without, not significantly different), but the authors note many individual studies show testing plus feedback beats testing alone. [meta] — [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- Rosenshine's principles: present new material in small steps and aim for a success rate of about 80% during guided practice, balancing confidence with challenge. [review of correlational classroom research + cognitive science] — [InnerDrive summary](https://www.innerdrive.co.uk/blog/guide-rosenshine-10-principles/); limits noted by Rosenshine & Stevens: works best where the goal is mastering a body of knowledge or a clearly stepped skill. — [Chartered College](https://my.chartered.college/early-childhood-hub/rosenshines-seminal-principles-of-instruction-a-dead-end-or-a-must-read-for-teachers-in-the-early-years/)
- Soderstrom & Bjork (2015): conditions that produce the most errors during acquisition sometimes produce the most learning; learners misread practice performance as a forecast of retention. [review] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25910388/); Bjork & Bjork (2011) desirable difficulties chapter. — [UCLA PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Struggle" in the evidence means one or two bounded attempts (a prediction, a guess, a short invention task with contrasting cases), not open-ended floundering. A practical ceiling for a phone lesson: one attempt, then tell. For Productive-Failure style invention, the attempt is longer but is scaffolded by contrasting cases and followed by an explanation that uses the learner's attempt.
- The telling for a novice should be immediate, explicit and explanatory (why the answer is right and why the learner's attempt failed), consistent with Shute, Kulik & Kulik's applied studies, and Metcalfe. Delaying feedback is defensible only for easy items or when the goal is transfer and the learner already has partial knowledge.
- A high success rate (around 80%) during practice and a prediction step with an expected surprise are compatible: the prediction is not graded, the practice is.
- Dan's "say it back" step is supported by both self-explanation (Bisra) and learning-by-teaching (Kobayashi) evidence; prompts should ask him to explain a concept or justify, not merely to reflect on how well he is doing.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis isolates the optimal duration or number of failed attempts before telling; this remains a design judgement.
- Whether pretesting benefits persist weeks later in authentic self-study is weakly supported (one classroom study showed a short-term benefit that faded by a week).
- Shute's timing prescriptions are drawn from older, mixed literature and she labels the transfer finding as needing more research.

## Key question 3: What within-course structures (quizzes, cumulative review, mixed practice) most improve retention weeks later?

### Takeaway
The three best-supported structures are retrieval practice (quizzes that require recall, with corrective feedback), spacing (re-testing after gaps that grow with the intended retention interval), and interleaving of problem types once the learner can tell them apart. Combining retrieval and spacing as "successive relearning" (relearn to criterion in several spaced sessions) is the strongest single recipe for retention weeks later. These are also the only two techniques Dunlosky et al. rate as high utility.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky et al. (2013): high utility = practice testing and distributed practice; moderate = elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice; low = summarisation, highlighting, rereading, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text. Practice testing with feedback "consistently outperforms practice testing alone." [review of >120 articles for testing] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade-2); [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=346)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), 118 articles/272 effects: overall g = 0.61 (fixed) / 0.70 (random); vs restudy g = 0.51 ("arguably the most accurate indicator ... for everyday application"); vs filler/no activity g = 0.93; practice-test format: mixed g = 0.80, multiple-choice 0.70, short-answer 0.48; identical practice/final formats g = 0.63 vs dissimilar 0.53; classroom g = 0.67 similar to lab 0.62; largest effect at 1-6 day retention intervals (g = 0.82) but robust across all intervals. [meta] — [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- Yang et al. (2021), 222 studies / 48,478 students: classroom quizzing raises achievement g about 0.499; moderated by control-condition strategy, test-format consistency, corrective feedback and number of test repetitions. [meta] — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021): 50 classroom experiments, narrow definition of classroom research, retrieval practice "consistently benefits student learning." [systematic review] — [retrievalpractice.org](https://retrievalpractice.org/review)
- Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013): interpolating short tests into a 21-min online statistics lecture roughly halved mind-wandering (non-tested viewers were off-task about 40% of the time), tripled note-taking, improved final retention, and reduced test anxiety and perceived cognitive demand. [lab, online lecture] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- Cepeda et al. (2008), >1,350 participants, gaps up to 3.5 months, final test up to a year later: performance rose then fell as the gap grew; optimal gap was about 20-40% of a one-week retention interval and about 5-10% of a one-year interval; "many educational practices are highly inefficient." [RCT, online] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), 59 studies/238 effects: interleaving g = 0.42 overall; paintings g = 0.67; mathematical tasks g = 0.34; expository texts no significant effect; word-based materials favoured blocking (g = -0.39); effect grew with between-category similarity and material complexity. [meta] — [accepted manuscript](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020): cluster RCT, 54 grade-7 maths classes, 787 students; same problems, interleaved vs blocked order over 4 months; unannounced test one month later: 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83; WWC "meets standards without reservations." [RCT] — [WWC record](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770); [PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf)
- Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky): in an intro-psychology course, flashcard retrieval spread across the term beat spaced restudy on the final test; in lab work with week-long gaps, a single week erased much of the gain from one session even when all items had been recalled; most benefit came from the first three follow-up sessions; raising the initial criterion gave only a small, short-lived advantage. [classroom + lab] — [retrievalpractice.org summary](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning); [Rawson & Dunlosky 2022 Curr Dir](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484); Higham et al. (2022) found the relearning benefit over restudy not associated with individual differences. [classroom] — [Southampton PDF](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/449677/1/Higham_et_al._in_press_JEdPsych.pdf)
- Carpenter, Pan & Butler (2022) Nature Reviews Psychology: synthesises spacing, retrieval practice and successive relearning "with an emphasis on research in applied educational settings"; cites Bahrick's 5-year spaced-retrieval vocabulary study and the finding that "retrieval practice plus feedback can increase motivation to keep on studying." [review] — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-022-00089-1)
- Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014): personalised spaced review (memory model + individual estimates) in a semester-long middle-school language class improved cumulative-exam retention by 16.5% over massed study and 10.0% over a one-size-fits-all spaced schedule, time-matched. [field experiment] — [Psychological Science record](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797613504302/)
- Kerfoot spaced education (phone/email-delivered questions): 2007 RCT, weekly emailed cases and questions improved end-of-year scores, with Cohen's d of 1.01 and 0.73 for students tested 6-8 and 9-11 months after rotation [RCT] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/); 2009 two-school RCT (n = 115) retained a topic-specific benefit at ~280 days (p = .04 for one topic, not the other) [RCT] — [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2009-kerfoot-2.pdf); residents' two-year follow-up: 70.2% vs 66.8%, effect size 0.35, spaced cohort stable while bolus cohort declined. [RCT follow-up] — [PMC table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3850724/table/T4)
- Mastery learning: Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), 108 controlled evaluations: positive exam effects, stronger for weaker students, but "self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes" and may increase time on task. [meta] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265); the widely quoted 0.52 average is attributed by Slavin (1987) to an earlier Kulik analysis, and Slavin found "essentially no evidence" for group-based mastery learning on standardised measures in K-12. [meta/critique] — [Slavin 1987 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf); Keller PSI meta (Kulik, Kulik & Cohen 1979, 75 studies): about 8% higher exam grades in PSI classes. [meta] — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232592123_A_meta-analysis_of_outcome_studies_of_Keller's_personalized_system_of_instruction)
- Bloom (1984) reported tutored mastery students about two SD above conventional classes [quasi-experimental, small studies] — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_Sigma_Problem); VanLehn (2011) found human tutoring d = 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems d = 0.76 vs no tutoring, not the believed 2.0 and 1.0. [review of experiments] — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)

### Inferences
- For retention "weeks later", the course should schedule the gap between first learning and the first review at roughly 10-20% of the target interval (Cepeda), then re-test to criterion in two or three further spaced sessions (successive relearning), using the answers the learner actually produced (this matches the app's "spaced review of what he answered").
- Quizzes should require production (short answer/explanation) at least some of the time, but mixed formats scored highest in Adesope, so a mix of recognition and recall items is both practical on a phone and evidence-aligned. Match quiz format to how the knowledge will later be used (transfer-appropriate processing).
- Interleave only after discrimination is possible: once two or more idea-types exist in a course, practice sets should mix them; for verbal/definitional content, blocking may be better.
- Mastery gating (do not advance below a criterion) has modest support for achievement but a documented cost in completion for self-paced learners; soft mastery (recommend, do not block) fits the "no guilt mechanics" rule better.

### Gaps
- Rowland (2014) testing-effect meta numbers were not retrieved (PDF blocked).
- No study was found that tests successive relearning delivered on a phone to adult hobby learners; evidence is from courses with exams.
- Interleaving evidence outside maths and visual categories is thin (EEF notes it "has only been examined in mathematics" in classrooms).

## Key question 4: How long should a lesson or session be, and how should a course be chunked, for phone-based self-study?

### Takeaway
There is no evidence-based fixed "attention span" number; the supported rules are: segment into learner-paced, meaningful chunks (multimedia segmenting effect), keep single media pieces short (MOOC video engagement caps around six minutes), interleave quizzes every few minutes of exposition, and spread the course across many short sessions rather than few long ones (spacing). Reading on screens carries a small comprehension penalty for expository text, larger under time pressure, which argues for short, self-paced text.

### Cited Findings
- Rey et al. (2019), 56 investigations/88 comparisons: segmenting multimedia into learner-paced segments gives a significant small-to-medium benefit for retention and transfer, reduces overall cognitive load and increases learning time; the effects also hold for system-paced segmentation; learners with high prior knowledge benefited more for retention. [meta] — [MPI record](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)
- Mayer (2017): segmenting d = 0.70, pre-training d = 0.46, modality d = 0.72, personalization (conversational style) d = 0.79, voice d = 0.74, embodiment d = 0.36; coherence d = 0.70, signalling d = 0.46, redundancy d = 0.87, spatial contiguity d = 0.79, temporal contiguity d = 1.30; multimedia principle median d = 1.67 from five comparisons; several principles are stronger for low- than high-knowledge learners. [syntheses of lab experiments; values differ across Mayer's editions] — [metatoc abstract](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning)
- Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant extras): Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), 58 studies, >7,500 learners, overall g = -0.33; worse when details sit beside relevant diagrams or stay on screen. [meta] — [e-teaching.org summary](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020); [WSU news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014), 6.9 million edX video sessions: "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length"; recommendation: "segment videos into short chunks, ideally less than 6 minutes." [obs] — [ACM PDF](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2556325.2566239)
- Bradbury (2016): "the available primary data do not support the concept of a 10- to 15-min attention limit"; the most-cited source for rapid attention decline "barely discusses student attention at all"; the biggest variance in attention is between teachers, not formats. [review/editorial] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)
- Szpunar et al. (2013): tests interpolated into a 21-min online lecture halved mind-wandering and improved retention. [lab] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- Delgado et al. (2018), 54 studies/171,055 participants: paper beat screens for comprehension by g = -0.21; the gap was larger under time pressure, present for informational texts but not narrative, and grew over publication years. [meta] — [UV PDF](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)
- Sung, Chang & Liu (2016), 110 studies: mobile devices in teaching gave a moderate mean effect of 0.523 vs comparison conditions. [meta, mostly school/college, not hobby self-study] — [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effects-of-integrating-mobile-devices-with-teaching-and-learn-2)
- Microlearning: Senadheera et al. (2024) pooled five higher-education studies: microlearning (n = 344) beat macro-learning (n = 310) on post-test scores by a mean difference of 12.6 points (95% CI 1.2-23.9). [meta, small, immediate post-tests] — [JMTR](https://jmtr.sljol.info/en/articles/2); Monib et al. (2024) describe microlearning effectiveness research as scattered and mixed [systematic review] — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11774797/); De Gagne et al. (2019): positive effects on knowledge and confidence in health-professions students, but only 5/17 studies measured behaviour. [scoping review] — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6683654/)
- Spacing of sessions (Cepeda 2008) and spaced education via short emailed questions (Kerfoot) are the strongest evidence that many short, separated sessions beat fewer long ones for retention (see Q3 findings). [RCTs]
- Kulik et al. (1990): self-paced mastery programs often reduce completion in college classes [meta] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265); Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019): 3.13% of MOOC participants completed in 2017-18, barely changed over six years; verified (paying) learners 46%. [obs, 5.63 million learners] — [MIT TSL summary](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/the-mooc-pivot/); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/7744)

### Inferences
- A defensible lesson unit for a phone: one idea per lesson, built from 2-6 minute segments (text or interactive), each ending in a retrieval or prediction prompt, with the learner controlling progression; total lesson length is then a count of segments rather than a clock target. The "6 minutes" figure is an engagement observation on video, not a learning limit, so it should be treated as a chunk size heuristic.
- Because screen reading of expository text is penalised mainly under time pressure, the page should never impose timers on reading and should keep paragraphs short with key terms signalled (Mayer's signalling, the app's amber key-term highlighting).
- Course chunking: many small lessons scheduled over days with cumulative spaced quizzes, rather than long modules; this is consistent with the spacing data and with the microlearning evidence, though the latter is weak.
- Session length should be left to the learner (learner-paced segmentation is where the evidence is), with a natural stopping point at every segment boundary so "one tap away" study is possible.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on optimal lesson length in minutes for adult phone self-study; the microlearning literature is small, heterogeneous and mostly uses immediate post-tests.
- Rey 2019 effect sizes (retention/transfer g) were not retrieved; only "small to medium."
- Sung 2016 and the MOOC completion data are from formal education contexts and may not transfer to a single motivated adult using a private app.

## Key question 5: Which popular techniques does the evidence NOT support?

### Takeaway
Learning-styles matching, highlighting, rereading and summarising (as usually done), interesting-but-irrelevant embellishments, unguided simulations/discovery, and points-badges-leaderboards gamification as a motivational engine all lack support or show harm. Hattie-style effect-size league tables should not be used to rank techniques.

### Cited Findings
- Learning styles: Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008) found only one study giving partial support to the meshing hypothesis and two contradicting it: "If classification of students' learning styles has practical utility, it remains to be demonstrated." [review] — [USF record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Deans for Impact summary](https://deansforimpact.org/learning-styles-what-does-the-research-say/); a 2024 study of 222 prospective teachers found no interaction between presentation mode and learning style. [lab] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09689-1)
- Highlighting, rereading, summarisation, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text: low utility in Dunlosky et al. (2013). [review] — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade-2)
- Seductive details harm learning, g = -0.33 (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020). [meta] — [summary](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020)
- Unassisted discovery: d = -0.38 vs explicit instruction (Alfieri 2011). [meta] — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2010-23599-001); pure discovery "three strikes" (Mayer 2004). [review] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14736316)
- Unguided simulations: de Jong & van Joolingen (1998) review the problems learners have with discovery in simulation environments and how instructional support addresses them [review] — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00190680); D'Angelo et al. (2014): adding scaffolding/feedback to a simulation improves achievement over the same simulation unmodified, g+ = 0.49 (50 effects). [meta] — [SRI brief](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf)
- Gamification: Sailer & Homner (2020) found small effects (cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36, behavioural g = 0.25) but only the cognitive estimate held in methodologically rigorous studies [meta] — [Augsburg record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); Hanus & Fox (2015), 16-week two-section comparison: the badges-plus-leaderboard section ended lower on motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and final exam scores, mediated by intrinsic motivation [quasi-experiment] — [review table citing it](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13); Mekler et al. (2017): points, levels and leaderboards did not change competence or intrinsic motivation and acted as extrinsic incentives raising output quantity [lab] — [Basel record](https://edoc.unibas.ch/32047/); gamification "suffers from the novelty effect." [quasi-experiment] — [ETHE paper](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6/figures/2)
- Serious games: Wouters et al. (2013), 77 learning comparisons: games beat conventional instruction on learning d = 0.29 (retention d = 0.36) but were not more motivating (d = 0.26, authors conclude no motivational advantage); gains were larger when games were combined with other instruction and spread over several sessions. [meta] — [UU record](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-cognitive-and-motivational-effects-of-seri/)
- "Attention span of 8 seconds / 10 minutes": not supported by primary data (Bradbury 2016). [review] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)
- Hattie's effect-size rankings: Simpson argues effect sizes are confounded with design features (comparison groups, range restriction, measurement design) that correlate with intervention type, so cross-study rankings mislead [methodological critique] — [CEM blog](https://cem.org/blog/serious-critiques-of-meta-analysis-and-effect-size-researched-2018); [Lovell podcast notes](https://www.ollielovell.com/johnhattie/); Bergeron & Rivard (2017) gave a statistician's critique in the McGill Journal of Education, and reader-reported sign errors in Visible Learning categories are unverified. — [Didau post](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/visible-learning-invisible-errors); Kraft (2020): effects "small by Cohen's standards are large relative to the impacts of most field-based interventions"; benchmarks ignore cost and scalability. [review] — [Annenberg record](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/interpreting-effect-sizes-education-interventions)
- EEF (2021), 295 classroom studies across spacing, interleaving, retrieval, cognitive load, schemas, multimedia/dual coding and embodied learning: approaches "can meaningfully affect pupil learning" but applied evidence is thin, strongest for maths and science and older pupils; principles have more support than specific techniques; "not a silver bullet." [systematic review] — [EEF page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/evidence-reviews/cognitive-science-approaches-in-the-classroom); [EEF press release](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-what-do-we-really-know-about-cognitive-science-in-the-classroom-eef-publishes-new-review-of-the-evidence/)
- Behavioural nudges at scale: Kizilcec et al. (2020), 247 courses, ~250,000 learners: expected medium-to-large effects "not supported"; scaling "can reduce their average effectiveness by an order-of-magnitude." [RCTs] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)

### Inferences
- The app should not offer "visual/auditory" modes as a learning-style feature; dark mode and text size are accessibility settings, which is the right framing.
- Points, streak counters and leaderboards should not be relied on to sustain study; if any game element is used, the evidence favours informational competence feedback (progress on mastery) over social comparison, consistent with the "no guilt mechanics" rule.
- For this project the lab literature may transfer better than the classroom literature: the lab studies are typically individual learners working through computer-delivered materials, which is closer to Dan's situation than a teacher-led classroom. The EEF caveat still applies to any claim about long-run outcomes.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence on streaks specifically (Duolingo research was not found).
- Hattie's specific numbers for mastery learning and direct instruction were not retrieved; only the critiques.

## Supporting question A: When do simulations and interactives teach, and when are they "play without learning"?

### Takeaway
Simulations beat non-simulation instruction on achievement (g+ = 0.62) and are better still when wrapped with scaffolding and feedback (g+ = 0.49 over the plain version). Guidance of any type helps inquiry, but heavy worksheet-style guidance suppresses exploration; the PhET evidence favours "minimal but nonzero" guidance with a goal, a prediction and a reflection. Interactivity without inference (the ICAP "active" level) is not enough.

### Cited Findings
- D'Angelo et al. (2014) SRI meta-analysis, 59 K-12 studies/128 effects: simulations vs similar instruction without them, achievement g+ = 0.62 (46 effects, 23% improvement index); enhanced simulations (extra scaffolding, certain feedback) vs the same simulation unmodified g+ = 0.49 (50 effects); inquiry/reasoning skills g+ = 0.26 (n.s., 6 effects) and enhanced-vs-plain g+ = 0.41; non-cognitive g+ = 0.69; number of sessions moderated achievement; non-embedded technology assessments showed smaller outcomes than embedded ones. [meta] — [SRI brief](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf)
- Lazonder & Harmsen (2016), 72 studies: guidance in inquiry learning improved learning activities d = 0.66, performance success d = 0.71 and learning outcomes d = 0.50; guidance type moderated only performance success; age conclusions were not firm. Guidance types range from process constraints and status overviews (least directive) to prompts, heuristics and scaffolds (most). [meta] — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.3102/0034654315627366); [TIE summary](https://www.tieonline.com/article/1967/the-goldilocks-level-of-teacher-support-in-inquiry-learning)
- Chamberlain, Lancaster, Parson & Perkins (2014), PhET Acid-Base Solutions with light/moderate/heavy guidance worksheets: interaction with exploratory features fell as guidance rose (85% / 68% / 9%, p < .0005); lighter-guidance students also explored more and attended more to their interactions on a redraw task a week later. [classroom experiment] — [RSC](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2014/rp/c4rp00009a)
- Adams, Paulson & Wieman (2008), ~250 interviews: "Minimal but nonzero guidance with many of these simulations creates the optimum engaged exploration." [interview study] — [PERC abstract](https://www.per-central.org/perc/2008/detail.cfm?ID=726); Podolefsky, Perkins & Adams (2010): with minimal explicit guidance, students explored wave interference "in ways that bear similarity to how scientists explore phenomena." [interview study] — [PDF](https://rachel.core2learn.org/modules/en-phet-static/publications/prst-per-2010.pdf)
- de Jong & van Joolingen (1998) catalogue learners' problems in simulation discovery (hypothesis generation, experiment design, data interpretation, regulation) and the instructional supports that address them. [review] — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00190680)
- A Newton's-laws simulation study (52 undergraduates): animated graphical feedback plus brief multimedia explanations improved both implicit and explicit understanding; graphics alone built tacit knowledge but not explicit understanding (attribution to Rieber and colleagues inferred; see Gaps). [lab] — [academia.edu abstract](https://midland.academia.edu/KellyTribble)
- Crouch et al. (2004): passive observation of a demonstration was no better than no demonstration; predicting first helped. [classroom] — [Mazur group](https://mazur.harvard.edu/presentations/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment)
- Moreno & Mayer (2007): guided activity, reflection, feedback, pacing control and pretraining are the supported principles for interactive environments. [review] — [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9047-2)
- Chi & Wylie (2014): learning rises from passive to active to constructive to interactive engagement. [framework/review] — [ASU record](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-wylie-r-2014-icap-framework-linking-cognitive-engagement-active-learning)

### Inferences
- The "play without learning" failure mode is an interactive used at the ICAP "active" level: dragging sliders with no prediction, no goal and no explanation. The fix with the most evidence is a thin wrapper: pre-train the controls and terms, state one goal, ask for a prediction, let the learner explore with few constraints, then ask for an explanation and give explanatory feedback.
- Heavy step-by-step instructions inside the interactive suppress exploration (9% exploratory interaction under heavy guidance); the guidance should sit around the interactive (goal, prediction, reflection), not script every click.
- The D'Angelo "number of sessions" moderator suggests revisiting the same interactive in later spaced reviews rather than using it once.

### Gaps
- All simulation meta-analytic evidence is K-12 or undergraduate; none is from adult hobby self-study.
- The Rieber, Tzeng & Tribble (2004) paper could not be located by title; the matching abstract was found only on an unattributed profile page.
- Wieman, Adams & Perkins (2008) Science piece was reachable only as a citation.

## Supporting question B: What sustains adult self-study, and what does gamification do and not do?

### Takeaway
Intrinsic interest and personally valued goals (identified regulation) are the motivation types most associated with persistence; rewards and avoidance are not. Reliable behavioural levers are weak at scale: planning prompts helped in one set of MOOCs and failed in four RCTs elsewhere; implementation intentions have a medium meta-analytic effect in general goal pursuit. Retrieval practice with feedback itself increases willingness to keep studying. Gamification produces small, unstable motivational effects and can backfire via social comparison.

### Cited Findings
- Howard et al. (2021), 344 samples/223,209 participants: intrinsic motivation related to success and well-being; identified regulation (personal value) "particularly highly related to persistence"; external regulation (rewards/punishment) unrelated to performance or persistence and associated with decreased well-being; amotivation related to poor outcomes. Associational. [meta] — [ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8w8z7/student-motivation-and-associated-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-from-self-determination-theory)
- Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), 94 tests: implementation intentions ("if situation X, then I do Y") d = 0.65 on goal achievement [meta] — [Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes); a later 642-test synthesis: .27 <= d <= .66, larger with if-then format, high motivation and rehearsal. [meta] — [Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- Yeomans & Reich (2017): open-ended planning prompts at course start in three HarvardX courses increased completion by 29% and certificate payment by 40%; plans focused on specific times predicted non-completion [field experiment] — [MIT TSL](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/planning-prompts-increase-and-forecast-course-completion-in-massive-open-online-courses/); Andor et al. ran four RCTs on openHPI/openSAP and found no overall completion effect (effects outside [-7%, 3%] ruled out), with a 19% gain in one course. [RCTs] — [RWI](https://www.rwi-essen.de/en/publications/scientific/ruhr-economic-papers/detail/do-planning-prompts-increase-educational-success-evidence-1051)
- Kizilcec et al. (2020): scaled behavioural interventions in 247 courses gave small, context-dependent benefits; machine-learning targeting helped little. [RCTs] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)
- Carpenter, Pan & Butler (2022) cite evidence that "retrieval practice plus feedback can increase motivation to keep on studying." [lab, via review] — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-022-00089-1)
- Szpunar et al. (2013): interpolated tests reduced anxiety about a cumulative test and perceived cognitive demand. [lab] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- Kulik et al. (1990): mastery programs improved attitudes toward content but self-paced versions reduced college completion. [meta] — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265)
- Sailer & Homner (2020): gamification g = 0.49 cognitive, 0.36 motivational, 0.25 behavioural; only the cognitive estimate stable in rigorous studies; game fiction, social interaction and competition-plus-collaboration moderated behavioural outcomes. [meta] — [Augsburg record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- Hanus & Fox (2015): compulsory badges and a leaderboard reduced motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and exam scores over a semester. [quasi-experiment] — [review table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)
- Mekler et al. (2017): points/levels/leaderboards raised output quantity without raising intrinsic motivation. [lab] — [Basel record](https://edoc.unibas.ch/32047/)
- Shute (2008): low-achieving learners should not receive normative feedback but self-referenced feedback on their own progress; do not draw attention to "self" during learning. [review] — [FSU PDF](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
- Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019): 3.13% completion among all MOOC participants vs 46% among verified (paying) learners, 2017-18. [obs] — [MIT TSL](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/the-mooc-pivot/)

### Inferences
- The strongest motivational design levers consistent with the evidence are: make the next step always visible and small (a form of implementation intention: "when I open the app, I do today's one review"), show self-referenced progress toward mastery rather than scores against others, and let retrieval-with-feedback itself supply the competence signal.
- Completion statistics from MOOCs are about anonymous free enrolment; the verified-learner rate (46%) is a better proxy for a self-chosen personal course, and still implies that a course design should tolerate pauses without penalty.
- Any planning prompt the app uses should be optional and generic (what and why) rather than demanding specific times, which predicted non-completion in Yeomans & Reich.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on motivation in single-user, self-built learning apps; all evidence is from courses with cohorts or institutions.
- Ryan & Deci's 2020 SDT review was not located; SDT findings are via Howard et al. and secondary descriptions.
- No sourced evidence on streaks or habit-formation timelines for study behaviour.

## Key question 6: Specific, actionable design rules for a pre-built course author, with key citations

### Takeaway
The rules below are each tied to at least one meta-analysis or multiple RCTs unless marked otherwise; where evidence is lab-only or review-only this is stated. They are grouped by lesson flow, within-lesson media, practice and review, interactives, and motivation.

### Cited Findings
Lesson flow
1. Branch on prior knowledge, not style: examples-first for novices, problems-first for learners with some knowledge. [lab, trade/electrical samples] — [Kalyuga 2001](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/); [Reisslein 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf); [KSC 2006](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ736299)
2. Open with a prediction or pre-question, then always show and explain the answer. [lab + review] — [Kornell 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265); [Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5); [Crouch 2004](https://mazur.harvard.edu/presentations/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment)
3. Pre-train names and parts before the main explanation. [lab syntheses] — [Mayer 2017](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning); [Moreno & Mayer 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9047-2)
4. Explain explicitly, with one correct fully worked example; do not attach self-explanation prompts to the example itself. [meta] — [Barbieri 2023](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [Alfieri 2011](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2010-23599-001)
5. Fade: completion problems with missing steps, then full problems; ask the learner to name the principle behind each faded step. [lab + field] — [Renkl & Atkinson](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196); [Atkinson 2003](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/transitioning-from-studying-examples-to-solving-problems-effects-/)
6. For partial-knowledge learners, use a short invention task with contrasting cases before the explanation, and have the explanation refer back to the attempt. [meta + review] — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105); [Loibl 2017](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/towards-a-theory-of-when-and-how-problem-solving-followed-by-inst/); [Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf)
7. Start concrete, fade to abstract, and say explicitly which features of an analogy transfer and which do not. [review + case studies] — [Fyfe 2014](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036777); [Taber on the water analogy](https://science-education-research.com/?p=1474)
8. End each lesson with a "say it back" explanation prompt (explain the concept or justify a step), not a how-did-I-do reflection. [meta] — [Bisra 2018](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [Kobayashi 2019](https://metatoc.com/papers/112234-learning-by-preparing-to-teach-and-teaching-a-meta-analysis)
9. Aim for about 80% success in guided practice; present material in small steps. [classroom research synthesis] — [Rosenshine summary](https://www.innerdrive.co.uk/blog/guide-rosenshine-10-principles/)

Within-lesson media
10. Segment into learner-paced chunks with a natural stop after each. [meta] — [Rey 2019](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)
11. Cut decorative extras; keep labels next to the thing they label; signal key terms. [meta + lab syntheses] — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020); [Mayer 2017](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning)
12. Write in a conversational second-person voice (personalization d = 0.79). [lab syntheses] — [Mayer 2017](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning)
13. Keep any single media piece under about six minutes and never time-pressure reading on a phone. [obs + meta] — [Guo 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/2556325.2566239); [Delgado 2018](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)

Practice and review
14. Quiz with recall, give corrective feedback, mix formats, and match the format to how the knowledge will be used. [meta] — [Adesope 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Yang 2021](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
15. Interpolate short quizzes every few minutes of exposition. [lab] — [Szpunar 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
16. Schedule the first review at roughly 10-20% of the target retention interval and re-test to criterion in two or three further spaced sessions. [RCT + classroom] — [Cepeda 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484); [Lindsey 2014](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797613504302/)
17. Interleave problem types in practice sets once more than one type exists; block for definitional/verbal items. [meta + RCT] — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [Rohrer 2020](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)
18. Give immediate, explanatory feedback on hard new material; delay only for easy items or transfer goals; never compare to others. [meta + review] — [Kulik & Kulik 1988](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543058001079); [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf); [Butler 2007](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-type-and-timing-of-feedback-on-learning-from-multip/)
19. Use soft mastery: recommend revisiting below criterion, but do not block progress. [meta] — [Kulik 1990](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265)

Interactives
20. Wrap every interactive with a goal, a prediction, light guidance and a reflection/explanation; do not script every click. [meta + classroom] — [D'Angelo 2014](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf); [Lazonder & Harmsen 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.3102/0034654315627366); [Chamberlain 2014](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2014/rp/c4rp00009a)
21. Pair graphical feedback in a simulation with short explanations. [lab] — [Newton's-laws simulation abstract](https://midland.academia.edu/KellyTribble)
22. Reuse interactives in later spaced reviews rather than once. [meta moderator] — [D'Angelo 2014](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf)

Motivation
23. Show self-referenced progress toward mastery; avoid leaderboards, compulsory badges and scores against peers. [meta + quasi-experiment + review] — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [Hanus & Fox 2015 via review](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13); [Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)
24. Keep today's study one tap away and small (an if-then cue); optional generic planning prompts only. [meta + field] — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes); [Yeomans & Reich 2017](https://tsl.mit.edu/research/planning-prompts-increase-and-forecast-course-completion-in-massive-open-online-courses/); [Andor et al.](https://www.rwi-essen.de/en/publications/scientific/ruhr-economic-papers/detail/do-planning-prompts-increase-educational-success-evidence-1051)
25. Do not build learning-style modes, highlighting tools or reread-to-review loops. [review] — [Pashler 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade-2)

### Inferences
- Classroom-to-self-study transfer caveats to attach to the rules: Rosenshine (rule 9), Yang 2021 (rule 14), Rohrer 2020 (rule 17) and the EEF review are classroom findings; the lab studies underpinning rules 2, 4, 5, 10-13, 15, 16 and 18 are closer to a lone adult on a device, but they mostly used undergraduates and short retention intervals.
- Taken together, the rules describe a lesson of roughly: pre-question -> pre-train -> explain + example -> guided interactive (predict, play, explain) -> faded practice with immediate explanatory feedback -> say-it-back recap -> spaced, mixed, cumulative review driven by the learner's own answers. This is consistent with Dan's brief (predict, play, explain, say it back, quick checks, spaced review).
