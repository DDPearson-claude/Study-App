## Key question 1: What lesson sequence does each framework prescribe, and where do they agree (a common core) and disagree?

### Takeaway
Every mainstream framework prescribes the same skeleton for a lesson: (1) orient and connect to what the learner already knows, (2) show or let the learner meet the new idea with guidance, (3) have the learner do something with it and get feedback, (4) consolidate and point toward transfer. They disagree mainly on *who goes first* (teacher explanation before practice, as in Gagné/Hunter/Rosenshine/Archer-Hughes, versus a problem or exploration before the explanation, as in Merrill, 4C/ID, 5E and productive failure), on *granularity* (small steps versus whole tasks), and on how much the learner should discover unaided (almost none, per the evidence).

### Cited Findings

**Gagné's nine events (1965; Gagné, Briggs & Wager 1992)**
- The nine events in order: 1 gain attention; 2 inform learners of objectives; 3 stimulate recall of prior learning; 4 present the content; 5 provide learning guidance (scaffolding, examples and non-examples, "case studies, visual images, analogies, and metaphors"); 6 elicit performance (practice); 7 provide feedback; 8 assess performance; 9 enhance retention and transfer ("avoid isolating course content", have students convert information from one format to another, e.g., concept maps). The events are "associated with and address the mental conditions for learning" and "can be modified to fit both the content and students' level of knowledge" — [NIU CITL, adapted from Gagné, Briggs & Wager 1992](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml)
- NIU lists feedback types for event 7: confirmatory, evaluative, remedial ("directs students to find the correct answer but does not provide the correct answer"), descriptive/analytic, and peer/self-evaluation — [NIU CITL](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml)

**Merrill's First Principles of Instruction (2002) and pebble-in-the-pond**
- The paper is Merrill, M. D. (2002), "First principles of instruction", Educational Technology Research and Development 50(3), 43–59; it set out to identify "fundamental invariant principles of good instructional design, regardless of pedagogic strategy" — [Wikipedia summary with citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Principles_of_Instruction); [Springer chapter citing it](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-030-26248-8_56-2.pdf)
- The five principles as summarised: problem/task-centred (a progression of whole tasks of rising complexity, "scaffold the learning process into manageable tiers of difficulty"); activation ("directed to recall prior knowledge, to recall a structure for organizing that knowledge, or are given a structure for organizing new knowledge"); demonstration (show, not just tell, tied to the task); application ("perform real-world tasks or solve real-world problems and receive feedback and appropriate guidance"); integration ("reflection, discussion, debate, and/or presentation of new knowledge") — [EduTechWiki](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/First_principles_of_instruction); [SUNY Cortland summary](https://web.cortland.edu/frieda/ID/IDtheories/44.html)
- Merrill describes the four phases as a cycle around a problem: "activation, demonstration, application, and integration" and claims they promote "effective, efficient, and engaging (e3) instruction" — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-030-26248-8_56-2.pdf)
- Pebble-in-the-pond (Merrill 2002, Performance Improvement 41(7), 39–44) is the design procedure: concentric "ripples" — the whole problem, a progression of problems of rising complexity, analysis of the skills/knowledge each needs, strategy, interface design, production — [EduTechWiki: Pebble in the pond](https://EduTechWiki.unige.ch/en/Pebble_in_the_pond)
- A Goodreads reviewer of Merrill's 2013 book noted it "did not validate the suggestions through supporting research or evidence" (reader opinion, not a study) — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/14766087-first-principles-of-instruction)

**4C/ID and Ten Steps to Complex Learning (van Merriënboer 1997; van Merriënboer & Kirschner 2007)**
- The basic assumption: "blueprints for complex learning can always be described by four basic components: learning tasks, supportive information, procedural information, and part-task practice." Learning tasks "provide authentic, whole-task experiences based on real-life tasks", "are organized in easy-to-difficult task classes", "have diminishing support in each task class (scaffolding)" and "show high variability of practice". Supportive information "supports the learning and performance of nonrecurrent aspects" (problem solving, reasoning, mental models) and "is typically presented before learners start to work on a new task class and kept available". Procedural information "precisely specifies how to perform routine aspects of the task, e.g., through step-by-step instruction", "is presented just in time ... and quickly fades". Part-task practice "provides a huge amount of repetition" for selected recurrent aspects and "only starts after the recurrent aspect has been introduced in the context of the whole task (i.e., in a fruitful cognitive context)" — [Kirschner & van Merriënboer, "Ten Steps to Complex Learning" chapter](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf)
- Sequencing rule: learners get "variable whole-task practice at a particular difficulty level until they reach the prespecified standards for this level, whereupon they continue to the next, more complex or difficult task class"; the model is meant for programmes "ranging in length from several weeks to several years" — [same chapter](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf)
- "Rather than working from part-tasks to the whole task, simple-to-complex versions of the whole task are used to set up the training program" — [SAGE Encyclopedia of Educational Technology](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/the-sage-encyclopedia-of-educational-technology/chpt/fourcomponent-instructional-design-4c-id)
- Original formal statement: van Merriënboer, Clark & de Croock (2002), "Blueprints for complex learning: the 4C/ID-model", ETR&D 50(2), 39–64 — [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ652497)

**Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012)**
- The ten principles: (1) begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning; (2) present new material in small steps with student practice after each step; (3) ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students; (4) provide models; (5) guide student practice; (6) check for student understanding; (7) obtain a high success rate; (8) provide scaffolds for difficult tasks; (9) require and monitor independent practice; (10) engage students in weekly and monthly review. They come from "(a) research in cognitive science, (b) research on master teachers, and (c) research on cognitive supports" and the three sources have "no conflict at all" — [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ971753); [Education Digest listing](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1002984)
- Quantitative claims in the article: "the most effective mathematics teachers spent about 23 minutes of a 40-minute period in lecture, demonstration, questioning, and working examples"; the least effective teachers "asked only nine questions in a 40-minute period"; in fourth-grade maths "82 percent of students' answers were correct in the classrooms of the most successful teachers, but the least successful teachers had a success rate of only 73 percent"; "the optimal success rate for fostering student achievement appears to be about 80 percent"; hands-on activities came "always after, not before, the basic material was learned" — [Rosenshine 2012, AFT PDF](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)

**Understanding by Design / backward design (Wiggins & McTighe)**
- Three stages: identify desired results; determine acceptable evidence ("think more like an assessor than an activity planner"; evidence includes performance tasks that "apply their learning to a new and authentic situation" plus "other evidence"); plan learning experiences. Goal is "the ability to make meaning of 'big ideas' and transfer their learning" — [NSW Dept of Education backward design guide](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/planning-a-sequence-of-lessons/backward-design-model)
- Six facets of understanding: explain, interpret, apply, shift perspective, empathise, self-assess; WHERETO as the lesson/unit checklist (Where/why; Hook; Equip/Explore/Experience; Rethink/Revise; Evaluate; Tailor; Organise) — [NSW guide](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/planning-a-sequence-of-lessons/backward-design-model); [Course Hero study guide (secondary)](https://www.coursehero.com/lit/Understanding-by-Design/plot-summary/)

**Bloom's taxonomy and mastery learning as design tools**
- Anderson & Krathwohl's 2001 revision has six levels from Remember through Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate to Create, used to select verbs for outcomes — [University of Liverpool verb guide](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/media/livacuk/centre-for-innovation-in-education/staff-guides/learning-outcomes-with-blooms-verb-guide/learning-outcomes-with-blooms-verb-guide.pdf)
- Mastery learning: "Students must demonstrate a high level of success on tests, typically at about the 80% level, before progressing to new content"; those who fall short get additional tuition or support; EEF rates it high impact (+5 months) for very low cost "based on limited evidence"; Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990, RER) pooled 108 controlled evaluations, found positive effects strongest for weaker students, but "self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes" — [EEF technical appendix](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning/technical-appendix); [Kulik et al. 1990 (academia.edu copy)](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)

**Hunter's lesson design and gradual release ("I do, we do, you do")**
- Hunter & Russell's 1976 elements: anticipatory set, objective/purpose, input, modelling, checking for understanding, guided practice, independent practice (closure sometimes added). Hunter "never used" the term "seven-step lesson plan" and Wolfe (ASCD 1987) argued they are "useful elements rather than a rigid formula" — [CSUN summary of Hunter](https://csun.edu/science/ref/plans/lesson_design_hunter.html); [Wolfe, Educational Leadership 1987](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/journals/ed_lead/el_198702_wolfe.pdf)
- Gradual release: Pearson & Gallagher (1983) described a continuum of responsibility moving from teacher to student in reading comprehension; Fisher & Frey later expanded it to four phases (focused instruction, guided instruction, collaborative, independent); the shift "may also take place over days, weeks, or the school year" — [flipeducation summary](https://flipeducation.ai/teaching-wiki/gradual-release)

**Direct Instruction (Engelmann) and explicit instruction (Archer & Hughes 2011)**
- Archer & Hughes define explicit instruction as "a structured, systematic, and effective methodology for teaching academic skills ... unambiguous and direct", quoting Rosenshine (1987): "a systematic method of teaching with emphasis on proceeding in small steps, checking for student understanding, and achieving active and successful participation by all students". Their 16 elements include: focus on critical content; sequence skills logically; break complex skills into smaller units ("Once mastered, units are synthesized"); organised focused lessons; begin with a clear statement of goals and "why it is important"; review prior skills; step-by-step demonstrations with think-alouds; clear concise language; "an adequate range of examples and non-examples"; guided and supported practice with difficulty increased as guidance decreases; require frequent responses; brisk pace; distributed and cumulative practice. Rosenshine & Stevens' six teaching functions: review, presentation, guided practice, corrections and feedback, independent practice, weekly and monthly reviews — [Archer & Hughes ch. 1, Guilford](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)
- Their sample lesson: "1. Have students complete five review items as a warm-up activity. 2. Review any additional prerequisites ... 3. Establish the goal and relevance of today's lesson. 4. Model the new skill/strategy. 5. Provide guided practice ... 6. Introduce independent practice ... 7. Provide small-group instruction to struggling students" — [Archer & Hughes ch. 1](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)
- Engelmann's Direct Instruction is the Project Follow Through model that "has endured, more or less intact, for more than 35 years" — [SAGE encyclopedia entry](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/cbt/chpt/project-follow-through-direct-instruction)

**5E (Bybee, BSCS)**
- Phases: engagement, exploration, explanation, elaboration, evaluation, derived from the Atkin & Karplus (1962) learning cycle (explore, explain, elaborate = the "3E"); "the teacher serves as a facilitator"; BSCS introduced the model in 1990 and Bybee et al. (2006) counted over 235,000 lesson plans using it — [Polanin et al. 2024 meta-analysis, ERIC EJ1455319](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1455319.pdf)
- BSCS traces its origins to Herbart and Dewey and says the model's sequence is supported by How People Learn — [BSCS report page](https://bscs.org/reports/the-bscs-5e-instructional-model-origins-and-effectiveness/); [BSCS 2006 executive summary](https://bscs.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/bscs_5e_executive_summary-1.pdf)

**Reigeluth's elaboration theory**
- The first lesson is an *epitome*: it "epitomizes, rather than summarizes or abstracts" the content, built on a single content type (concepts, procedures or principles) at the application level; subsequent lessons elaborate; the Simplifying Conditions Method picks "the simplest version of a task that still represents the whole task" then teaches "progressively harder versions"; supporting content is introduced alongside the steps it relates to; proponents claim "more stable cognitive structures", better retention/transfer and motivation (claims, not reported trials) — [EduTechWiki](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Elaboration_theory); [SUNY Cortland](https://web.cortland.edu/frieda/ID/IDtheories/15.html); [TECFA summary](https://tecfa.unige.ch/themes/sa2/act-app-dos2-fic-elaborat.htm)

**Keller's ARCS**
- Keller (1987), Journal of Instructional Development 10(3), 2–10: the model "contains a four category synthesis of variables [Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction] ... and a motivational design process that is compatible with typical instructional design models"; field-tested in two in-service teacher programmes; Keller said it "warrants more controlled studies" — [Keller 1987 PDF (TU Delft OCW)](https://ocw.tudelft.nl/wp-content/uploads/Development-and-Use-of-the-ARCS-Model-of-Instructional-Design.pdf)
- Sub-strategies: Attention via perceptual arousal (surprise) and inquiry arousal (posing problems); Relevance via goal orientation and motive matching; Confidence via learning requirements, success opportunities, personal responsibility; Satisfaction via intrinsic reinforcement, extrinsic rewards, equity — [EBSCO research starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/kellers-arcs-model)

**Knowles' andragogy**
- Six assumptions in the later versions: need to know why; self-concept moving toward self-direction; experience as a resource; readiness tied to social roles; "life-centered, task-centered, or problem-centered" orientation; internal motivation. Knowles started with four assumptions (1980) and added a fifth in 1984; the infed entry (Smith 2002) is the standard secondary account — [infed: Malcolm Knowles](https://infed.org/dir/welcome/thinkers/et-knowl.htm); [slide summary of assumptions](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/how-do-adults-learn/231687)

**Common core and points of disagreement (cross-framework)**
- Merrill presents the First Principles as the shared core of many effective models and the Springer chapter says they "encompass the general phases of effective problem-centered instruction" — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-030-26248-8_56-2.pdf)
- Rosenshine and Archer-Hughes put hands-on work "always after, not before, the basic material was learned"; 5E and productive failure put exploration/problem-solving first — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf); [Sinha & Kapur 2021 summary](https://futurity.org/?p=2629782)
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) argue "the advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge" — [Kirschner et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf)

### Inferences
- Mapped onto one timeline, the frameworks line up almost one-to-one: Gagné 1–3 = Hunter's anticipatory set/objective = Archer-Hughes 5–6 = Rosenshine 1 = Merrill "activation" = 5E "engage" = ARCS "attention/relevance". Gagné 4–5 = Hunter input/modelling = Rosenshine 2,4 = Merrill "demonstration" = 5E "explain" = 4C/ID "supportive + procedural information". Gagné 6–7 = guided then independent practice = Merrill "application" = 5E "elaborate" = 4C/ID "learning tasks". Gagné 8–9 = Hunter closure = Rosenshine 10 = Merrill "integration" = 5E "evaluate". The disagreement is confined to the order of the middle two blocks and the grain size of practice.
- For a self-study interactive where the "exploration" is a bounded simulation with a prediction prompt and immediate feedback, the explicit-first and explore-first camps converge: Alfieri et al.'s "enhanced discovery" (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) is exactly that bounded form (see KQ2).

## Key question 2: Which frameworks have strong evidence for self-study, interactive courses, and which are mostly tradition?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced *ingredients* are the cognitive-science ones that most frameworks share: retrieval practice with feedback, spaced and cumulative practice, worked examples that fade as expertise grows, self-explanation prompts, and Mayer's multimedia principles. Among whole frameworks, explicit/direct instruction (Rosenshine, Archer-Hughes, Engelmann) and 4C/ID have meta-analytic support; 5E has strong RCT evidence in school science but little in adult self-study; Merrill's First Principles, UbD, Hunter, elaboration theory, ARCS and andragogy are mostly design logic plus field tests, with ARCS the exception (one meta-analysis). Unassisted discovery is the one approach the evidence clearly rejects.

### Cited Findings

**Whole-framework evidence**
- Direct Instruction: Stockard et al. (2018, RER 88(4), 479–507) analysed "328 studies involving 413 study designs and almost 4,000 effects" from 1966–2016; "All of the estimated effects were positive and all were statistically significant except results from metaregressions involving affective outcomes ... Effects showed little decline during maintenance, and effects for academic subjects were greater when students had more exposure"; effects were "moderate to large" — [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1185486); [JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/45277261). Caveat: lead author Stockard worked part-time for NIFDI, a DI advocacy/support body, until 2017 — [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=360). Hattie's 2009 synthesis put direct instruction at d = 0.59 — [visible-learning.org](https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/)
- Explicit instruction in CAI: Hughes and colleagues' analyses of effective computer-assisted instruction found "error correction procedures with elaborated and corrective feedback, teaching in small steps, clear demonstrations" as common design features; Kroesbergen & Van Luit (2003, 50+ studies) found explicit methods "more effective than less direct instructional methods such as discovery learning" for maths difficulties — [Archer & Hughes ch. 1](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)
- 4C/ID: Costa, Miranda & Melo (2022, Learning Environments Research 25, 445–463) meta-analysed programmes built with 4C/ID "in face-to-face and online learning environments" and found "a high impact on performance (d = 0.79 standard deviations), regardless of the academic area, the design of the study and the outcome (knowledge and complex skills)"; effect larger at higher-education level — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10984-021-09373-y); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1338352)
- 5E: Polanin et al. (2024, AERA Open) pooled "61 randomized controlled trial studies, estimating 156 effect sizes; 70% of studies met WWC standards"; "improved science outcomes (g = 0.82, 95% CI [0.67, 0.97]), but a large amount of heterogeneity requires some caution" — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/1cb8d26439d249b4ab45c81a1f080399). An AIR brief on the same programme of work reported science/math/motivation effects of 0.87, 0.70 and 0.24 — [AIR brief Oct 2023](https://air.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/5E-EGM-brief-Oct-2023.pdf). Joswick & Hulings (2024) found "the 5Es are more impactful than traditional instruction ... and the 5Es are not necessarily more effective than other inquiry methods" — [IJSME](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10763-023-10357-y). BSCS's 2006 review cited an earlier meta-analysis of the learning cycle: "the average effect of the learning cycle on conceptual change was about one-quarter of a standard deviation" — [BSCS 2006 exec summary](https://bscs.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/bscs_5e_executive_summary-1.pdf). All of this is K-12 classroom evidence.
- Mastery learning: EEF, +5 months, "limited evidence"; older appendix: five meta-analyses, "none ... conducted in the last 10 years", pooled effects "range from 0.04 to 0.60 so do not provide a consistent estimate" — [EEF technical appendix](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning/technical-appendix); [2019 copy (Summa)](https://mapa.summaedu.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/MasteryLearning_synthesis.pdf). Hattie listed mastery learning at about 0.57 — [visible-learning.org](https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/)
- ARCS: Göksu & Bolat (2021) meta-analysis of "38 controlled experimental studies ... 110 extracted effect sizes ... 8690 students" found "the overall effect of ARCS on achievement was at medium level (ES = 0.74) and the overall effect on motivation was at small level (ES = 0.43)" — [Göksu & Bolat 2021](https://gcris.artuklu.edu.tr/entities/publication/bcdc76ff-9439-4f73-afb2-9934efe40eb6)
- Merrill's First Principles: no meta-analysis found; Merrill's own claim is that the principles are abstracted from other models; one Iranian study tested pebble-in-the-pond on learning and retention — [sanad.iau.ir](https://sanad.iau.ir/en/Article/900132) (not read; weak)
- Hunter: a 1991 Education Week piece reported "one leading researcher said there was no solid evidence for Hunter's ambitious claims"; Hunter herself said there was "no such thing as a 'Madeline Hunter-type' lesson" — [Education Week 1991](https://www.edweek.org/education/madeline/1991/10); Dewey-based critique — [Gibboney, ASCD 1987](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/journals/ed_lead/el_198702_gibboney.pdf)
- Andragogy: "often criticized due to the lack of empirical evidence"; falls "short of scientific standards" (Merriam, Caffarella & Baumgartner 2007; Pratt 1993; Rachal 2002); Brookfield called American andragogy "an unproven theory"; assumptions "posited as self-evident axioms" — [UTA ED492132](https://cdn.prod.web.uta.edu/-/media/project/website/administration/crtle/documents/ed492132.pdf); [Pudelko 2017 review](https://edu1040.teluq.ca/files/2017/03/Pudelko-2017.pdf); [IRMA chapter](https://www.irma-international.org/chapter/strengthening-the-practice-and-research-foundation-of-andragogy-amidst-some-growing-controversy-and-resistance-to-it/266337)
- UbD: no effectiveness studies surfaced in searches; NSW describes it as "a way of thinking purposefully about curriculum planning rather than a rigid program" — [NSW guide](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/planning-a-sequence-of-lessons/backward-design-model)
- Hattie's rankings generally: criticised for "meta-meta-analysis" mixing, an "arbitrary" 0.4 hinge, and statistical errors; Hattie reports inquiry-based teaching at 0.31 and project-based learning at 0.15 — [Didau critique](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/visible-learning-invisible-errors); [visible-learning.org Q&A](https://visible-learning.org/2016/07/ask-professor-john-hattie-a-question/)

**Guidance versus discovery**
- Alfieri, Brooks, Tenenbaum & Aldrich (2011, JEP 103(1), 1–18), 164 studies: explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d = −0.38 for discovery, 580 comparisons); "enhanced discovery" beat other instruction (d = 0.30, 360 comparisons); "unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations do" — [Alfieri et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006, Educational Psychologist 41, 75–86): minimally guided instruction "ignore[s] both the structures that constitute human cognitive architecture and evidence from empirical studies" — [PDF](https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf)
- Productive failure: Sinha & Kapur (2021, RER) pooled 53 studies/166 comparisons of problem-solving-then-instruction vs instruction-then-problem-solving: "Hedge's g 0.36 [95% CI 0.20; 0.51]" favouring PS-I, rising to 0.37–0.58 when fidelity to productive-failure principles was high; instruction-first was as good or better for younger learners and for procedural skills — [Futurity summary](https://futurity.org/?p=2629782); [WEF summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/); DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105

**Ingredient-level evidence relevant to an interactive self-study lesson**
- Retrieval practice: Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, RER 87(3), 659–701): "practice tests are more beneficial for learning than restudying and all other comparison conditions"; feedback amplifies effects; hybrid formats strongest; benefits on transfer as well as retention — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013): practice testing and distributed practice rated "high utility"; interleaving, elaborative interrogation and self-explanation "moderate"; summarising, rereading, highlighting "low"; "practice testing with feedback also consistently outperforms practice testing alone" — [UNB summary of Dunlosky 2013](https://www.unb.ca/fredericton/cetl/_assets/documents/teaching-tips/instructional-methods/effectivelearningtechniquescogedpsychology.pdf); [Questionmark summary](https://www.questionmark.com/only-two-out-of-ten-learning-techniques-have-high-utility/?lang=de)
- Self-explanation: Bisra et al. (2018, Educational Psychology Review), 69 effect sizes from 64 reports, pooled g = 0.55; "self-explanation was more effective than instructor explanation"; benefit not explained by extra time; larger for studying text (0.79) than problem solving (0.48) — [Bisra 2018 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [TUM Clearinghouse table](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/KR_25_ENG_Table.pdf)
- But Barbieri et al. (2023) worked-examples meta-analysis (55 studies, 181 effects, g = 0.48 in maths) found that adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples "yield[ed] a negative effect" and correct-only examples beat incorrect/mixed — [UDel poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf); [Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)
- Spacing: Cepeda et al. (2006, Psych Bulletin 132, 354–380) reviewed "839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments"; the optimal inter-study interval grows with the retention interval — [Cepeda et al. 2006](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)
- Expertise reversal: Reisslein et al. (2006): "Low prior knowledge learners benefited most from traditional example-problem pairs, while their high prior knowledge counterparts benefited most from problem-example pairs" — [Reisslein 2006 PDF](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf); Salden et al. (2010) found "improved learning results from adaptive fading over fixed fading over problem solving" in a tutoring system — [LearnTechLib](https://learntechlib.org/p/106349)
- Completion problems: van Merriënboer (1990) found students taught by completing/extending existing programs did better on construction measures and dropped out less than those designing from scratch — [Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-programming-instruction-in-high-school-program-com/)
- Multimedia (Clark & Mayer): multimedia, contiguity, modality, redundancy, coherence, personalisation principles; segmenting ("chunking the material into parts learners move through at their own pace") and pretraining (name the parts before the procedure); the book itself notes "research on segmenting and pretraining is not as well developed ... we do not yet know how large a segment should be"; the practice chapter's guidelines include "making practice mirror the job", "distributing and mixing practice", "increasing challenge as learners progress" — [O'Reilly excerpt](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/e-learning-and-the/9780787986834/9780787986834_what_we_don_apostrophy_t_know_about_segm.html); [Articulate summary](https://community.articulate.com/articles/evidence-based-guidelines-for-e-learning); [Wiley 4th ed.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119239086)
- Online vs face-to-face: the US Department of Education meta-analysis (Means et al. 2010) found online conditions outperformed face-to-face with an average effect of +0.24 across ~50 contrasts; blended +0.35 (p < .001); purely online smaller and in some versions non-significant; the authors warned blended conditions "often included additional learning time and instructional elements" — [SRI abstract page](https://www.sri.com/publication/education-learning-pubs/digital-learning-pubs/evaluation-of-evidence-based-practices-in-online-learning-a-meta-analysis-and-review-of-online-learning-studies/); [summary](https://jesperbalslev.dk/evaluation-of-evidence-based-practices-in-online-learning/); the 2013 journal version reached the same picture — [Means et al. 2013](https://docs.edtechhub.org/lib/3G5AHTWT)
- Simulations: PhET sims are built through "an iterative design process of student interviews ... and classroom testing" and SERC summarises that "effective use of PhET simulations can lead to improved conceptual learning over traditional lectures, demonstrations, and labs" — [PhET, The Physics Teacher 44(1)](https://spot.colorado.edu/~finkelsn/phet_abs.html); [SERC](https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/library/phet/index.html)
- Predict-Observe-Explain (White & Gunstone 1992): rationale is that "unless students are asked to predict first what will happen, they may not observe carefully"; suits demonstrations with immediate observable results; evidence in searches was practitioner-level — [NZCER](https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7187); [ANU](https://learningandteaching.anu.edu.au/resources/predict-observe-explain-activity)
- Microlearning: Senadheera et al. (2024) pooled only five studies (344 vs 310 students), post-test gap 12.6 (95% CI 1.2–23.9); Monib et al. (2025, 40 studies) say effects on outcomes "remain largely underexplored"; no workplace meta-analysis found — [Senadheera 2024](https://doaj.org/article/9ad595556a674506a6718d3973d7166c); [Monib 2025](https://doaj.org/article/2b4da6d99a2a49608c3fde49b9b3174d)
- EEF's 2021 cognitive-science review (Perry et al.): "while cognitive science approaches can have a meaningful impact on pupil learning, the evidence around how they can be applied successfully in classrooms remains limited"; spaced learning within lessons used ~10-minute blocks separated by an unrelated activity; "some pupils need to have strong foundational knowledge ... prior to making effective comparisons between interleaved tasks" — [EEF news release](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-what-do-we-really-know-about-cognitive-science-in-the-classroom-eef-publishes-new-review-of-the-evidence/); [Eton CIRL summary](https://cirl.etoncollege.com/cognitive-science-a-review-of-the-evidence/)

### Inferences
- The two strands of evidence that apply most directly to a phone-based interactive lesson for an adult are (a) the explicit-instruction/worked-example/retrieval literature (robust, many adult and CAI studies) and (b) the "enhanced discovery"/productive-failure literature, which says a short, bounded attempt at a problem *before* the explanation helps when the learner has some prior knowledge and the task is conceptual rather than procedural. Dan is a practising electrician learning theory, so he fits the "high prior knowledge, conceptual target" case where a predict-first step is supported.
- Framework "brand" evidence (DI, 5E, 4C/ID effect sizes) is almost all from taught classrooms, often children; it is weaker ground for a self-study app than the ingredient-level evidence. A team should justify the template by ingredients (retrieval, spacing, fading worked examples, self-explanation, multimedia principles), not by naming a framework.
- The Barbieri finding (self-explanation prompts hurt when bolted onto worked examples) and Bisra (self-explanation helps overall) are reconcilable if prompts are placed after the learner has made a prediction or attempt, not interleaved into a worked example; the template below uses them that way.

### Gaps
- Stockard et al.'s subject-level effect sizes were not retrieved (only the abstract); a Hattie figure (0.59) and a 2011 RCT-only average (0.66) are reported second-hand.
- No meta-analysis of Merrill's First Principles or of UbD was found; absence of evidence here is itself a finding.
- The EEF live mastery-learning page was unreachable; evidence ratings differ across cached copies (limited vs moderate vs low).
- No study was found that tests POE in a self-study app, nor PhET-style sims with adult tradespeople.

## Key question 3: How should a course be sequenced — prerequisite graph, simple-to-complex, whole-task first, spiral, or thematic?

### Takeaway
No framework or trial shows one sequencing scheme beating the others head-to-head. The defensible synthesis is a hybrid: a prerequisite graph (knowledge components) to decide what must come before what; simple-to-complex *whole* tasks (4C/ID, elaboration theory's epitome) to decide the order within a theme; and spaced, cumulative return to earlier ideas (spiral as spacing) to decide when things are revisited. Gagné-style fine-grained hierarchies have mixed validation; a topic-by-topic "thematic" ordering without a prerequisite check is the least defended.

### Cited Findings
- 4C/ID: whole tasks "organized in easy-to-difficult task classes"; learners progress to the next task class on reaching "prespecified standards"; part-task practice "only starts after the recurrent aspect has been introduced in the context of the whole task"; "learning tasks provide whole-task practice to prevent compartmentalization and fragmentation" — [Ten Steps chapter](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf)
- Elaboration theory: start with an epitome ("the simplest version of a task that still represents the whole task"), elaborate with "progressively harder versions"; "stepwise tasks get a steps-first treatment, reasoning-heavy tasks put principles up front"; learners get some control over which version comes next; the theory "grows out of Ausubel (advance organizers) and Bruner (spiral curriculum)" — [EduTechWiki](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Elaboration_theory); [Cortland](https://web.cortland.edu/frieda/ID/IDtheories/15.html)
- Merrill: a "progression" of problems of rising complexity is the second ripple of pebble-in-the-pond — [EduTechWiki: Pebble in the pond](https://EduTechWiki.unige.ch/en/Pebble_in_the_pond)
- Knowledge space theory (Doignon & Falmagne 1985): a knowledge state is the set of items a learner can solve; prerequisites are "surmise relations" forming a quasi-order; ALEKS is "an instantiation of knowledge space theory" with probabilistic assessment, a learning mode following the structure, and periodic re-assessment (Cosyn, Uzun, Doble & Matayoshi 2021) — [Wikipedia: Knowledge space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_space); [McGraw Hill KST paper page](https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/resources/research/library/math/practical-perspective-on-knowledge-space-theory-aleks-data.html)
- Knowledge components: Koedinger, Corbett & Perfetti's KLI framework (2012, Cognitive Science 36(5), 757–798) links kinds of knowledge, learning processes and instructional methods; Scheines, Silver & Goldin (2014) note "knowing the prerequisite structure among the knowledge components in a domain is crucial for designing instruction and for assessing mastery" and model prerequisites as a causal graph over latent KCs — [KLI (CMU)](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/The_knowledge-learning-instruction_framework_bridging_the_science-practice_chasm_to_enhance_robust_student_learning_/6470522); [Scheines et al. 2014](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2021/week06/HCI%20prereq%20discovery/ScheinesSilverGoldin-EDM2014.pdf)
- Gagné's learning hierarchies: Gagné acknowledged that validity "has been difficult to demonstrate"; a later review says "many studies cast significant doubt on the whole learning hierarchy concept" while a 1974 paper reports "strong evidence in support" — [FJER article](https://journals.flvc.org/fjer/article/download/133788/137741/246379); [AERJ 1974](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312011002121)
- Spiral curriculum: Bruner grounded it "in the structure of separate academic disciplines"; a publisher white paper frames spiralling as "distributed" or "spaced" practice; a controlled sixth-grade physics comparison found no significant aggregate difference between a spiral and a linear curriculum — [SAGE Encyclopedia of Curriculum Studies](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/curriculumstudies/chpt/spiral-curriculum); [Everyday Mathematics spiral paper](https://everydaymath.uchicago.edu/about/research-results/EM_Spiral_20121125.pdf); [Baylor thesis](https://baylor-ir.tdl.org/items/35cb89ea-e2a5-45a7-82bc-bed74533516c/full)
- Whole-task vs part-task: a training review concluded "if the task is simple ... whole-task training is preferred; however, if the task is dangerous or highly complex and can be easily divided into subtasks, part-task training is the better choice", and "trainees must be given an idea of the whole system before presenting the parts" — [DTIC report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA285954.pdf)
- Interleaving: suited to "problem-solving tasks where pupils select strategies"; "the greatest benefit of interleaved practice is experienced by students at the lower end of knowledge of the discipline" and most positive studies are in maths — [EEF review summary](https://cirl.etoncollege.com/cognitive-science-a-review-of-the-evidence/); [Questionmark on Dunlosky](https://www.questionmark.com/only-two-out-of-ten-learning-techniques-have-high-utility/?lang=de)
- Ofsted's 2023 FE business curriculum review argued for "building core knowledge in maths before going onto business education", e.g. teaching percentage change first, then price elasticity; "less effective providers however say mathematical knowledge is 'embedded' in their courses but not taught explicitly" — [FE Week](https://feweek.co.uk/ofsted-publishes-first-fe-curriculum-review)
- Archer & Hughes' scaffolding list: "Sequencing skills so that they build on each other. Selecting examples and problems that progress in complexity" — [Archer & Hughes ch. 1](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)

### Inferences
- For an electrical-theory course the three schemes are complementary, not rivals. Example: knowledge components for "cable sizing" require Ohm's law, power, resistance-per-metre and voltage drop; a prerequisite graph forbids teaching voltage drop before Ohm's law, but says nothing about *which* whole job (e.g., "will this shower circuit be OK on 6 mm²?") to anchor each lesson in. 4C/ID/elaboration theory supplies that anchor and the simple-to-complex order of jobs; spacing/spiral supplies the revisit schedule (which the app already does with spaced review).
- The prerequisite graph is also what allows a course to be *pre-built* yet adaptive: ALEKS-style "ready to learn" (outer-fringe) logic needs only a DAG and a mastery flag per node.
- Thematic ordering alone (e.g., "Part 4 of BS 7671, then Part 5") mirrors how 18th Edition courses are organised because the exam is open-book and tests navigation of the book, not derivation; it is a poor model for teaching science, where prerequisites bite.

### Gaps
- No direct trial comparing prerequisite-graph vs whole-task-first vs spiral sequencing for adult technical learning was found.
- The ALEKS KST paper (McGraw Hill) was not readable; details on how ALEKS validated its structure with data are not recorded here.

## Key question 4: How does trade and adult education teach theory to practitioners, and what can a self-study app borrow?

### Takeaway
UK electrical qualifications specify theory as "understand/explain/determine by calculation" outcomes assessed by closed-book assignments (2365 unit 302) or open-book multiple-choice tests (2382), and the sector's self-study products are bite-sized modules with mini-assessments, practice exams and 12-month access. The vocational-pedagogy literature (Lucas, Spencer & Claxton 2012; Casey et al. 2006; Gatsby/Huddersfield) and inspection evidence say theory sticks when it is recontextualised into the job, when learners see why it matters at work, and when it is taught explicitly rather than assumed to be "embedded"; small trials of contextualised maths are positive on engagement but not reliably on pass rates.

- Engineering-technology students "continuously applied incorrect 'water-analogy' to the concepts of current flow" across all cohorts and "perceive electricity ontologically incorrect as a 'Substance-that-can-be-used-up'" — [Goris 2016, i-JEP](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jep/article/view/5146)
- Pre-service teachers could not "distinguish between the definition of resistance and Ohm's law describing constant resistance", showed "an inability to consider a circuit as a system", and "when the context was changed understanding was often lost" — [UNISA thesis record](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/13504?show=full)
- Québec electrical-technology students (17–20) treated every circuit as linear and unchanging — [IJSE 1996 abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0950069960180205)
- Taber: students "commonly think that resistance in a circuit has local effects" and "when an analogy is used in teaching there is also the potential for it to mislead" — [Taber, science-education-research.com](https://science-education-research.com/?p=1474)
- How People Learn: "Students come to the classroom with preconceptions about how the world works. If their initial understanding is not engaged, they may fail to grasp the new concepts ... or revert to their preconceptions outside the classroom"; competence requires "a deep foundation of factual knowledge ... in the context of a conceptual framework ... organize[d] ... in ways that facilitate retrieval and application"; metacognition — [NAP, How Students Learn ch. 1](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/11100/chapter/2)

**Practitioner ID sources (labelled opinion)**
- Cathy Moore's action mapping (2008) starts from "a single measurable business goal", then "the observable actions people must take", then "practice activities that let people rehearse those actions", then "the minimum information required to complete the practice"; actions "should be things that employees will do"; practice should "echo the tasks people face on the job" — [Devlin Peck summary](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/action-mapping-for-instructional-designers)
- Julie Dirksen, Design for How People Learn (2nd ed., New Riders 2016): retrieval over rereading, chunking, anchoring new material to existing mental models or realistic scenarios, curiosity/manageable challenge, measurable objectives shared upfront, analogies and stories — [LaSalle library record](https://elib.lasalle.edu.sg/cgi-bin/spydus.exe/FULL/WPAC/BIBENQ/120525521/90189,993) (content summaries in search came from low-quality mirrors; treat as practitioner advice)

### Inferences
- The qualification handbooks give a ready-made outcome grammar and weighting for an electrician's theory course: outcomes phrased as "explain the relationship between X and Y", "determine by calculation and measurement", "calculate", "specify methods of", each with a Range statement listing the cases that count. A pre-built course that mirrors this structure will feel legitimate to a UK electrician and can map onto what he has already passed.
- The sector's own self-study products (IET, EC4U, The Guilds) converge on 4–5 minute micro-modules grouped by book Part with mini-assessments and practice exams, but they teach *regulations navigation*, not understanding; they are evidence of what the market accepts on a phone, not of learning effectiveness.
- Vocational pedagogy says the theory a practitioner retains is theory that is "drawn out" of practice and reconnected to the job (Lucas et al.'s "coaching which draws out theory"; Gatsby's "recontextualisation"; Ofsted's "see why maths matters for their jobs"). For an app, that means every lesson should open with a job situation the learner recognises and close by returning to it; the explanation sits inside the job, not before it.
- The contextualisation trials warn that *context alone* does not raise pass rates; the explicit teaching of the underlying idea must still happen (Ofsted's "embedded but not taught explicitly" failure mode). A template should therefore pair the job hook with an explicit, named rule and a worked example.
- Misconception research argues for a predict-then-see step aimed at the specific wrong models tradespeople carry (current "used up", resistance acting locally, the water analogy over-extended), and against leaning on the water analogy as the primary explanation.

### Gaps
- The ETF "Effective Practices in Post-16 Vocational Maths" (Dec 2014) PDF could not be fetched (connect error); its key findings are not recorded.
- Ofsted has not (as far as searches show) published an FE curriculum review on maths or on electrical installation; the closest is the 2023 business review.
- No published research on how 2365/5357 tutors sequence Ohm's law and circuit calculations in class was found; the only material is qualification specifications and provider marketing.
- Lucas et al.'s full tables of method-to-outcome mapping were only partially excerpted.

## Key question 5: What does a good set of learning outcomes and checks look like for a short self-study module, per these frameworks?

### Takeaway
All frameworks that address outcomes converge: state 1–3 observable outcomes per short module using a Bloom/SOLO verb at the level you will actually assess, tell the learner why it matters (Gagné event 2, Archer-Hughes element 5, Knowles' "need to know", ARCS relevance), and write the check *before* the content (UbD stage 2, Biggs' constructive alignment, Moore's action mapping). Checks should be retrieval-based with feedback, mix formats, include transfer to a changed context, and gate progression at roughly 80% success.

### Cited Findings
- UbD: decide "acceptable evidence" before planning activities; include "performance tasks" in a "new and authentic situation" plus "other evidence"; six facets as the assessment lens — [NSW guide](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/planning-a-sequence-of-lessons/backward-design-model)
- Biggs: "The verbs should reflect different levels of understanding, with the topic content being the objects the verbs take ... The same verbs need to be embedded in the teaching/learning activities, and in the assessment tasks" — [Biggs, Constructive Alignment](https://www.biologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:94dbcc23-1c4d-4892-bab8-ba16abc4ef66/ConstructiveAlignment_Biggs.pdf)
- ABCD objectives (Heinich, Molenda, Russell & Smaldino): Audience, Behaviour, Condition, Degree; "the behavior in your objective should lead directly to some form of assessment" — [University of Memphis UM3D](https://blogs.memphis.edu/um3d/2020/09/15/abcd-model-for-writing-effective-learning-objectives)
- Revised Bloom verbs: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create — [Liverpool verb guide](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/media/livacuk/centre-for-innovation-in-education/staff-guides/learning-outcomes-with-blooms-verb-guide/learning-outcomes-with-blooms-verb-guide.pdf)
- 4C/ID step 3 "Set performance objectives" sits inside the learning-task component; standards per task class gate progression — [Ten Steps chapter](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf)
- Gagné: "Provide objectives before instruction begins ... Describe required performance ... Describe criteria for standard performance"; assessment should "test students in ways that allow them to demonstrate their comprehension and application ... (as opposed to simply memorization and recall)"; use pre/post tests and "continually incorporate questions from previous tests" — [NIU CITL](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml)
- Archer & Hughes: "Tell learners clearly what is to be learned and why it is important"; "Provide an adequate range of examples and non-examples ... so that students do not underuse it ... [or] use the skill inappropriately"; "Require frequent responses"; "cumulative practice ... address[es] both previously and newly acquired skills" — [Archer & Hughes ch. 1](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)
- Rosenshine: check understanding "at each point", aim for ~80% success in guided practice, "weekly and monthly review" — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Mastery learning: ~80% threshold before progression; support for those who miss it — [EEF technical appendix](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning/technical-appendix)
- Retrieval practice: hybrid formats (multiple-choice plus cued recall) "most effective"; feedback amplifies; transfer benefits found — [Adesope et al. 2017](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842)
- Dunlosky: practice testing "with feedback ... consistently outperforms practice testing alone"; elaborative interrogation and self-explanation work best "if the student, not the instructor, generates the explanation" — [UNB summary](https://www.unb.ca/fredericton/cetl/_assets/documents/teaching-tips/instructional-methods/effectivelearningtechniquescogedpsychology.pdf)
- How People Learn: assess preconceptions ("if their initial understanding is not engaged"), and build metacognition by having students "set goals ... and check their progress" — [NAP ch. 1](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/11100/chapter/2)
- UK qualification grammar: C&G outcomes use "explain the relationship", "determine by calculation and measurement", "specify", "calculate", "identify", "interpret", each with Range statements and percentage weightings — [2365-03 handbook](https://www.cityandguilds.com/-/media/productdocuments/building_services_engineering/electrical_installation/2365/2365_level_3/centre_documents/2365-03_l3_diploma_qualification_handbook_v1-13-pdf.pdf); [2382-22 handbook](https://www.cityandguilds.com/-/media/productdocuments/building_services_engineering/electrical_installation/2382/level_3/centre_documents/2382-22-requirements-for-electrical-installations-bs7671-2018-2022-v1-2-pdf.pdf)
- Action mapping: write the on-the-job action first, then the practice that rehearses it, then only the information needed — [Devlin Peck](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/action-mapping-for-instructional-designers)
- Misconception checks: students lose understanding "when the context was changed" — [UNISA](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/13504?show=full)

### Inferences
- A good 10–20 minute module should carry: one headline outcome written as an on-the-job action with a Bloom verb at Apply or above ("Given a 230 V circuit and a measured resistance, determine the current and say whether a 6 A breaker will hold"), one or two supporting "explain/identify" outcomes, and a "why it matters on the job" sentence. Checks: (1) a predict-before-you-see question targeting a known misconception; (2) a worked example then a completion problem; (3) two or three retrieval items in mixed formats (numeric entry, multiple choice with plausible misconception distractors, short "say it back"); (4) one transfer item in a changed context (different circuit, different units); (5) a mastery gate of about 80% with a route back to the explanation; (6) the items feed spaced review.
- Because the C&G assessments are closed-book calculation assignments (2365) and open-book regulation lookup (2382), a course for a UK electrician should include both item types: calculation with a stated rule, and "find and interpret the regulation" tasks.

### Gaps
- No framework gives an evidence-based number of items per 10–20 minute module; the "80%" threshold comes from classroom mastery/Rosenshine research, not from self-study apps.
- Ofqual's general conditions on outcome/assessment-criteria wording were not retrieved; the grammar above is inferred from the C&G handbooks.

## Key question 6: What concrete lesson template would a careful instructional designer propose for a 10–20 minute interactive, phone-first lesson in a pre-built course, and how would they justify each part?

### Takeaway
A defensible template is an eight-part cycle that takes the common core of all the frameworks, orders the middle in the "bounded attempt first, then explicit explanation" shape supported for knowledgeable adults, and leans on the ingredient-level evidence for every step: Job hook and why-it-matters → outcome in one line → recall/activate → predict (targeting a misconception) → play with a bounded interactive and observe → explicit explanation with a named rule and one worked example → faded practice with feedback → say it back and a transfer check → re-anchor to the job and schedule review. Each part below is tied to the frameworks and evidence that justify it.

### Cited Findings (justifications per part)
- Part 1, job hook and "why this matters on the job" (about 1 minute): Gagné events 1–2; Archer & Hughes element 5 ("why it is important"); ARCS attention via "inquiry arousal" (a problem posed) and relevance via "goal orientation"; Knowles' "need to know"; Lucas et al.'s authenticity requirement ("a real requirement for learning methods to be more authentic"); Ofsted's finding that learners must "see why the maths mattered" — [NIU](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml); [Archer & Hughes](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1); [EBSCO ARCS](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/kellers-arcs-model); [infed Knowles](https://infed.org/dir/welcome/thinkers/et-knowl.htm); [Lucas et al. 2012](https://tte-lisa.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/vle_content/LessonResource/pedagogy/How-to-teach-vocational-education.pdf); [Boston College Ofsted](https://www.boston.ac.uk/app/uploads/2018/05/Boston_College_s_2017_Ofsted_Inspection.pdf)
- Part 2, the outcome in one line (seconds): Gagné event 2; UbD stage 1; Biggs alignment; Dirksen's "communicated upfront" — [NSW UbD](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/planning-a-sequence-of-lessons/backward-design-model); [Biggs](https://www.biologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:94dbcc23-1c4d-4892-bab8-ba16abc4ef66/ConstructiveAlignment_Biggs.pdf)
- Part 3, recall/activate (1–2 minutes, one or two retrieval items from earlier lessons): Rosenshine principle 1 ("daily review"); Gagné event 3; Merrill activation ("recall prior knowledge ... or ... a structure for organizing new knowledge"); Archer & Hughes element 6 and the "five review items as a warm-up"; retrieval-practice evidence — [Rosenshine](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf); [EduTechWiki Merrill](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/First_principles_of_instruction); [Adesope 2017](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842)
- Part 4, predict (30–60 seconds, one forced choice or slider aimed at a known wrong model): POE ("unless students are asked to predict first ... they may not observe carefully"); How People Learn on engaging preconceptions; productive failure's problem-before-instruction effect (g = 0.36, larger with fidelity; weaker for procedures and young children, so keep it conceptual and short); 5E "engage" — [NZCER POE](https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7187); [NAP](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/11100/chapter/2); [Sinha & Kapur summary](https://futurity.org/?p=2629782); [Polanin 2024](https://doaj.org/article/1cb8d26439d249b4ab45c81a1f080399)
- Part 5, play and observe (2–4 minutes with a bounded interactive whose controls map to the variables in the rule): 5E "explore"; Merrill demonstration via "simulated scenarios"; Alfieri's "enhanced discovery" with feedback and scaffolding (d = 0.30) and the rejection of unassisted discovery (d = −0.38); PhET's design evidence; Lucas et al. on simulation "relating classroom and workplace together"; Mayer's coherence principle (no decorative detail) and segmenting — [Alfieri 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf); [PhET](https://spot.colorado.edu/~finkelsn/phet_abs.html); [Lucas et al.](https://tte-lisa.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/vle_content/LessonResource/pedagogy/How-to-teach-vocational-education.pdf); [Articulate on Mayer](https://community.articulate.com/articles/evidence-based-guidelines-for-e-learning)
- Part 6, explicit explanation with the named rule, one worked example and a non-example (3–5 minutes): Rosenshine 2 and 4 ("small steps", "models"); Archer & Hughes 7–9 ("step-by-step demonstrations", "clear and concise language", "examples and non-examples"); 5E "explain"; 4C/ID supportive information "presented before learners start ... and kept available"; worked-example effect (g = 0.48) with correct examples; Kirschner et al. on guidance; Ofsted's warning against "embedded but not taught explicitly"; the "explanation of a shown rule" also satisfies the product's faithfulness rule — [Rosenshine](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf); [Archer & Hughes](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1); [Ten Steps](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf); [Barbieri 2023 poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf); [FE Week on Ofsted](https://feweek.co.uk/ofsted-publishes-first-fe-curriculum-review)
- Part 7, faded practice with feedback (3–5 minutes: a completion problem, then an independent problem, then a varied one): Rosenshine 5–9 (guided practice, ~80% success, scaffolds, independent practice); Archer & Hughes 10–11, 16; Merrill application with "diminishing coaching"; 4C/ID "diminishing support in each task class" and "high variability of practice"; completion-problem evidence; expertise reversal (fade faster for a knowledgeable adult); feedback types from Gagné (prefer remedial/descriptive over bare evaluative); mastery gate — [Rosenshine](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf); [Ten Steps](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf); [van Merriënboer 1990](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-programming-instruction-in-high-school-program-com/); [Reisslein 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf); [NIU feedback types](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml); [EEF mastery](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/mastery-learning/technical-appendix)
- Part 8, say it back and a transfer check (1–2 minutes): self-explanation (g = 0.55; learner-generated, placed after the attempt rather than inside the worked example per Barbieri); Gagné event 9 ("convert information learned in one format into another"); UbD "transfer" and the "explain" facet; a changed-context item because understanding "was often lost" when context changed — [Bisra 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [Barbieri](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [NIU](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml); [UNISA](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/13504?show=full)
- Part 9, re-anchor and schedule (under a minute): Merrill integration; Gagné 9 ("avoid isolating course content"); Rosenshine 10 ("weekly and monthly review"); Archer & Hughes "distributed and cumulative practice"; spacing evidence (Cepeda; Dunlosky "high utility"); the app's spaced review consumes the items answered — [Cepeda 2006](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html); [UNB Dunlosky](https://www.unb.ca/fredericton/cetl/_assets/documents/teaching-tips/instructional-methods/effectivelearningtechniquescogedpsychology.pdf)
- Length and chunking: EEF-reviewed within-lesson spacing used ~10-minute blocks; Clark & Mayer segmenting ("parts learners move through at their own pace") with the caveat that "we do not yet know how large a segment should be"; sector norm of 4–5 minute micro-modules with mini-assessments (IET, The Guilds) — [Eton CIRL summary](https://cirl.etoncollege.com/cognitive-science-a-review-of-the-evidence/); [O'Reilly Clark & Mayer](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/e-learning-and-the/9780787986834/9780787986834_what_we_don_apostrophy_t_know_about_segm.html); [IET course sheet](https://www.theiet.org/media/2782/18th-edition-academy-course-sheet.pdf)
- Course-level wrapper (how the template sits in a course): pebble-in-the-pond progression of whole problems; 4C/ID task classes with standards; prerequisite graph for "ready to learn"; epitome lesson first per theme; cumulative review items drawn from earlier lessons — [EduTechWiki pebble](https://EduTechWiki.unige.ch/en/Pebble_in_the_pond); [Ten Steps](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf); [Wikipedia KST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_space); [EduTechWiki elaboration](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Elaboration_theory)

### Inferences
- Where the template departs from Rosenshine/Archer-Hughes (predict and play before the explanation) it does so only for a bounded, feedback-rich interactive on a conceptual point with a learner who has prior trade knowledge; the moment the lesson target is procedural (e.g., the steps of a cable-sizing calculation), the designer should collapse Parts 4–5 to a single prediction and go straight to the worked example, consistent with Sinha & Kapur's procedural-skill caveat and Kirschner et al.
- The worked example in Part 6 should be a *trade* example with real numbers computed from the shown rule (consistent with the product's "numbers are controls, computed from a shown rule, or cited constants"), and the non-example should be the misconception from Part 4 shown failing.
- Every check item should be written so it can be reused as a spaced-review item; this is where the template links lesson design to the course's "learning that sticks" rule, and it is the single most evidence-backed element in the whole design (practice testing with feedback plus spacing).
- Justification language for the team: cite ingredients (retrieval, spacing, worked examples with fading, self-explanation, multimedia principles, enhanced discovery) with their meta-analyses; cite frameworks (Gagné, Merrill, 4C/ID, Rosenshine, 5E, UbD) as the *shape* that organises them; label ARCS, andragogy, Hunter and action mapping as design heuristics with thin or no outcome evidence.

### Gaps
- No study directly tests a nine-part micro-lesson of this shape with adult tradespeople on a phone; the justification is by assembly of ingredient-level evidence, not by a trial of the template.
- Time allocations per part are design judgement; no source gives minute-level prescriptions for self-study.
- The Means (2010) moderators on learner control and reflection prompts were not read in the primary report; only the headline effect sizes are recorded.
