# Explain first, practise lightly, review for months

The ready-made courses on My University's shelves should be built as **levelled courses of short, explain-first lessons, each closing with two or three low-stakes practice items whose answers feed the app's existing spaced review, with a mixed, closed-book Level Review after every four to eight lessons**. That shape borrows Brilliant's skeleton (courses of 4–9 numbered Levels, each 3–8 one-idea lessons capped by a Level Review; lessons of 10–25 small commit-then-reveal steps; wrong answers explained, never punished) and repairs Brilliant's two best-documented failures, forgetting and the absence of any cumulative record, with the two techniques the learning-science literature rates highest, retrieval practice and spacing, which the app already has as FSRS cards and the dossier. Inside a lesson, the evidence for an adult with trade knowledge supports one short, ungraded prediction, then an explicit explanation with a named rule and one fully worked example, then a goal-wrapped interactive, then faded practice with immediate explanatory feedback, then a say-it-back and recap: the order Dan asked for on 7 October ("an overall explanation, a specific concept, reinforce it with an interactive, final bits to solidify it, quick recap"), with the guessing and checking kept light rather than dropped. The organic flow cannot deliver this: it writes a whole lesson in one roughly 25-second model call from thin research, hard-codes five stages with exactly one predict, one interactive and 2–3 checks, and scored **3.13/5** on its last eval panel with the same seven faults in nearly every lesson; its read mode fixes the feel but discards review entirely. A course written over hours, research first and accuracy-checked, can afford a prerequisite graph, consistent vocabulary, cumulative reviews and interactives that draw the cause, and it fits today's storage if each Brilliant-style Level is stored as one 5–8-idea topic and a shelf course becomes a folder of Levels. The single point where Dan's recorded preference (read mode by default) and the evidence diverge is whether checks exist at all; the recommendation is to keep them, skippable and guilt-free, because every product and study reviewed treats a delayed, mixed, closed-book check as the only meaningful proof that something was learned.

## Brilliant's lesson is a chain of 10–25 small commitments, and its courses are Levels capped by reviews

Brilliant describes itself as a maker of "games for learning" with "no videos, everything is interactive", built to help learners "develop intuition through interaction, build understanding through experimentation" ([Brilliant About](https://brilliant.org/about)). Its 2026 repositioning as "your personal tutor for math and coding", with the Koji tutor layered on, did not change the course library underneath ([Brilliant FAQ](https://brilliant.org/faq/)). No official step-by-step specification of a lesson is public, and Brilliant's "Behind the scenes" design pages were unreachable, so the anatomy below is assembled from Brilliant's own descriptions and several reviewers who walked through lessons; it is consistent across them.

A lesson is a linear run of roughly **10–25 steps in a 15–30 minute lesson**; each step opens with "a brief concept introduction, usually 2–4 sentences with an accompanying illustration or animation", then immediately asks a question using that concept, with a hint available that "nudges you toward the right reasoning without giving away the answer" ([Skillscouter review](https://skillscouter.com/brilliant-review-math-science-coding/)). "Explanations appear after each problem, reinforcing 'why' an answer works, not just 'what' the answer is", and "each problem builds naturally on the previous one" ([Educational App Store](https://www.educationalappstore.com/app/brilliant-solve-learn-grow)). A wrong answer carries no sound, no lives and no penalty: the step "just shows the correct answer and explains why it is correct", so "wrong answers are treated as part of the learning process rather than a mistake" ([Organized Homeschooler](https://www.organizedhomeschooler.com/brilliant-review/)), often "backing it up with another example" ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/brilliant-worldwide-brilliant/reviews)). Brilliant's own design statement frames the lesson as a layered reveal: "A Brilliant lesson is an opportunity for a student to own a discovery, no matter how small... It unfolds in layers as they are invited to manipulate its contents... Mistakes aren't dead-ends — they're opportunities" ([About Brilliant lessons](https://brilliantorg.notion.site/About-Brilliant-lessons-f90ec481d92c4f0fb28c5f5d346db90d)). Interactions are sliders, drags, named choices, numeric entry and, in the Python courses, executable code; critics note that "the interactivity is limited to slider and quiz-based activities" ([Brighterly](https://brighterly.com/blog/is-brilliant-org-worth-it/)).

The course grammar is uniform. Circuits has **31 lessons and 334 exercises** in seven-plus Levels, Level 1 being Current Loops, Short Circuits, Choosing Bulbs, Splitting Current and a Level Review ([Circuits](https://brilliant.org/courses/circuits/)); Thinking in Code has **48 lessons and 588 exercises in 9 Levels**, each ending in a Level Review ([Thinking in Code](https://brilliant.org/courses/thinking-in-code/)); Probability in Data has 19 lessons and 144 exercises and ends with a "Case Study" Level ([Probability in Data](https://brilliant.org/courses/probability-fundamentals/)); How AI Works has 31 lessons but only 94 exercises ([How AI Works](https://brilliant.org/courses/how-llms-work/)). The rule of thumb that falls out: one lesson is one manipulable sub-idea with a two-to-four-word title that names the idea; one Level is 3–8 lessons on one concept, capped by a review; a course is 4–9 Levels, with application levels ("Applications in Automation", "Case Study") placed after the mechanics. Placement is by "a lightweight math diagnostic", and a learner who jumps ahead must "pass the level check that gates the later material" ([Course placement guide](https://brilliant.org/help/parents-and-families/course-placement-guide/)). Class Central's catalogue puts most of its 111 Brilliant courses at 2–5 hours or 10+ hours ([Class Central](https://www.classcentral.com/provider/brilliant)). Exercise density differs sharply: about 11–12 exercises per lesson in Circuits, Thinking in Code and Thinking in Python (70 lessons, 874 exercises, "61 of them ask learners to diagnose or repair a bug") versus 3 per lesson in How AI Works ([Interactive Python for teens](https://brilliant.org/help/why-brilliant/interactive-python-for-teens)), which is where reviewers' "thinner, less rigorous" complaints about the newer conceptual courses concentrate.

Brilliant's core design rule is to withhold the answer until the learner has committed. Sue Khim's image is a climbing wall: "A good tutor should not be an express train to the answer... We are giving you holds. We are giving you a route... but you still have to climb", and the team deliberately refused the engagement-boosting option of telling a student the answer when asked ([Edtech Insiders podcast](https://edtechinsiders.buzzsprout.com/1877869/episodes/19457440-building-an-ai-tutor-that-gets-students-to-think-with-sue-khim-of-brilliant)). Koji extends the rule to free-form help: it "guides you through the thinking step by step, without ever just giving you the answer", and its hints are questions such as "Which of these operations is the next step?" ([How does Koji work?](https://brilliant.org/help/features/how-does-koji-work/)). The switch from "think" to "tell" is step-local, not lesson-local: every step is commit → feedback → explanation, so the learner is never more than one answer away from being told why, which is why reviewers call the same product both "problem-first" and "hand-holding". The design time behind it is substantial: "We spend weeks designing the core game for each topic, and even more time building the levels, aiming for that perfect difficulty curve", and once a level is right "we always want to make a few more practice sets just like it... a different tool makes variations on the puzzles while keeping the same learning objective" ([Hand-crafted, machine-made](https://blog.brilliant.org/hand-crafted-machine-made/)). Learning designers are hired to "decompose complex ideas into approachable, well-sequenced steps that empower learners to reason from first principles" ([Math Learning Designer posting](https://jobs.lever.co/brilliant/b0b97281-179b-4b47-b5d6-a0cfaad3f425)), and producers hold AI-generated practice to bars for "clarity, difficulty ramp, voice, and bingeability... not walls of text" ([Producer posting](https://jobs.lever.co/brilliant/a4bca51a-eff6-42c6-9ac9-65571153e69e)). Correctness is treated as existential: "For learning games, 'almost right' is completely wrong", so every generated problem passes evals and "multiple rounds of human review" ([Evals for AI learning games](https://blog.brilliant.org/when-almost-right-is-catastrophically-wrong-evals-for-ai-learning-games/)).

What Brilliant does for retention is "more reps in the same game": per-lesson practice sets, the Level Review, redoable lessons, and habit mechanics (a streak needs "3 problems or a full lesson in a single day" ([Streak help](https://brilliant.org/help/using-brilliant/what-is-a-streak)); weekly leagues of 30 ([Leagues help](https://brilliant.org/help/features/what-are-leagues-and-leaderboards))). There is no evidence of a scheduled spaced-review system and no notebook. Learners say so: "it lacks the ability to easily review past information... the courses need to have an ongoing summary review of information you've already covered, like a student would in a notebook" ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/brilliant-learn-math-coding/id913335252)); "I could not retrieve what I learned in any practical situation – only within the app... Without review rounds or regular repetition, retaining the knowledge can be pure luck" ([Nibble review](https://nibble-app.com/de/blog/lohnt-sich-brilliant)); Brilliant "just kinda straps you in and takes you for a ride... they could add notes to copy down like definitions and theorems in the margins that accumulate as you progress" ([r/math](https://www.reddit.com/r/math/comments/1bcopio/brilliantorg_is_actually_awesome/)). Maths educators add that "the exercises are too easy. So it doesn't represent real practice" ([r/matheducation](https://www.reddit.com/r/matheducation/comments/1oauodc/what_do_you_think_about_brilliantorg/)). Brilliant's only quantified learning claim, "6x more effective than lecture videos", traces to Koedinger's 2015 self-selected MOOC comparison (final averages **57% vs 66%**) and overstates it ([Chronicle](https://www.chronicle.com/blogs/wiredcampus/in-online-courses-students-learn-more-by-doing-than-by-watching)); no independent efficacy study of Brilliant exists.

The praise and the criticism are two sides of one design. Step-level scaffolding and low-stakes checks make lessons feel doable and pleasant, and the same choices make exercises feel easy and the learning hard to transfer. For the shelves, the borrowable core is the course grammar (Levels, one-idea lessons with idea-naming titles, Level Reviews, application levels last), the step loop (commit before reveal, explanation of every wrong answer with a second example, hints that narrow the route rather than state the concept), practice variants on one objective, and the correctness bar. What to leave behind is equally clear: the missing cumulative record, the missing review schedule, streaks and leagues, and a "% of people who got this right" indicator that Shute's feedback guidelines explicitly warn against. The app already has the two things Brilliant's users most ask for: a dossier that accumulates each idea's explanation, plate and glossary, and FSRS review of what the learner answered.

## Every serious product converges on lesson → unit → mixed delayed check, and none has a say-it-back

Across Khan Academy, Math Academy, Duolingo, the Open University, Coursera, Codecademy, Execute Program and the explorable-explanation tradition, the same four-level shape appears, course → unit → lesson → item, but what a "lesson" is differs enormously, and the differences are instructive.

| Product | Lesson atom | First move | What counts as mastered | Review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brilliant | 10–25 commit-then-reveal steps, about 15 min | Puzzle or manipulable, then a question | Level Review; practice sets | None scheduled; redo and habit mechanics |
| Math Academy | 3–4 knowledge points: worked example + 2–5 problems | Read a worked example, then try | Timed, closed-book quiz every 150 XP | Reviews are queued tasks; repetitions flow to prerequisites |
| Khan Academy | Videos or articles + one exercise ("3 of 4") | Watch, then practise | Mixed-skill unit test, which can demote | Mastery Challenge after a wait |
| Duolingo | About 15 exercises, guidebook beside the unit | Do, with hints | "Legendary" lesson with no hints | Mistakes replayed at lesson end; half-life regression |
| Execute Program | Paragraph + live code example, alternating | Read, then run | Pass the prerequisite reviews | Automatic review next day, then widening gaps |
| Coursera | About 30 min of 6–9 min videos + quiz | Watch, with an in-video question | Module quiz | None |
| Open University | Multi-hour unit of text with numbered activities | Read | Assignment, self-assessment test | Study calendar and tutor |

Math Academy's atom is the clearest statement of the explain-then-practise pattern: "Each lesson starts out with an introduction, and then moves to a worked example, followed by 2-5 practice questions on the same type of problem as the worked example", with "about 3-4 'knowledge points' of increasing difficulty" per lesson and a net ratio of "about 7 active practice problems per passive worked example" ([The Math Academy Way](https://www.justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)). Progress within a point is two correct in a row, "Every question has an explanation", and a quiz "is made available to students every 150 XP... timed and students may not refer back to examples", with missed topics "immediately assigned as review" ([Math Academy: How It Works](https://www.mathacademy.com/how-it-works)). Its unit of work, "One XP represents one minute of fully-focused, fully-productive work", is the only pacing device in the survey that is also a learning device, because it turns "how long will this take" into a number the learner can set per day ([The Math Academy Way](https://www.justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf)). Khan defines mastery the same way from the other side: a skill is "Mastered" only after a learner "correctly answered questions on this skill on a mixed-skill assessment", and levels fall when such questions are missed ([Khan mastery levels](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/5548760867853--How-do-Khan-Academy-s-Mastery-levels-work)). Duolingo's "Legendary" lesson removes hints ([Duolingo: ways to practice](https://blog.duolingo.com/ways-to-practice-in-duolingo)). The shared definition is "mixed, closed-book, after a delay": a single-skill exercise answered right after the explanation never counts as mastered anywhere.

The products with the strongest internal logic make review scheduled work, not an optional tab. Execute Program: "When you complete an EP lesson, you automatically receive a short review the next day", and reviews "require you to answer questions correctly before moving forward" ([Execute Program: Why EP?](https://www.executeprogram.com/why-ep)); Duolingo reviews mistakes at the end of the lesson "to take advantage of a little spaced repetition" and schedules practice "right before you're about to forget" using a trained forgetting model ([Dear Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/spaced-repetition-for-learning); [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf)). Duolingo's own research found that "lessons mattered more than time spent learning" ([Duolingo efficacy studies](http://duolingo.com/efficacy/studies)), undercutting attendance metrics; MOOCs, which stop at quizzes and have no review or habit layer, completed **3.13%** of enrolments in 2017–18 and 46% among paying verified learners ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/node/7744)), and the OU's supported model still loses about **45%** of a module's starters, clustering at assignments ([Simpson, EADTU slides](https://www.slideshare.net/EADTU/student-dropout-in-distance-education-how-many-who-when-what-are-the-consequences-for-them-why-they-dropout-and-how-do-we-reduce-dropout)). The OU is also the one institution that audits how much of a unit is passive reading: its learning-design framework classifies every activity as assimilative, communicative, finding information, productive or practice ([OU Activity Types Framework](https://www.open.ac.uk/blogs/learning-design/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/OU-LD-Activity-Types-Framework-October-2021-FINAL.pdf)), and "the results often show a skew towards Assimilative activities" ([UCL on OULDI](https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/digital-education/2013/03/05/golden-ouldi)). That audit is a cheap discipline for a course author: count the minutes of reading against the minutes of doing in each Level.

On what comes first, the products split. "Show first" (Khan, Coursera, Math Academy, Execute Program) places a small dose of instruction immediately before the matching problem; "try first" belongs to discovery traditions whose claims rest on anecdote, such as Art of Problem Solving, where "each section starts with problems, giving the student a chance to solve them without help before proceeding" ([AoPS](https://artofproblemsolving.com/store/book/intro-algebra)), and the Moore method. Nicky Case's pattern catalogue names the cheapest bridge: "Place Your Bets!", for "topics with a clear right or wrong answer", asks the reader to guess or sketch before the reveal, while "Puzzle It Out" suits topics that "lend themselves to simulation" ([Case: 4 More Design Patterns](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations-4-more-design-patterns/)); he also warns "Don't try to explain everything with something interactive... Text: Best at describing very abstract concepts... Interactives: Best at showing processes, systems, models" ([Case 2014](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations/)). Bret Victor's layering is the design that best fits "nothing ever gets in the way" on a phone: an explorable "works as a static explanation — it can be read like normal text. The reader is not forced to interact in order to learn", and "most interactive widgets dump the user in a sandbox and say 'figure it out for yourself'. Those are not explanations" ([Victor](http://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/)). Amit Patel's touch rules are the only guidance found that was written for fingers: "I've moved sliders and buttons below the diagram so that when you're interacting with it, your finger/hand don't cover up the diagram"; "Be careful about how touch-drag interacts with scrolling"; "It's often better to show all the variants than to ask the reader to interact with the diagram to see the variants" ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/making-of/little-things/)).

Two findings from this survey matter most for the shelves. First, bolted-on AI tutoring adds little unless the product makes the learner use it at the moment of a mistake: in the two-year Khanmigo RCT, gains were **about 0.06–0.08 SD per year, resembling Khan practice without AI**, and students messaged the tutor "in only 17 percent of the exercise sessions in which they made a mistake" ([NBER w35620](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620)). Second, none of the surveyed mastery engines asks the learner to explain anything back; Michael Pershan's complaint about Math Academy, "I kept answering questions correctly, and MA kept pushing me onward, without a chance to deepen my understanding" ([Pershan](https://pershmail.substack.com/p/math-academy-wants-to-supercharge)), names the gap that the app's say-it-back step fills. Bloom's comparison, which Math Academy's book opens with, put tutoring at 2.0 SD against 1.0 each for feedback-corrective mastery, cues and explanations, and time on task ([Bloom's 2 sigma problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_sigma_problem)); VanLehn's later review found human tutoring at d = 0.79 and intelligent tutoring systems at 0.76, not 2.0 and 1.0 ([VanLehn 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)). A pre-built course can copy the three 1.0-sized ingredients; the "defend your reasoning" step of a tutorial is what say-it-back, and an Ask Claude sheet that refuses to hand over answers, approximate.

## The science backs a bounded attempt, explicit telling, faded practice and spaced retrieval

The evidence sorts into a frame, a lesson order, a telling style and a retention recipe, and it rules out several popular moves.

The frame is guidance. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argued from cognitive architecture that minimally guided instruction is less effective, and that "the advantage of guidance begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge to provide 'internal' guidance" ([Kirschner et al. 2006](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ736299)); Alfieri's 164-study meta-analysis found **explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d = −0.38 for discovery)** while enhanced discovery with "feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations" beat other instruction (d = 0.30) ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2010-23599-001)). Both camps agree on a bounded, feedback-rich attempt; neither supports floundering.

The lesson order depends on prior knowledge, and two of the most relevant studies used electrical content. Kalyuga's trade apprentices "benefited most from the worked examples condition" when inexperienced, while "with more experience in the domain, worked examples became redundant and problem solving proved superior" ([Kalyuga et al. 2001](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/)); Reisslein's computer-based circuit-analysis study found low-prior-knowledge learners did best with example-then-problem pairs and high-prior-knowledge learners with problem-then-example ([Reisslein et al. 2006](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)). Worked examples carry a medium effect in maths (**g = 0.48** across 55 studies), correct-only examples beat mixed sets, and attaching self-explanation prompts to the example itself reduced the effect ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)); fading (full example → incomplete examples → problem) beat example-problem pairs, and far transfer appeared only when fading was combined with prompts to name the principle behind each step ([Renkl & Atkinson](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196)). Problem-solving-then-instruction has its own meta-analysis, **g = 0.36** across 53 studies, rising to 0.37–0.58 with faithful designs but reversing for young learners and roughly tying for procedural skills ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)); it works "only if specific design features, namely contrasting cases or building instruction on student solutions, are implemented" ([Loibl et al. 2017](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/towards-a-theory-of-when-and-how-problem-solving-followed-by-inst/)). The cheapest and best-supported "attempt" is a prediction: students who predicted a demonstration's outcome understood significantly more, while passive observers "understood the underlying concepts no better than students who never saw the demonstration" ([Crouch et al. 2004](https://mazur.harvard.edu/presentations/classroom-demonstrations-learning-tools-or-entertainment)); pretesting improves later learning "despite many erroneous responses" as long as the answer follows ([Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5); [Kornell et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265)), and predicting works by boosting "surprise about unexpected answers, which leads to enhanced attention to the correct answer" ([Brod 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33768503)). Mayer's pre-training principle (name the parts before the mechanism) carries d = 0.46 and segmenting into learner-paced chunks d = 0.70 ([Mayer 2017](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning)); Rey's 56-study meta-analysis confirms segmenting helps retention and transfer, more so for learners with higher prior knowledge ([Rey et al. 2019](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)). Explaining back is well supported: self-explanation prompts give g = 0.55 across 64 reports, strongest when they ask the learner to explain a concept rather than to reflect on how they are doing ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)); teaching after preparing to teach gives g = 0.56 ([Kobayashi 2019](https://metatoc.com/papers/112234-learning-by-preparing-to-teach-and-teaching-a-meta-analysis)). The Barbieri and Bisra findings reconcile if the explanation prompt comes after the learner's own attempt, not inside the worked example.

The telling should be explicit, immediate for hard new material, and about the task. Kulik and Kulik's 53-study review found that "applied studies using actual classroom quizzes and real learning materials have usually found immediate feedback to be more effective than delayed" ([Kulik & Kulik 1988](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543058001079)); Shute's guidelines say to focus feedback on the task not the learner, give it in manageable elaborated units, never give normative comparisons, use immediate feedback "for difficult tasks" and "for retention of procedural or conceptual knowledge", and note that for novices "hints may not be as helpful as more explicit, directive feedback" ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Errors followed by corrective feedback help, most when the learner was confident in the error, and the feedback "should include analysis of the reasoning leading up to the mistake" ([Metcalfe 2017](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf)). Rosenshine's synthesis of master-teacher research puts the optimal success rate in guided practice at **about 80%** and places hands-on work "always after, not before, the basic material was learned" ([Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)).

Retention weeks later rests on three structures. Retrieval practice: across 118 articles practice tests beat restudy with **g = 0.51** (g = 0.61 pooled over all comparisons), mixed formats scored highest (g = 0.80) and effects held in classrooms as in labs ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)); classroom quizzing across 222 studies gave g of about 0.50 ([Yang et al. 2021](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)); and practice testing and distributed practice are the only two techniques Dunlosky's review rates high utility, with summarising, highlighting and rereading rated low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade-2)). Interpolating short tests into a 21-minute online lecture roughly halved mind-wandering and improved retention ([Szpunar et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)). Spacing: in a 1,350-person study with tests up to a year later, the optimal gap was about 20–40% of a one-week retention interval and 5–10% of a one-year one ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)); successive relearning, relearning to criterion in several spaced sessions, is the strongest single recipe, and a single week erased much of one session's gain even after every item had been recalled ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)). Interleaving: the same problems in interleaved rather than blocked order gave **61% vs 38%** on an unannounced test a month later in a 787-student cluster RCT ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)); across 59 studies interleaving gave g = 0.42, but nothing for expository texts and a reversal for word lists, so it belongs in problem sets, not definitions ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). Mastery gating has modest support but a documented cost: "self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes" ([Kulik et al. 1990](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265)), which argues for recommending a revisit rather than blocking.

Interactives teach when they are wrapped, not when they are sandboxes. Simulations beat non-simulation instruction at g+ = 0.62, and the same simulation with added scaffolding and feedback beat the unmodified one at **g+ = 0.49**, with more sessions giving larger gains ([D'Angelo et al. 2014](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/simulations-for-stem-learning-brief.pdf)); guidance in inquiry improved learning outcomes d = 0.50 ([Lazonder & Harmsen 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.3102/0034654315627366)). But heavy scripting kills exploration: with light, moderate and heavy worksheets around a PhET simulation, exploratory interaction fell from **85% to 68% to 9%** ([Chamberlain et al. 2014](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2014/rp/c4rp00009a)), and PhET's interview work concludes that "minimal but nonzero guidance with many of these simulations creates the optimum engaged exploration" ([Adams et al. 2008](https://www.per-central.org/perc/2008/detail.cfm?ID=726)). In ICAP terms, moving a slider without inferring is only "active"; predicting and explaining are "constructive", and learning rises with that level ([Chi & Wylie 2014](https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-wylie-r-2014-icap-framework-linking-cognitive-engagement-active-learning)). The guidance belongs around the interactive, as a goal, a prediction and a reflection, not inside it as a script.

The evidence also rules things out. Learning-style matching has no demonstrated utility ([Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)); interesting-but-irrelevant extras cost g = −0.33 ([Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020)); the "10–15 minute attention span" has no primary support ([Bradbury 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)), though median engagement with MOOC videos tops out around six minutes ([Guo et al. 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)); screens cost expository comprehension mainly under time pressure, so reading should never be timed ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)). Gamification's effects are small and unstable (motivational g = 0.36, with only the cognitive estimate holding in rigorous studies) ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)); a semester of compulsory badges and a leaderboard lowered motivation, satisfaction and exam scores ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-017-0042-5/tables/13)); and across 223,209 participants, identified regulation (personal value) predicted persistence while external rewards did not ([Howard et al. 2021](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8w8z7/student-motivation-and-associated-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-from-self-determination-theory)). The app's "no guilt mechanics" rule is the evidence-aligned position, and retrieval with feedback itself "can increase motivation to keep on studying" ([Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-022-00089-1)).

### Frameworks agree on the skeleton; trade education adds the job hook and the outcome grammar

Gagné's nine events, Merrill's First Principles, 4C/ID, Rosenshine, Hunter, Archer and Hughes, 5E and UbD prescribe the same four-block skeleton: orient and connect to prior knowledge, meet the new idea with guidance, do something with it and get feedback, consolidate toward transfer ([NIU on Gagné](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml); [EduTechWiki on Merrill](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/First_principles_of_instruction); [Archer & Hughes ch. 1](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/archer_ch1.pdf?t=1)). They disagree only on whether explanation or problem comes first in the middle and on grain size. Framework-level evidence is almost all from taught classrooms, often of children: 4C/ID programmes d = 0.79 ([Costa et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10984-021-09373-y)), 5E g = 0.82 across 61 RCTs in school science ([Polanin et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/1cb8d26439d249b4ab45c81a1f080399)), Direct Instruction positive across 328 studies with a conflict-of-interest caveat on the lead author ([Stockard et al. 2018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1185486)); Merrill, UbD, Hunter and andragogy have design logic but no outcome meta-analyses. A course author should justify the template by ingredients (retrieval, spacing, worked examples with fading, self-explanation, multimedia principles, enhanced discovery) and use the frameworks as the shape that organises them.

For sequencing a course, the frameworks are complementary rather than rival. 4C/ID orders whole tasks "in easy-to-difficult task classes" with "diminishing support in each task class" and "high variability of practice", and only starts drilling a part after it has appeared "in the context of the whole task" ([Ten Steps](https://web.mit.edu/xtalks/TenStepsToComplexLearning-Kirschner-VanMerrienboer.pdf)); elaboration theory opens with an epitome, "the simplest version of a task that still represents the whole task", then "progressively harder versions" ([Elaboration theory](https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Elaboration_theory)); knowledge-space theory and knowledge-component models supply the prerequisite graph that says what cannot come before what, and are what let a pre-built course be adaptive with nothing more than a DAG and a mastery flag per node ([Knowledge space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_space); [Scheines et al. 2014](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/nynke/726-2021/week06/HCI%20prereq%20discovery/ScheinesSilverGoldin-EDM2014.pdf)); spacing supplies the revisit schedule. Ofsted's first FE curriculum review found less effective providers claiming mathematical knowledge was "embedded" in their courses "but not taught explicitly" ([FE Week](https://feweek.co.uk/ofsted-publishes-first-fe-curriculum-review)), and a 2026 DfE RCT of contextualised adult numeracy found "no statistically significant evidence... that it improved pass rates" ([IES](https://www.employment-studies.co.uk/resource/adult-numeracy-randomised-controlled-trials-contextualised-approach/)): a job hook motivates, but the named rule and worked example still have to be taught.

Dan is a practising electrician, which makes two more things usable. The UK qualification grammar gives a ready-made outcome language: City & Guilds 2365 unit 302 assesses "explain the relationship between resistance, inductance, capacitance and impedance", "determine electrical quantities in alternating current circuits" and "calculate power factor", with Range statements listing the cases that count ([C&G 2365-03 handbook](https://www.cityandguilds.com/-/media/productdocuments/building_services_engineering/electrical_installation/2365/2365_level_3/centre_documents/2365-03_l3_diploma_qualification_handbook_v1-13-pdf.pdf)); Biggs's alignment rule, that "the same verbs need to be embedded in the teaching/learning activities, and in the assessment tasks" ([Biggs](https://www.biologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:94dbcc23-1c4d-4892-bab8-ba16abc4ef66/ConstructiveAlignment_Biggs.pdf)), turns each lesson's one-line outcome into its checks. The sector's own self-study products are "bite-sized learning modules, typically lasting 4–5 minutes" with knowledge checks and 12-month access ([The Guilds](https://www.theguilds.co.uk/18th-edition-electrical-regulations-2382)), and IET Academy's 20-hour course is "sub-divided into separate modules, with mini-assessments between them" ([IET Academy](https://www.theiet.org/media/2782/18th-edition-academy-course-sheet.pdf)): evidence of what the trade accepts on a phone, not of learning. Vocational pedagogy says theory sticks when it is drawn out of practice and reconnected to the job, through "coaching which draws out theory" and simulation that relates "classroom and workplace together" ([Lucas, Spencer & Claxton 2012](https://tte-lisa.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/vle_content/LessonResource/pedagogy/How-to-teach-vocational-education.pdf)), so electrical systems are an unusually strong base for analogies into other domains. The misconception literature adds a caution in the other direction: engineering students "continuously applied incorrect 'water-analogy' to the concepts of current flow" from freshman to senior year ([Goris 2016](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jep/article/view/5146)), and a teaching analogy can be "a teaching model meant to support learning, which actually misleads the learner" ([Taber](https://science-education-research.com/?p=1474)); every analogy needs its non-matching features named, which is what the app's "Where it breaks" field is for.

## The organic flow writes a lesson in 25 seconds; the shelves can afford what it cannot

The organic flow is built for a topic Dan types in the moment. The plan prompt asks for "5-8 ideas in teaching order", each "ONE thing he can understand in five minutes, ideally by manipulating something" ([app/src/js/30-prompts.js:193](app/src/js/30-prompts.js#L193)); research runs in the background with a budget of "at most 8 searches and 4 fetches in all" for the whole course ([app/src/js/30-prompts.js:270](app/src/js/30-prompts.js#L270)); each lesson is then written in one model call, repaired once, and fact-checked and built side by side, with measured latencies of "plan 3 s, research 45 s, write-lesson 25 s, build/repair 35 s" ([docs/review/performance.md:30](docs/review/performance.md#L30); [docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:916](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L916)). The study screen hard-codes five stages, Predict, Play, Explain, Say it back, Check ([app/src/js/50-lesson.js:39](app/src/js/50-lesson.js#L39)), and the validator enforces exactly one predict, one interactive with 1–2 controls, one say-it-back and 2–3 checks as hard counts ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:506](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L506)). The read mode Dan asked for on 7 October runs two stages, Read then Explore, because "meeting the picture first, its sentences assumed an idea he had not read yet" ([app/src/js/50-lesson.js:43](app/src/js/50-lesson.js#L43)), and it makes no cards: "This one was just for reading, so nothing comes back in review" ([app/src/js/50-lesson.js:1456](app/src/js/50-lesson.js#L1456); [docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:385](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L385)).

The eval record shows what one 25-second pass from thin research produces. The run-2 three-judge panel scored eight lessons **3.13/5 overall (predict 2.96, interactive 3.00, checks 3.17, explain 3.54, say-it-back 3.79, accuracy 3.63)** ([docs/eval/run2-panel.md:5](docs/eval/run2-panel.md#L5)) and found seven causes shared across most lessons: "The predict answers itself" (6 of 8), "The checks test recall" (all 8), "The interactives show only the effect" (7 of 8), "Absolute claims are false" (all 8), "The explanation skips the causal step" (7 of 8), "Research is too thin for the lesson" (7 of 8) and "Labels are hard to read on a phone" (all 8) ([docs/eval/run2-panel.md:23](docs/eval/run2-panel.md#L23)). The panel's warning about review is the sharpest: "These cards become Dan's spaced-review deck, so recall-only cards waste months of review" ([docs/eval/run2-panel.md:80](docs/eval/run2-panel.md#L80)), and over-broad claims "become review cards Dan sees for months, with the caveat left behind in an ignores panel the cards never show" ([docs/eval/run2-panel.md:124](docs/eval/run2-panel.md#L124)). Dan's own feedback from his first real lessons adds two more faults: "120 words is tiny if it needs a decent explanation" and the picture arriving before the idea it assumes ([docs/NEXT.md](docs/NEXT.md)). The three lowest-scoring parts (predict, interactive, checks) are exactly the parts a Brilliant-style design would rebuild; the two highest (explanation, say-it-back) are worth keeping as they are. Nearly every fault is a symptom of writing a whole lesson in one call with no second look, which is why the write-lesson rules have grown to about 3,000 words ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:825](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L825)): the organic flow is trying to prevent by instruction what a slow build prevents by process.

What the platform fixes is narrower than it looks. Review cards are made only from what Dan answered: "each check he answered becomes a card of the same type; his say-it-back becomes" a recall card ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:624](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L624); [app/src/js/60-today.js:288](app/src/js/60-today.js#L288)), scheduled by FSRS-4.5 at day granularity with a 15-card daily cap, interleaved so "one idea never comes twice in a row and topics alternate" ([app/src/js/40-fsrs.js:1](app/src/js/40-fsrs.js#L1); [docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:1137](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L1137)). So dropping checks drops review for that idea, and any new practice item that should feed review must be one of the five card types (choice, order, estimate, target, recall) with a stable id. The verifier's frozen fields show what is load-bearing once Dan has started: the predict question and options, the interactive spec, check ids and answers, and sources ([app/src/js/34-verify.js:60](app/src/js/34-verify.js#L60)). The kit can pose a situation, let him move it, hide a readout in quiz mode and let the host grade one numeric outcome, but it has no text or number entry, no sequence of graded problems inside one frame and no in-frame right/wrong feedback ([app/kit/KIT.md:3](app/kit/KIT.md#L3)), so a Brilliant-style run of several small problems has to live in the host's card renderer, not the iframe. Storage fits without schema change: one topic doc plus one lesson doc per idea, each under 256 KiB with interactives under 150 KB ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:328](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L328)), and the shelves already hold **100 courses across four shelves, all with tid: null**, each showing "Not built yet" until a build session writes a tid ([app/src/js/76-shelves.js:1](app/src/js/76-shelves.js#L1); [docs/shelves-maths.json](docs/shelves-maths.json)). The Saturday plan is to build them in "mode 'read' unless he says otherwise" ([docs/NEXT.md:51](docs/NEXT.md#L51)), and Dan paused it with the note that "the off-the-shelf courses need not follow the organic lesson flow" ([docs/NEXT.md:57](docs/NEXT.md#L57)).

Two structural mismatches follow. A Brilliant course is 19–70 lessons; the app's plan is capped at 5–8 ideas and the topic page, dossier and lesson writer all read that shape. Rather than lift the cap, the clean mapping is one Brilliant Level = one app topic of 5–8 ideas, and one shelf course = a folder of Levels, each with its own tid, which keeps every existing screen and the dossier-per-topic working and only asks the shelf record to hold several tids per course. And read mode, chosen because its order is right, is the wrong vehicle because it is also the mode with no retrieval; the evidence in the previous section says that is the single most costly thing to drop, and Brilliant's users say the same in plain words.

## Recommended shape: levelled courses of explain-first lessons with light practice and cumulative review

At course level, mirror Brilliant's grammar and add what it lacks. A shelf course is 3–6 Levels; a Level is 4–8 lessons, each one manipulable sub-idea with a title that names the idea, in prerequisite order with `deps` made explicit, opening with an epitome (the simplest whole version of the course's hook question) and placing application or case-study lessons last within the Level. Every Level ends in a Level Review: 6–10 mixed-format items drawn from the Level's lessons plus one or two from earlier Levels, interleaved, closed-book, available the day after the last lesson rather than immediately, with every item written to stand alone weeks later so it can become a review card. The existing two-question calibration warm-up serves as a light placement that "never locks anything"; mastery is soft, a "worth another look" recommendation rather than a gate. Each Level's finished ideas bind into the dossier, so the course accumulates the notebook Brilliant's learners ask for. Course size then follows Brilliant's arithmetic: a five-Level course of six lessons at roughly 10–15 minutes each is 5–8 hours, in the same band Class Central reports.

At lesson level, the recommended order is the explain-first track with the lightest viable retrieval. It is chosen because Dan has partial knowledge in some shelves (electrical science, everyday maths) and none in others (number theory, group theory), and the evidence says to branch on prior knowledge and default to guidance; the attempt step is the only part that should flip for an idea he marks "Know it well".

| Part | Organic study flow today | Pre-built course | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orient | Topic hook on the topic page; the idea's one line hidden during Predict | One line stating the outcome and, where natural, the job or everyday situation it answers; terms and parts named before the mechanism | Gagné events 1–3; pre-training d = 0.46; Ofsted "see why it matters" |
| Attempt | Predict what the interactive will do (often leaks the answer; 2.96/5) | One ungraded "place your bets" question aimed at a known misconception, skippable; dropped for procedural ideas | Crouch 2004; Pan & Carpenter 2023; Sinha & Kapur procedural caveat |
| Explain | After Play: 2–4 paragraphs with analogy and practice | Before Play, as long as needed: the idea in one sentence, the named rule, one correct fully worked example with numbers computed from the rule, the misconception shown failing, the trade analogy with "where it breaks" | Dan, 7 Oct; Barbieri g = 0.48; Alfieri; Taber |
| See it work | One interactive, 1–2 controls, "Try this" lede, "What am I looking at" fold | Same interactive, wrapped: a stated goal, a predict-before-you-move line, the cause drawn not just the effect, a "What happens" reflection after; variants shown side by side when interaction adds nothing | D'Angelo g+ = 0.49 for wrapping; Chamberlain 9% under heavy scripting; Patel |
| Practise | 2–3 "checks" presented as a test; first answer counts | 2–4 items presented as practice: a completion step, a full problem, a changed-context case; every wrong answer explained with a second example, retry allowed, no penalty; the first answer still feeds review | Math Academy knowledge point; Brilliant feedback; Renkl fading; Adesope mixed formats; Shute immediate feedback |
| Say it back | Open "why/how" question graded against a rubric (3.79/5) | Keep unchanged; optional only in courses Dan marks read-only | Bisra g = 0.55; Kobayashi g = 0.56; Pershan's gap |
| Recap | "In short" (read mode) or the Done card | "In short" plus "Put it into practice" plus the next idea; the lesson's items scheduled for the next day | Successive relearning; Cepeda's gap ratios |
| Level Review | None | After every 4–8 lessons: mixed, cumulative, closed-book, next-day | Khan and Math Academy mastery; Rohrer 61% vs 38%; Rawson & Dunlosky |

Several details carry the evidence. The attempt is one question, never a flounder: a prediction with an expected surprise, not graded, and the explanation that follows refers back to the common wrong answer, which is what makes problem-first designs work and what the organic predict never did because the writer could not see Dan's answer. The worked example must be correct-only and clean, with the self-explanation prompt placed after practice, not inside the example. Practice items are written as retrieval items with misconception distractors and immediate, explanatory, task-focused feedback, aiming for roughly 80% success; they double as review cards, so each is true "of the real world as well as the model" and after every later lesson, the rule the app already imposes. The interactive's guidance sits around it, as goal, prediction and reflection, and the same interactive returns in later reviews as a target card rather than being used once. The analogy runs from Dan's strong knowledge outward (electricity → the new domain) with its breaks named, never from water to electricity. Nothing in the lesson is timed, every segment boundary is a natural stop, and progress is self-referenced (ideas learned, cards strong) with no streaks, leagues or "% got this right".

The pre-built process is where the biggest gains are, independent of structure. Research first and per Level, not per course with a 12-call budget; vocabulary fixed once for the whole course so a new learner does not "end up carrying two vocabularies" ([docs/eval/run2-panel.md:195](docs/eval/run2-panel.md#L195)); the experts' 51-line accuracy list used as a checklist; the predict and practice items written after the explanation and tested against it for leakage, using the "four consecutive words" and "right option 1.5x longer" judgements the validator already makes ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:576](docs/ARCHITECTURE.md#L576)); and a Brilliant-style correctness bar, where one wrong answer key costs more than a missing explanation, enforced by the existing verify-lesson pass and interactive self-test before anything is saved as ready.

What this implies for the codebase is deliberately small, and it is flagged here rather than specified. A teach-first lesson with light practice is the existing study-mode schema in a different screen order (Explain before Play, the predict optional, checks labelled as practice with retry), so it needs a third stage list rather than a new lesson shape; the alternative of building the shelves in read mode is a one-line choice that forfeits review. A Level Review can be a card session scoped to a Level's ideas on the Today tab rather than a new lesson type, since the scheduler already interleaves and caps. The shelf record needs to hold several tids per course. Multi-problem practice should stay in the host's five card types; adding text entry inside the iframe is not needed. The remaining gap is a mechanism for a Claude Code build session to write finished topics and lessons into the live db as ready, which the repo plans but does not contain ([docs/NEXT.md:51](docs/NEXT.md#L51)).

Where the evidence is thin, the report says so. No study tests this whole lesson template with adult tradespeople on a phone; the ordering is assembled from pairwise comparisons, most with undergraduates over short retention intervals, and the framework effect sizes are from classrooms of children. Brilliant's internal rules for Level length and for when a lesson may open with an explanation are not public. No evidence fixes the number of practice items per lesson or the minutes per part; those are design judgements, and the Brilliant and Math Academy ratios (7–12 exercises per lesson; 7 problems per worked example) are the only observed anchors. And whether Dan wants checks in the shelves at all is not recorded anywhere in the repository; the recommendation to keep them light rather than absent is this report's judgement from the evidence, not his stated wish.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the choice Dan posed. "Need not follow the organic flow" was read as permission to drop predict, say-it-back and checks; the evidence says the thing to drop is the organic process (one call, thin research, no second look) and the thing to keep, in a lighter and friendlier form, is the retrieval and spacing that the organic flow got right and that every mature product has converged on. The shelves' real advantage over both Brilliant and the organic flow is time: time to research first, to fix vocabulary across a course, to write practice that is still true weeks later, to draw causes rather than effects, and to run an accuracy pass. Paired with the two assets the app already has and Brilliant's users most miss, a dossier that accumulates and a scheduler that returns, a levelled, explain-first course with light practice and cumulative Level Reviews is better placed than Brilliant's own product to make learning stick, which is the product rule the shelves were in danger of quietly dropping.

The second implication is organisational. Treating a Brilliant Level as an app topic makes a 100-course shelf a few hundred topics of 5–8 ideas, buildable folder by folder with the screens that exist, and it makes the first built course the experiment the eval record lacks: a read of its Level Review results and FSRS bands after a month will say more about the template than any further prompt tuning, and it is the cheapest way to settle the one open question, whether light checks earn their place in a course Dan chose to be taught.
