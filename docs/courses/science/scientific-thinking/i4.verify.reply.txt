Kinetic energy may be lost, but the momentum total is not.\n\nA ball's momentum is its mass × its velocity, in kg m/s. Velocity has a direction, so momentum has one too. Pick one way as positive, and a ball going the other way counts as negative. Now think about the hit itself. Each ball pushes on the other. By Newton's third law the two pushes are equal and opposite. They also last exactly as long, since each ball touches the other only while the other touches it. So whatever momentum one ball gains, the other loses. The total cannot change unless something outside pushes, such as a cushion or a hand. A set of things with no outside push is a [[closed system]].[^1] In one, total momentum before a hit equals total momentum after.[^2] On the pool table, each ball's arrow changes in the hit, but the total arrow beside the table does not.\n\nTry it with numbers. A 0.4 kg ball at 3.0 m/s hits a still 0.2 kg ball, and they stick. Before, the momentum is 0.4 × 3.0 = 1.2 kg m/s. After, the joined 0.6 kg carries the same 1.2 kg m/s, so it moves at 1.2 ÷ 0.6 = 2.0 m/s. Now the kinetic energy, ½mv². Before, ½ × 0.4 × 3.0² = 1.8 J. After, ½ × 0.6 × 2.0² = 1.2 J. So 0.6 J has gone from the movement. Here is why energy can go when momentum cannot. In the hit, some movement energy goes into squashing the balls. A perfectly springy ball would give it all back as it springs out. These balls stick, so part of it stays as heat and sound. The pushes still give equal and opposite momentum, so the momentum total stays.[^3]\n\nA hit that keeps all the kinetic energy is an [[elastic collision]]. One that loses some is an [[inelastic collision]]. Textbooks add that a sticking hit loses the most kinetic energy. Neither quantity need stay the same ball by ball. With no outside push, the momentum total is kept for the whole set in every hit, and the kinetic energy total only in an elastic collision. On a glancing hit, split each momentum into a part along the first line and a part across it. The pushes are equal and opposite, so their parts in any direction are too. So each direction keeps its own total. The ideal bouncy hit is a teaching model.
