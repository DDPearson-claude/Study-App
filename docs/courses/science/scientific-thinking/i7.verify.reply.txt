   "now": "Light bends where it crosses into water or glass because it changes speed there. Snell's law, which links the angles on each side, says by how much.\n\nLight is slower in water than in air.[^2] One way to picture it: its front is a row of marchers, arm in arm. Meet the water at a slant, and one end crosses first and slows first. The other end still moves at the faster speed, so the row swings round towards the normal. So a ray entering a slower material bends towards the normal, and a ray leaving it bends away. Square on, both ends slow together, so the ray goes straight on, just more slowly. The [[refractive index]], n, measures the slowing. It is light's speed in empty space divided by its speed in the material.[^3] Light in empty space travels at 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. Fresh water's index is 1.333,[^1] so light crosses water at 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.333 = 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s. You know this kind of ratio from cable fault location. A cable's velocity factor is a pulse's speed in it divided by the speed in empty space, so it is the same kind of ratio turned over.\n\nSnell's law gives the angles: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r. Here i is the angle of incidence and r the angle of refraction, both measured from the normal. Coming in from air, with n₁ taken as 1, the index is simply sin i ÷ sin r.[^4] From air into water at 40°, sin r = sin 40° ÷ 1.333 = 0.643 ÷ 1.333 = 0.482, so r = 28.8°. Going the other way, from water out into air, the angle outside is the larger one. At the [[critical angle]] it reaches 90°, and the bent ray lies along the surface.[^5] Put sin 90° = 1 into the law, and sin c = n₂ ÷ n₁. For water into air, sin c = 1 ÷ 1.333 = 0.750, so c = 48.6°. Past that angle, sin r = 1.333 × sin i would be above 1, and no angle has a sine above 1. So no light leaves, and all of it reflects back inside. That is [[total internal reflection]]. It cannot happen going into a slower material, because there sin r is always smaller than sin i.\n\nThe same rule explains the coin that looks too high. Its light bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, and your eye traces it straight back, to a point above the coin. Looking nearly straight down, the angles are small, and a small angle's sine and tangent are almost equal. Light can run either way along a ray, so name the angles as for light coming in: i in the air, r in the water. A ray leaves the surface a small sideways step from straight above the coin. Going down to the coin, that step is the real depth × tan r. Traced back from the eye, it is the apparent depth × tan i. So the apparent depth is the real depth × tan r ÷ tan i, almost exactly the real depth × sin r ÷ sin i, which is the real depth ÷ n. So a tank 1.2 m deep looks 1.2 ÷ 1.333 = 0.90 m deep. Textbooks add that glass's index is a little higher for violet light than for red, so a prism's sloping faces bend violet most and fan white light into colours. Rays, flat surfaces and one index for each material make this a teaching model, and real indexes also shift a little with temperature. Like the winch, the boundary is a puzzle you can test on the picture and then work on paper from one rule. **Light bends at the edge of water or glass because it changes speed there, and n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r says by how much.**[^4]"
