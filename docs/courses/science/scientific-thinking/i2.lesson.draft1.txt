    "text": "A [[compound gear train]] runs gear pairs one after another, with the driven gear of one pair on the same shaft as the driver of the next. Those two gears turn together. So the train's overall ratio is the ratios of its pairs multiplied together.\n\nStart with one pair. A motor turns a small gear, called a [[pinion]], with 12 teeth. It drives a wheel with 36 teeth. Speed × teeth is the same on both, so the wheel turns at a third of the motor's speed. That is a ratio of 3 : 1, three turns in for one turn out. Each meshed pair like this is one [[stage]] of the train. Its ratio is the driven gear's teeth ÷ the driver's teeth.\n\nNow fix a second 12-tooth pinion to the wheel's shaft. Two gears fixed to one shaft must turn at the same speed.[^1] So the second pinion turns at a third of the motor's speed, even though it has the same 12 teeth as the motor's pinion. It drives a second 36-tooth wheel, which divides that speed by 3 again. Nine turns of the motor make three turns of the middle shaft and one turn of the output. So the overall ratio is 3 × 3 = 9, written 9 : 1. Adding the ratios would give 6 : 1. That is wrong, because the second stage divides the speed the first stage leaves, not the motor's speed. To find any compound train's ratio, work out each stage's ratio and multiply them together.[^2] Each stage is one mesh, so each one flips the direction. The two gears on a shaft turn the same way.\n\nThat is where a winch's pull comes from, and what it costs. Textbooks add that an ideal train keeps the power the same, and power is torque × turning speed. So a stage that divides the speed by 3 multiplies the torque by 3. A 5 kN pull on a drum of 0.2 m radius needs 5000 × 0.2 = 1000 N m. Through an ideal 50 : 1 train, the motor needs only 1000 ÷ 50 = 20 N m. It pays in speed, turning 50 times for each turn of the drum. This ideal train is a model: real gears lose some power to friction at every mesh, so a real motor needs a little more torque. **Two gears fixed to one shaft turn together.[^1] So a compound train's overall ratio is its stage ratios multiplied.[^2]**"

A real box gives a little less, because friction takes some at each mesh.\n- Direction: each mesh flips it. Gears on one shaft turn the same way.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** gears fixed to one shaft share one speed. A train that cuts the speed by some ratio raises the torque by nearly the same ratio.\n\n**Worked example:** a motor turns a 14-tooth pinion at 960 rev/min. It drives a 42-tooth wheel. A 15-tooth pinion on that shaft drives a 75-tooth wheel. Stage ratios: 42 ÷ 14 = 3 and 75 ÷ 15 = 5. Overall ratio: 3 × 5 = 15. Output speed: 960 ÷ 15 = 64 rev/min. Two meshes, so the output turns the motor's way. A motor giving 10 N m gives at most 10 × 15 = 150 N m at the output.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Adding the stage ratios. In the example, 3 + 5 = 8 would give 120 rev/min, nearly twice the real speed.\n- Treating the two gears on a shaft as meshed. They turn together, the same way and at the same speed.\n- Expecting two pinions with the same teeth to turn at the same speed. Where each sits in the train decides its speed.\n- Expecting a train to raise both speed and torque. It trades one for the other.\n- Leaving a pull in kN. Turn it into newtons before you work out the torque in N m."
