   "now": "Let a damped spring or an RLC loop go and it does one of three things. It creeps back, it returns as fast as it can without ringing, or it rings while its swings die. Two numbers decide which: the roots of m r² + c r + k = 0.\n\nIn decay, e^(rt) solved a first-order rule, because its rate is r times itself. Try the same guess here. Then x′ = r e^(rt) and x″ = r² e^(rt). Put them in and every term carries e^(rt): (m r² + c r + k) e^(rt) = 0. An exponential is never zero, so m r² + c r + k = 0. This quadratic is the [[characteristic equation]]. Each root is a rate, per second. The quadratic formula gives r = (−c ± √(c² − 4mk)) ÷ 2m. The sign of c² − 4mk sorts the three cases.[^1]\n\nWhen c² is bigger than 4mk there are two real roots, both negative. The answer is two decays added together. Take the 1 kg mass and 4 N/m spring with damping 5. The roots are −1 and −4. Let go from 0.5 m at rest, x = 0.667e^(−t) − 0.167e^(−4t). The fast part has almost gone by 1 s, so the slow root sets the creep. This is [[overdamped]]. The two roots always multiply to k ÷ m, which is 4 here. So past critical, more damping pushes the fast root away from zero and pulls the slow one in. Heavier damping creeps back slower. At damping 6 the roots are −0.76 and −5.24, and the mass is still 0.13 m out at 2 s, against 0.09 m at damping 5.\n\nWhen c² equals 4mk, here at damping 4, there is one root, −2, twice. But e^(−2t) alone has one constant, and the start needs two values, position and speed. The second answer is t e^(−2t), as you can check by putting it back in. From 0.5 m at rest, x = (0.5 + t)e^(−2t). With the product fixed at 4, two equal roots put the slower one as far from zero as a real root can go. So of all the settings that do not ring, critical damping settles fastest.[^2] A little less damping crosses rest sooner, but it overshoots.\n\nWhen c² is smaller than 4mk, the square root is of a negative number. The roots are a mirror pair, α ± jβ, with α = −c ÷ 2m and β = √(4mk − c²) ÷ 2m. At damping 2 they are −1 ± j1.732. Now e^((α + jβ)t) is e^(αt) × e^(jβt). The second factor is an arrow of length 1 turning at β radians a second. The first shrinks it. The mirror root's arrow turns the other way, so the two add to a real answer: the arrow's shadow, a swing inside a dying envelope e^(αt). This is [[underdamped]]. The real part sets how fast it dies, and the j part how fast it rings. At damping 2 the envelope keeps 0.368 of itself each second. With no damping the roots sit at ±j2, and in this ideal model it rings for ever.\n\nThe RLC loop has the same three cases, with L, R and 1 ÷ C in place of m, c and k.[^3] This is a simplified model with ideal parts. Its drag is in step with the speed, which is why each swing keeps the same fraction of the last. A drag of fixed size, such as dry friction, is not in this equation, and its swings do not shrink that way.\n\n**The roots of m r² + c r + k = 0 set the motion: two real roots creep back, one repeated root returns quickest without ringing, and a complex pair α ± jβ rings at β inside a dying envelope e^(αt).**"
