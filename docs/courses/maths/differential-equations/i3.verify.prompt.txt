  "text": "Switch a coil onto a supply and its current climbs to a steady value, E ÷ R. On the way it is that steady value plus a gap. The gap dies away just as a discharge does.\n\nTake a 24 V supply, a 0.5 H coil and 10 Ω in series. Round the loop the voltages add up: E = L di/dt + R i. The resistor takes R i, and the coil takes the rest. At switch-on the current is 0, so all 24 V sits across the coil. Then di/dt = 24 ÷ 0.5 = 48 A a second. As the current grows, the resistor takes more and leaves the coil less. So the rise slows. It stops when the coil's share reaches zero. Set di/dt = 0 and 24 = 10 i, so i = 2.4 A. That is the steady state, E ÷ R.[^1] It comes from the supply and the resistance alone. L does not appear in it.\n\nNow the key step. Divide the rule by L, and di/dt = (E − R i) ÷ L. Take R out of the top, and di/dt = (E ÷ R − i) ÷ (L ÷ R). The top is how far the current is from its steady value. So the rate is in proportion to that gap. Call the gap g = i − E ÷ R. Below the line g is negative. While the supply holds, E ÷ R does not move. So the gap changes exactly as fast as the current. So dg/dt = −g ÷ τ, with τ = L ÷ R.[^2] That is the decay rule you separated and integrated. So g = g₀ e^(−t/τ), where g₀ is the gap at the start. Here τ = 0.5 ÷ 10 = 0.05 s and g₀ = −2.4 A. Each τ keeps 0.368 of the gap: −2.4 A, then −0.883 A, then −0.325 A. So the current reads 1.52 A after one τ and 2.08 A after two. Each τ closes 0.632 of what is left. By this rule the gap never quite reaches zero.[^3] After five τ, 0.25 s, the current reads 2.38 A, 99.3% of the way.\n\nPut together, i = E ÷ R + (i₀ − E ÷ R) e^(−t/τ).
