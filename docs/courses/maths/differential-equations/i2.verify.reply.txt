   "now": "When a quantity falls at a rate in proportion to what is left, it loses the same fraction in every equal stretch of time. Its curve is y = A e^(−t/τ), and the rule itself leads there. Take a capacitor charged to v volts, discharging through a resistor R. By Ohm's law the current leaving it is v ÷ R. You know i = C dv/dt. That current drains the capacitor, so C dv/dt = −v ÷ R. Divide both sides by C, and the rule is dv/dt = −v ÷ RC. Say R = 20 kΩ and C = 100 µF. Then RC = 2 s. At 12 V the voltage falls at 6 V a second. At 6 V it falls at 3 V a second. Half the voltage gives half the rate. A coil's current, dying through a resistor once its supply is taken away, falls the same way. A sample of radioactive atoms decays exponentially too, at a rate that depends on their decay constant.[^1]\n\nThat proportion is why equal times take equal fractions. The rate is always the same share of the voltage. So each second takes the same share, whatever the voltage is. Here each second keeps 0.607 of the voltage, from 12 V down to 7.28 V or from 2.68 V down to 1.62 V. The flat line v = 0 is this rule's steady state. Each second keeps a fraction of what is left, so by this rule the curve closes in on it but never quite gets there.[^2] Flip the sign to dy/dt = +y ÷ τ and the same reasoning gives growth. Each equal time then multiplies the amount by the same factor.\n\nTo find the curve, [[separate the variables]]: gather every v on one side and every t on the other.[^3] Divide both sides of the rule by v, to get (1 ÷ v) dv/dt = −1 ÷ RC. It is usually written dv ÷ v = −dt ÷ RC. That is shorthand for integrating both sides over time, not for cancelling the d's. Textbooks add that the slope of ln v is 1 ÷ v, for a positive v. So integrating 1 ÷ v gives ln v. Integrating the constant −1 ÷ RC gives −t ÷ RC. Each side brings its own constant, and the two join into one + c. So ln v = −t ÷ RC + c. Raising e to a power undoes ln, so v = e^(−t/RC + c). A power of a sum splits into a product, so v = e^c × e^(−t/RC). Call e^c by one letter, A. At t = 0, e^0 = 1, so v = A. A is the start voltage, and here v = 12 e^(−t/2). Write τ for RC, and the answer is y = A e^(−t/τ). Now put it back to check. Textbooks add that the slope of e^(kt) is k times e^(kt). Here k is −1 ÷ τ, so the slope is −y ÷ τ at every t. That is the rule.\n\nτ is the [[time constant]], and it has two readings. After each τ the amount is e^(−1), or 0.368, of what it was. A coil's current falls this way, to 0.368 of the preceding value in each time constant.[^4] The other reading comes from the starting slope, −A ÷ τ. A straight line at that slope would reach zero at exactly one τ. The curve is only down to 0.368 of its start by then. For a capacitor τ = RC, in seconds. For a coil τ = L ÷ R.[^5]\n\n**A rate in proportion to what is left separates and integrates to y = A e^(−t/τ), with A the start and τ the time constant.
