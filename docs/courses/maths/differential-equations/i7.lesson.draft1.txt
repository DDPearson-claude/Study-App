    "text": "Push a lightly damped spring or circuit back and forth and it settles into a steady swing at the push's own rate. That swing is biggest when the push comes near the rate the system would ring at by itself.\n\nGive lesson 5's mass a push F cos ωt, where ω is how fast the push swings in radians a second. The rule becomes m x″ + c x′ + k x = F cos ωt, and every term is a force in newtons. The answer is a steady part set by the push plus a transient set by the start, which dies away.[^2] The transient is the last lesson's free ringing. With 1 kg, 0.5 N·s/m and 4 N/m its envelope has a time constant of 2m ÷ c = 4 s, so by 20 s only the steady swing is left.\n\nWhy at the push's rate? Each derivative of a cosine at ω is another cosine at ω, so every term can match the push at every instant. The spring's pull is kX, the mass term mω²X points the opposite way, and the damper's drag cωX sits at right angles, like the three phasors of a series RLC loop. They add to F, so X = F ÷ √((k − mω²)² + (cω)²). At 1 rad/s with a 1 N push, X = 0.329 m. The spring and mass terms cancel at ω = √(k ÷ m) = 2 rad/s, the [[natural frequency]], so only the damper is left and X = F ÷ cω = 1 m. The swing is always largest at the natural frequency.[^1] That peak is [[resonance]]. More damping gives a smaller peak.[^4] Damp it enough and the hill goes, so an overdamped system never resonates.[^3] The RLC loop with an AC supply obeys the same equation.[^5]\n\nThis is how a circuit that rings can be driven to a big swing. It is a simplified model with ideal parts.\n\n**Pushed back and forth, a lightly damped spring or circuit settles to a steady swing at the push's frequency, largest near its natural frequency.**[^1]"
