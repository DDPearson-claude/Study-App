    "text": "Push a lightly damped spring or circuit back and forth and it settles into a steady swing. That swing keeps time with the push, not with the system's own ringing. It is biggest when the push comes near the rate the system rings at by itself.\n\nTake the same 1 kg mass, 4 N/m spring and 0.5 N·s/m damper as before. Add a 1 N push, F cos ωt. Here ω is how fast the push swings, in radians a second. The rule becomes m x″ + c x′ + k x = F cos ωt. Every term is a force, in newtons. The answer has the shape you met with the coil switched on. It is a steady part set by the push, plus a transient set by the start.[^2] The transient is the free ringing from the last lesson. It dies inside an envelope with a time constant of 2m ÷ c, which is 4 s here. By 20 s less than 1% of it is left.\n\nWhy does the steady swing keep the push's time? Try a swing X cos(ωt − φ) in the rule. Each derivative of a cosine at rate ω is a cosine at the same rate, scaled by ω. So every term swings at ω, and together they can match the push at every instant. Now the sizes. The spring's pull is kX. The mass term is mω²X, and it points the opposite way. The damper's drag is cωX, a quarter cycle round from both. These are the three phasors of a series RLC loop: two opposite and one at right angles. They must add to F, so X = F ÷ √((k − mω²)² + (cω)²). At 1 rad/s that is 1 ÷ √(9 + 0.25), which is 0.329 m.\n\nNow sweep the push. A slow push barely asks the mass to move, so the spring takes it all and X is near F ÷ k, 0.25 m. A fast push leaves the mass no time to follow. At 4 rad/s the mass term is 16X against the spring's 4X, and X falls to 0.08 m. In between, the spring and mass terms cancel when mω² = k. That is at ω = √(k ÷ m) = 2 rad/s, the [[natural frequency]], the rate it would ring at with no damping. There only the damper holds the push back, so X = F ÷ cω = 1 ÷ (0.5 × 2) = 1 m. A lightly damped system swings hardest when the push is near its natural frequency.[^1] That peak is [[resonance]].\n\nDamping sets the height. At 2 rad/s the swing is F ÷ cω, so doubling the damping halves it. More damping gives a smaller peak.[^4] The top also slides a little lower, because the damper's share cω is smaller at lower ω. At damping 2 the top is 0.29 m near 1.41 rad/s. Heavy damping removes the hill altogether. From c² = 2mk, near 2.83 N·s/m here, the damper's share grows faster than the spring and mass gap closes, so the swing only shrinks as the push speeds up. That is still short of critical at 4. So a peak in the swing needs light damping, and an overdamped spring has none.[^3]\n\nAn AC supply on a series RLC loop gives the same equation, L q″ + R q′ + q ÷ C = V cos ωt.[^5] The capacitor's charge swings as the mass does, and its voltage is the charge ÷ C. The current is the charge's swing times ω. That extra ω moves the current's peak to exactly 1 ÷ √(LC), where ωL equals 1 ÷ ωC. That is the series resonance you already know. The capacitor's charge, and so its voltage, peaks a touch below it.\n\nSo a loop that rings when switched on can also be driven into a big swing, with the capacitor's voltage far above the supply's. This is a simplified model with ideal parts, whose pull and drag grow in step with stretch and speed. Real springs, and a ship's roll, follow it only for small swings.\n\n**Pushed back and forth, a lightly damped spring or circuit settles to a steady swing at the push's frequency, largest when the push is near its natural frequency.**[^1]"

A very slow push gives the spring's own stretch, F ÷ k. Wait about five envelope time constants for the transient to die before you read a steady value.\n\n**Worked example:** a 10 V supply at 1000 rad/s, which is 159 Hz, feeds 100 mH, 10 µF and 40 Ω in series.
