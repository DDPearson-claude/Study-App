Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Ohm’s law is applied in AC circuits with impedance: current is voltage divided by impedance, I = E/Z. [R1]
- Impedances add in series, and their reciprocals add in parallel, just as resistances do. [R2]
- One university course says all the DC circuit analysis techniques work with AC if you handle the complex arithmetic. [R3]
- The phase angle between voltage and current is the same as the phase angle of the impedance. [R4]
- In a parallel AC circuit the branch currents add to form the total current, as in DC. [R5]
- A capacitor in parallel with an inductive load draws a leading current that cancels part or all of the load’s lagging reactive current, lowering the supply current. [R6][R7][R8]
- A complex number is drawn on a plane with a horizontal real axis and a vertical imaginary axis. [R9]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Adding complex numbers is essentially the same as adding two-dimensional vectors. [R10]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Addition is most easily done in Cartesian form, multiplication and division in polar form, so you choose the form that suits the sum. [R11]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- On the complex plane, an impedance on the real axis such as 6 + j0 is a pure resistance, and one on the j axis such as 0 + j4 a pure reactance. [R12]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The conjugate of a + bi is a - bi: the same number with the sign of the imaginary part changed. [R13]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- On the plane the conjugate is the mirror image of the number in the real axis. [R14]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A complex number times its conjugate is a real number. [R15]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Division is done by multiplying numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate of the denominator. [R16]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- In polar form, to multiply you multiply the moduli and add the arguments. [R17]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- To divide in polar form, you divide the moduli and subtract the arguments. [R18]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Multiplication and division are most easily done in polar form, while addition and subtraction are easier in Cartesian form. [R11]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A complex number r∠θ can also be written in exponential form re^(jθ), with θ in radians. [R19][R20]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Impedance is measured in ohms and is the AC analogue of resistance, combining the effects of resistance, capacitive reactance and inductive reactance. [R21][R22]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- On the impedance plane, resistance points right, inductive impedance (90 degrees) points up and capacitive impedance (minus 90 degrees) points down. [R23]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Inductive reactance is X_L = 2 pi f L when frequency f is in hertz, and capacitive reactance is X_C = 1/(omega C). [R24][R25]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The peak current in an inductor is proportional to the angular frequency, while a capacitor’s reactance tends to zero at very high frequencies. [R26][R27]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A secondary source (Kirtley's power-systems text) notes that the phase angle between voltage and current is the same as the phase angle of the impedance. [R4]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Most ships at sea run at 60 Hz. [R28]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The mains supply in the UK is alternating current at 50 hertz and 230 volts; UK regulations declare a low voltage supply as 50 hertz and 230 volts between phase and neutral, unless otherwise agreed. [R29][R30]  (whole topic)
- Impedance, like resistance, is measured in ohms. [R22]  (whole topic)
- Engineers write j for the square root of minus one because i already stands for electric current. [R31]  (whole topic)
- Adding a capacitor in parallel with a lagging inductive load draws a leading current that partly or completely neutralises the load’s lagging reactive current, so the supply current falls. [R6][R7][R8]  (whole topic)
