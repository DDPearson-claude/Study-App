  "text": "Read a complex number as an arrow from 0, as in the earlier lessons. There are two ways to say where an arrow goes. You can give its parts, across and up, as a + jb. Or you can give how long it is and which way it points: its length r and its angle θ, written r∠θ. Both name the same arrow. Adding is easiest with the parts, and multiplying and dividing are easiest with the length and the angle,[^1] which is why the next lesson needs this one, and why later lessons write AC currents this way.\n\nTake 3 + j4: 3 across and 4 up. The across part, the up part and the arrow make a right-angled triangle, with the arrow as the longest side, so Pythagoras gives its length: √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. That is the 5 you met in the conjugate lesson. The up side is 4 long, the size of the j part, so square 4, not j4. (j4)² is −16, and √(9 − 16) = √−7 is not a length at all. The length is the distance of the tip from 0. It is called the [[modulus]], or magnitude, and it is never negative.[^2] For any arrow, r = √(a² + b²).\n\nThe angle is measured from the positive real axis round to the arrow,[^3] anticlockwise counting as positive, and it is called the [[argument]]. In the triangle the up side is opposite the angle and the across side is next to it, so tan θ = 4 ÷ 3, and θ = tan⁻¹(4 ÷ 3) = 53.13°. So 3 + j4 = 5∠53.13°, which is [[polar form]].[^4] Now take −3 − j4, the same arrow turned half round. Its b ÷ a is −4 ÷ −3, which is 4 ÷ 3 again, so the calculator says 53.13° again: the picture's dashed ghost arrow, pointing up and right. The calculator sees only that one number, and the two minus signs have cancelled before it looks, so it cannot tell the two arrows apart. It always answers with an angle between −90° and 90°, pointing into the right-hand half of the plane.
