Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Impedance is measured in ohms and is the AC analogue of resistance, combining the effects of resistance, capacitive reactance and inductive reactance. [R1][R2]
- On the impedance plane, resistance points right, inductive impedance (90 degrees) points up and capacitive impedance (minus 90 degrees) points down. [R3]
- Inductive reactance is X_L = 2 pi f L when frequency f is in hertz, and capacitive reactance is X_C = 1/(omega C). [R4][R5]
- The peak current in an inductor is proportional to the angular frequency, while a capacitor’s reactance tends to zero at very high frequencies. [R6][R7]
- Multiplying a number by minus one turns its arrow through 180 degrees, a half turn. [R8]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Multiplying a real or complex number by j rotates it by plus 90 degrees, and j squared is minus one. [R9][R10]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Engineers use the symbol j for this number because i is already used for current. [R11]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The same number is written i in maths and j in electrical engineering. [R12]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Introducing j as the square root of minus one is contested: mathcentre starts from j = √−1 and gets j² = −1 from it, while one paper on teaching calls that start unjustifiable because it leads to misconceptions. [R10][R13]  [CONTESTED: mathcentre defines j as √−1 and derives j² = −1; the Scimath teaching paper argues against defining it as √−1.]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A complex number is drawn on a plane with a horizontal real axis and a vertical imaginary axis. [R14]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Adding complex numbers is essentially the same as adding two-dimensional vectors. [R15]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Addition is most easily done in Cartesian form, multiplication and division in polar form, so you choose the form that suits the sum. [R16]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- On the complex plane, an impedance on the real axis such as 6 + j0 is a pure resistance, and one on the j axis such as 0 + j4 a pure reactance. [R17]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The modulus is the distance of the point (a, b) from the origin, and it is never negative. [R18]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The argument is the angle between the positive real axis and the line from the origin to the point. [R19]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A complex number with modulus r and argument theta is written r angle theta. [R20]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- When finding the angle with an inverse tangent, check the quadrant, because a calculator’s answer may not be in the correct one. [R21]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Going back from length and angle: the across part is r cos theta and the up part is r sin theta. [R22]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The argument is normally taken in the interval minus pi (not included) to pi, that is more than −180° and up to 180°, though adding any whole number of turns gives the same direction; HN units call this the principal argument. [R23][R24]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A phasor is a complex number that represents the magnitude and phase of a voltage or a current; the marine syllabus resolves phasors into their two components. [R25][R26]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The mains supply in the UK is alternating current at 50 hertz and 230 volts; UK regulations declare a low voltage supply as 50 hertz and 230 volts between phase and neutral, unless otherwise agreed. [R27][R28]  (whole topic)
- Impedance, like resistance, is measured in ohms. [R2]  (whole topic)
- Engineers write j for the square root of minus one because i already stands for electric current. [R11]  (whole topic)
- Adding a capacitor in parallel with a lagging inductive load draws a leading current that partly or completely neutralises the load’s lagging reactive current, so the supply current falls. [R29][R30][R31]  (whole topic)

The current wave changes fastest as it crosses zero and stops changing for an instant at its peak.
