  "text": "To divide by a complex number, multiply the top and the bottom by the bottom's [[conjugate]]: the same number with the sign of its j part flipped.[^2] That turns the bottom into an ordinary real number, and dividing by a real number is easy: divide each part. Later lessons divide this way to work out AC circuits.\n\nThe conjugate of 3 + j4 is 3 − j4. The real part stays as it is; only the j part's sign flips. On the complex plane 3 + j4 is 3 across and 4 up, and 3 − j4 is 3 across and 4 down, so the conjugate is the number's mirror image in the real axis.[^3] Flip both signs instead and you get −3 − j4, which is the arrow turned half round, times −1: a different number. A number on the real axis, such as 5, sits on the mirror line, so it is its own conjugate: its j part is 0, and flipping 0 changes nothing. The two roots of x² + 2x + 5 = 0 from the first lesson, −1 + j2 and −1 − j2, are a mirror pair.\n\nNow multiply a number by its conjugate, with the four tiles from the last lesson: (3 + j4)(3 − j4). The tiles are 3 × 3 = 9, 3 × (−j4) = −j12, j4 × 3 = j12, and j4 × (−j4) = −j × j × 16. The two j tiles are the same size with opposite signs, so they cancel. In the last tile j × j = −1, so it becomes +16, not −16. The answer is 9 + 16 = 25, with no j part at all: an arrow lying flat on the real axis. The same happens for any number: (a + jb)(a − jb) = a² − jab + jab + b² = a² + b². So a number times its conjugate is always a real number,[^4] and because a² and b² are squares, it is never negative. From Quadratics you might expect the difference of two squares, a² − b². It is a² − (jb)², and (jb)² = −b², so the minus becomes a plus. The arrows show the same thing. In the last lesson, multiplying by 3 + j4 turned and stretched any arrow the way 1 is turned and stretched into 3 + j4: up by 3 + j4's angle, and 5 times longer, since by Pythagoras 3 + j4 is 5 long. The mirror 3 − j4 points down by exactly that angle, so the turn brings it level with the real axis, and the stretch makes it 5 × 5 = 25 long.\n\nThat is what makes dividing work. Take 10 ÷ (3 + j4). Multiply the top and the bottom by 3 − j4.[^5] Since (3 − j4) ÷ (3 − j4) is 1, this is multiplying by 1, so the answer does not change. The top becomes 10 × (3 − j4) = 30 − j40 and the bottom becomes 25, so the answer is 30 ÷ 25 and −40 ÷ 25, which is 1.2 − j1.6. Textbooks call this step [[rationalising]] the bottom. Check by multiplying back: (1.2 − j1.6)(3 + j4) = 3.6 + j4.8 − j4.8 + 6.4 = 10. Dividing part by part does not work, because each part of the bottom multiplies both parts of the answer.
