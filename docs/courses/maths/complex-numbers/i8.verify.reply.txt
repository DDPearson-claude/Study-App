   "now": "Ohm's law works for AC just as it does for DC, once the opposition is the impedance Z, a complex number: V = IZ, so I = V ÷ Z.[^3] Impedances in series add, and for parts side by side their reciprocals add, the same rules as for resistors.[^4] One university course puts it plainly: all the DC methods work with AC, as long as you handle the complex arithmetic.[^5] What is new is that every answer comes with an angle as well as a size, and with the supply drawn at 0° a current's angle tells you how far it is behind or ahead of the voltage.\n\nTake the resistor and coil from the last lesson, Z = 8 + j6 = 10∠36.87° Ω, on 230 V mains.[^1] A single wave's angle depends on when you start the clock, so we choose the supply voltage as the zero and draw it along the real axis, V = 230∠0°. Divide in polar form, as in Multiplying and dividing in polar form: divide the lengths and take the angles away.[^6] I = 230∠0° ÷ 10∠36.87° = 23∠−36.87° A. The length, 23 A, is what a clamp meter would read. The angle says the current lags 36.87° behind the voltage. Why behind? Turn it round: V = I × Z, and multiplying by Z stretches I ten times and turns it forward 36.87°, because multiplying adds the angles.[^7] V sits at 0°, so I must sit 36.87° back. The angle between the voltage and the current is the impedance's angle.[^8] A drawing of a circuit's voltages and currents as arrows from one point is a [[phasor diagram]]. Drag Z's tip higher and I swings further back; drag it below the real axis, as a capacitor does, and I swings ahead of V.\n\nIn series one current flows through every part, so each part's voltage is that current times the part's own impedance. Across the resistor, I × 8 = 184∠−36.87° V, lying along I because 8 has no angle. Across the coil, I × j6 = 23∠−36.87° × 6∠90° = 138∠53.13° V, a quarter turn ahead of I.[^2] A meter would read 184 V and 138 V, and 184 + 138 = 322 V, more than the supply. Nothing is wrong: the two voltages stand at a quarter turn, so they add as arrows, tip to tail.
