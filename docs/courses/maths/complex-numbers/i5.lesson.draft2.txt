    "text": "Read a complex number as an arrow from 0, as in the earlier lessons. There are two ways to say where an arrow goes. You can give its parts, across and up, as a + jb. Or you can give how long it is and which way it points: its length r and its angle θ, written r∠θ. Both name the same arrow. Adding is easiest with the parts, and multiplying and dividing are easiest with the length and the angle,[^8] which is why the next lesson needs this one, and why later lessons write AC currents this way.\n\nTake 3 + j4: 3 across and 4 up. The across part, the up part and the arrow make a right-angled triangle, with the arrow as the longest side, so Pythagoras gives its length: √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. That is the 5 you met in the conjugate lesson. The up side is 4 long, the size of the j part, so square 4, not j4. (j4)² is −16, and √(9 − 16) = √−7 is not a length at all. The length is the distance of the tip from 0. It is called the [[modulus]], or magnitude, and it is never negative.[^1] For any arrow, r = √(a² + b²).\n\nThe angle is measured from the positive real axis round to the arrow,[^2] anticlockwise counting as positive, and it is called the [[argument]]. In the triangle the up side is opposite the angle and the across side is next to it, so tan θ = 4 ÷ 3, and θ = tan⁻¹(4 ÷ 3) = 53.13°. So 3 + j4 = 5∠53.13°, which is [[polar form]].[^3] Now take −3 − j4, the same arrow turned half round. Its b ÷ a is −4 ÷ −3, which is 4 ÷ 3 again, so the calculator says 53.13° again: the picture's dashed ghost arrow, pointing up and right. The calculator sees only that one number, and the two minus signs have cancelled before it looks, so it cannot tell the two arrows apart. It always answers with an angle between −90° and 90°, pointing into the right-hand half of the plane. So whenever a is negative and the arrow points into a left-hand quarter, the calculator's answer points exactly the wrong way, half a turn out, and turning it 180° fixes it. −3 − j4 points down, so take 180° away: 53.13° − 180° = −126.87°. For −3 + j4 the calculator says −53.13°, which is the direction of 3 − j4, again the arrow turned half round. This arrow points up, so add 180°: 126.87°. That is why you sketch the arrow first and check that the angle lands in the same quarter.[^4] Adding or taking away 360°, a whole turn, leaves an arrow pointing the same way, so −126.87° and 233.13° name the same arrow. Textbooks add that the usual choice is an angle more than −180° and up to 180°, which college texts call the principal argument.\n\nGoing back is easier. The across part is the arrow's shadow on the real axis and the up part its shadow on the j axis, as with Trigonometry's arrow and its shadows, so a = r cos θ and b = r sin θ.[^5] So r∠θ = r cos θ + j r sin θ, which books also write r(cos θ + j sin θ). 10∠30° is 10 × 0.866 = 8.66 across and 10 × 0.5 = 5 up, so it is 8.66 + j5. No fix is needed this way round, because cos and sin come out negative by themselves in the quarters where the shadow points left or down, as you saw on Trigonometry's circle: 10∠150° is −8.66 + j5. **Find the length with Pythagoras and the angle with tan⁻¹(b ÷ a), check the angle against a sketch and turn it 180° when the arrow points left;[^4] go back with a = r cos θ and b = r sin θ.[^5]**"
