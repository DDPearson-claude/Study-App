    "text": "An average rate of change says how much one thing changed for each unit of another, between two points. You already work these out. 120 km in 2 hours is 60 km per hour, the change in distance divided by the change in time.\n\nCalculus writes the rule behind a graph as f(x), read \"f of x\". A boat leaving a buoy at a steady 4 metres a second is f(x) = 4x metres away after x seconds, and f(3) means the rule applied to 3, which is 12 m. Pick two points on the graph, P at 1 s and Q at 3 s, and join them with a straight line, a [[chord]]. Δ, the Greek letter delta, means \"change in\". From P to Q, Δx is 2 s and Δy is 8 m, so Δy ÷ Δx is 4 m per s. The units come with the division, metres over seconds. On a straight line every chord gives the same answer, because each second adds the same 4 m, so Δy is always 4 times Δx.\n\nA boat speeding up from the quay is a curve. Say it covers f(x) = x² metres in x seconds, where x² means x × x. From 0 s to 1 s it goes 1 m, but from 3 s to 4 s it goes 16 − 9 = 7 m, so the chords no longer agree. Δy ÷ Δx over a stretch is the [[average rate of change]], the ratio of the change in f to the change in x.[^1] It is the steady rate that would give the same change over the same stretch. Calculus books use the slope of such a chord to estimate the rate at which one quantity changes in relation to another.[^2] A falling graph gives a minus rate: a tank going from 200 L to 50 L in 5 minutes changes by −150 L, so −30 L per minute.\n\nThe course's puzzle, the work done by a gas whose pressure keeps falling, starts from reading a graph like this, one quantity changing per unit of another. **The average rate of change between two points is Δy ÷ Δx, the change in y divided by the change in x,[^1] the slope of the chord joining them, in units of y per unit of x.**"
