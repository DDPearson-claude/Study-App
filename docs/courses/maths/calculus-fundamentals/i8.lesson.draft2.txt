    "text": "Adding up a rate over a stretch of time gives the total it delivers: current added up over time is charge, and speed added up over time is distance. You can add it up with bars, and the reverse rule from the last lesson gets the bars' exact total in one subtraction.\n\nWhen a speed holds steady, the distance is the speed times the time, the area of the rectangle under its flat graph.[^7] Any steady rate works the same way: a steady 3 A for 4 s moves 3 × 4 = 12 C, because an amp is a coulomb each second. When the rate changes, no single rectangle fits, so cut the time into narrow bars, take the rate as steady across each one, and add their amounts. That is what an integral is, a quantity described as a sum of many small pieces.[^6] Take a current that rises, i = 3t² amps, from 0 s to 2 s. Cut the 2 s into 4 bars, each 0.5 s wide, and give each bar the current at its start: 0, 0.75, 3 and 6.75 A. Each bar's charge is its current times 0.5 s, and together they make 5.25 C. That is short, because the current keeps rising across each bar while the bar's flat top does not, so each bar misses a thin sliver under the curve. Thinner bars miss thinner slivers: 8 bars give 6.56 C, 100 give 7.88 C, and the total settles on 8 C. Taking each bar's height at its middle gets close much sooner. The bar's top then crosses the curve: on the bar's left half it pokes above the curve, on its right half the curve rises above it, and the two slivers nearly cancel, so 4 bars give 7.875 C. On a straight line the two slivers are the same size, so mid-height bars give the exact total. Textbooks call this the [[mid-ordinate rule]], and it is one way to add up a graph you have only as readings.\n\nNow the reverse rule. Charge is what you get by integrating the current over the time,[^4] and the last lesson gives q = t³ + c. The charge that flows between 0 s and 2 s is the charge at the end take away the charge at the start: (8 + c) − (0 + c) = 8 C, the number the bars settle on. The c cancels, so any c will do.[^5] Here is why the two agree. Each thin bar is the current, how fast the charge grows, times a short time, so it is very nearly how much q rises across that bar. The rise across the first bar, plus the rise across the second, and so on, is the rise from start to end, because each bar picks up where the last left off. Thinner bars make each \"very nearly\" closer, so the bars' total settles on exactly that rise. So instead of adding bars, take the reverse rule's answer, call it F, put in the end time b and the start time a, and subtract: F(b) − F(a).[^2] Adding bars only approximates; the rule gets it at once.[^1] This is a [[definite integral]], an integral with two ends, written ∫₀² 3t² dt = 2³ − 0³ = 8, the small numbers on the ∫ being the start and end.\n\nA current can flow back. Say i = 4 − 2t amps from 0 s to 3 s: it flows forwards until 2 s, then turns negative. A negative current times a time is a negative amount, charge taken away, so bars below the axis count as minus: 4 C forwards, then 1 C back, 3 C in all. Count the 1 C back as plus and you get 5 C, more than ever arrived. The reverse rule does the minus for you: F(t) = 4t − t² is 12 − 9 = 3 at 3 s and 0 at 0 s, so 3 C. A definite integral gives the change, and the new value is the starting value plus the integral of the rate.[^3] The capacitor from the last lesson held 3 C at 0 s with a current of t amps. The reverse rule turns t into 0.5t², so ∫₀⁴ t dt = 0.5 × 16 − 0 = 8, and it held 3 + 8 = 11 C at 4 s, as you found there. You will hear that the integral is the area under the curve, and it is, counting area below the axis as minus. But physics-education researchers found that students who could say so did not necessarily connect the area with the adding up, so they could not use it on new problems.[^8] Under a current graph the area's units are amps times seconds, coulombs: a charge. **Bars under a rate graph add up the amount, rate × time, bar by bar; as they thin, their total settles on F(b) − F(a), the reverse rule's value at the end minus its value at the start,[^1][^2] with bars below the axis counting as minus.**"

    "text": "- Check the units first: the rate times the time should give the amount you want (amps × seconds = coulombs, metres per second × seconds = metres).\n- If you have the rate as a rule, integrate it term by term with the reverse rule. Leave out the c, because it cancels.\n- Put in the end time, then the start time, and take the second from the first: F(b) − F(a).\n- If you have only a graph or readings, split the time into equal strips, read the rate at the middle of each strip, add those readings and multiply by the strip width: the mid-ordinate rule.\n- Count parts below the axis as minus. That gives the net change. For the total either way, such as how far a boat went out and back, work out each part's size on its own and add the sizes.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** more, thinner strips get closer. On a straight stretch a mid-height strip is exact. The answer is the change, so to find where the amount ends up, add it to the starting value.[^3]\n\n**Worked example:** Say a current falls steadily, i = 8 − 2t amps, and you want the charge that flows from 1 s to 3 s. The reverse rule gives F(t) = 8t − t². At 3 s, F = 24 − 9 = 15; at 1 s, F = 8 − 1 = 7. The charge is 15 − 7 = 8 C. Check with one mid-height strip: at 2 s the current is 8 − 4 = 4 A, and 4 A × 2 s = 8 C, exact because the graph is straight.\n\n**Where you will meet it:** an HN engineering maths unit applies integration to the area under a velocity time curve, giving distance travelled.[^22]\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Reading the height as the amount: a current at one moment is not a charge.\n- Adding the heights and forgetting to multiply by the width.\n- Forgetting to take away F(a) when the start is not at 0.\n- Putting the end times into the rate instead of its reverse rule.\n- Taking the end from the start, F(a) − F(b), which flips the sign.\n- Counting parts below the axis as plus when you want the net change."
