    "text": "Gas pushing a piston does work, and on a graph of its pressure against its volume that work is the area under the curve.[^1] When the pressure falls as the gas expands, you add the area up in thin bars, as in the last lesson, and for a curve that follows pVⁿ = C the reverse rule gets it exactly in one line: (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂) ÷ (n − 1).\n\nStart with a steady pressure. Textbooks add that work is a force times the distance it moves, and that pressure is force on each square metre. So gas at pressure p pushes a piston of area A with a force p × A. If the piston moves out a distance d, the gas's volume grows by A × d, its change in volume, ΔV. The work is p × A × d, which is p × ΔV: the piston's area drops out, so a wide cylinder and a narrow one follow the same rule. Draw pressure up the side and volume along the bottom, a [[pV diagram]], and a steady pressure is a flat line, with the work the rectangle under it, height p times width ΔV. In kPa and m³ the work comes out in kJ, because a pascal is a newton on each square metre, and newtons per square metre times cubic metres is newton metres, joules. Say 200 kPa pushes the volume from 0.1 to 0.3 m³: the work is 200 × 0.2 = 40 kJ. It is the change in volume that counts: 200 × 0.3 = 60 kJ would count the 0.1 m³ the gas filled before the piston moved. In a real cylinder the pressure falls as the gas expands, so no one rectangle fits. Cut the change in volume into thin bars, take each bar's pressure at its middle, multiply by its width, and add, the mid-ordinate rule from the last lesson. Your syllabus draws the curve as pVⁿ = C:[^4] pressure times volume to the power n stays the same number, C, and a process that follows it is called [[polytropic]].[^5] Say gas at 400 kPa fills 0.1 m³ and expands to 0.2 m³ with n = 2. Then C = 400 × 0.1² = 4, and at 0.2 m³ the pressure has fallen to 4 ÷ 0.2² = 100 kPa. Four bars give 19.82 kJ, eight give 19.95 kJ and a hundred give 20.00 kJ, settling on 20 kJ. That settled total is the integral of p over the change in volume, the area under the pV curve.[^1] The MCA's Applied Heat syllabus says the same, that the area of the pV diagram represents the work done,[^3] and it asks for the area of an indicator card by the mid-ordinate rule.[^8] Textbooks add that an indicator card is a pV diagram traced from a running engine's cylinder.\n\nNow the exact area. pVⁿ = C gives p = C ÷ Vⁿ, which is also written C × V⁻ⁿ, because a power below zero means one over: V⁻ⁿ is 1 ÷ Vⁿ. The reverse rule works for this power too: add one, giving 1 − n, and divide by it, so V⁻ⁿ integrates to V¹⁻ⁿ ÷ (1 − n). Put in the end volume, take away the start, and the work is C × (V₂¹⁻ⁿ − V₁¹⁻ⁿ) ÷ (1 − n). It tidies up. V¹⁻ⁿ is V ÷ Vⁿ, and C ÷ Vⁿ is the pressure at that volume, so C × V₂¹⁻ⁿ is just p₂V₂ and C × V₁¹⁻ⁿ is p₁V₁. That leaves (p₂V₂ − p₁V₁) ÷ (1 − n). Swapping the order of both subtractions turns the top and the bottom minus together, which leaves the value alone, and gives W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂) ÷ (n − 1), the area under pVⁿ = C your maths syllabus asks you to derive.[^4] For the example, (400 × 0.1 − 100 × 0.2) ÷ (2 − 1) = (40 − 20) ÷ 1 = 20 kJ, the total the bars settle on. n = 1 will not go: the power would be −1, the one power the reverse rule cannot take, because it would divide by 0.[^12] Textbooks add that that case needs logarithms, a later course.\n\nRun it backwards and the gas is squeezed, from 0.2 m³ at 100 kPa back to 0.1 m³ at 400 kPa. The curve is the same and still above the axis, but each bar's width, the change in volume, is now minus, so each pressure times change in volume is minus. The rule gives (100 × 0.2 − 400 × 0.1) ÷ 1 = −20 kJ. Work done by the gas is positive for expansion and negative for compression:[^2] the gas did −20 kJ, which means the piston did 20 kJ of work on it. Some books give the work done on the gas instead, (p₂V₂ − p₁V₁) ÷ (n − 1),[^6] the same size with the sign turned round, so check which a question asks for. Last, a rectangle with the same area over the same change in volume has a height of area ÷ width, 20 kJ ÷ 0.1 m³ = 200 kPa: the steady pressure that would do the same work. Textbooks add that this is called the [[mean effective pressure]]. It is not the average of the end pressures, 250 kPa, because the curve spends most of its width below 250 kPa. **The work a gas does is the area under its pV curve, pressure times change in volume added up bar by bar,[^1] and for pVⁿ = C that area is exactly (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂) ÷ (n − 1), positive as the gas expands and negative as it is squeezed.[^2]**"
