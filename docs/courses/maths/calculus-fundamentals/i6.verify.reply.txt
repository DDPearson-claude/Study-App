   "now": "A smooth curve is flat for an instant wherever it turns over at a top or a bottom, so its derivative there is zero, and setting the derivative to zero finds the largest or smallest value. Here is why. Ride a point along a curve that climbs and then falls. While it climbs, the tangent slopes up and the slope is plus; once it falls, the tangent slopes down and the slope is minus. On a smooth curve the slope changes gradually, with no jumps, so on its way from plus to minus it passes through zero, and it does so just where climbing stops and falling starts: at the top. There the tangent is level, and the derivative is zero where a graph has a level tangent.[^1] A place where a curve turns like this is a [[turning point]], and at a turning point the derivative is zero.[^2] On the slope tracker you see it twice over: the slope readout passes through zero, and the dots on the bottom graph cross the axis, just as P rides over the top.\n\nTake 40 m of barrier tape round a rectangular work area. If one side is x metres, two neighbouring sides take half the tape, 20 m, so the other side is 20 − x, and the area is A = x(20 − x) = 20x − x² square metres. A long thin strip encloses very little, so which shape encloses the most? The power rule gives dA/dx = 20 − 2x. Set it to zero: 20 − 2x = 0, so x = 10 m, the other side is 20 − 10 = 10 m too, and the area is 10 × 10 = 100 m², a square. Either side it is less: at x = 4 the area is 4 × 16 = 64 m² with slope 12, and at x = 16 it is 64 m² again with slope −12. To tell a top from a bottom without trying values, look at how the slope itself changes: its rate of change is the second derivative from the last lesson. Here d²A/dx² = −2, so the slope is falling, from plus through zero to minus, and the flat point is a top, the largest the area can be, its [[maximum]]. On a cup-shaped curve the slope rises from minus through zero to plus, the second derivative is plus, and the flat point is a bottom, its [[minimum]]. Engineering maths asks for exactly this: first and second derivatives give the position and the nature of a turning point.[^3]\n\nA zero slope is a candidate, not a guarantee. Away from the ends, a top or a bottom of a smooth curve can only be where the slope is zero,[^4] but a zero slope need not be a top or a bottom.[^5] The curve y = x³ climbs, is level for an instant at x = 0, then climbs again: its slope, 3x², is 3 at x = −1, 0 at x = 0 and 3 at x = 1, plus on both sides. Its second derivative, 6x, is 0 there too, so that test cannot decide; look at the slope just either side instead. Two more limits keep the rule honest. A curve with a sharp corner can turn there without being flat, because its slope jumps, as the capacitor's voltage slope jumped from climbing to 0 in the last lesson. And when x can only run between two ends, the largest value may sit at an end; if it is not at an end, it is at a turning point.[^6] So check the ends as well.\n\nThis is how engineers find the most of something. A source such as a battery has a resistance inside it, r, and textbooks add that the voltage at its terminals is its open-circuit voltage E less the drop across r, V = E − Ir. So the power into the load is P = VI = (E − Ir)I = EI − I²r. A small current carries little power, and a large current drops most of E across r, leaving little voltage at the terminals, so somewhere between, the power is largest. Take an example source with E = 12 V and r = 0.5 Ω: P = 12I − 0.5I², so dP/dI = 12 − I, which is zero at I = 12 A, where P = 144 − 72 = 72 W, against none at 0 A or at the short-circuit 24 A, and the second derivative, −1, says it is a top. With letters, E − 2rI = 0 gives I = E ÷ 2r, the terminal voltage falls to E ÷ 2, and so the load's resistance, V ÷ I, comes out equal to r: the most power reaches a load whose resistance matches the source's. Finding that condition by differentiating is a standard engineering problem.[^7] It is the most power, not the best use of it: at 12 A the source also turns 12² × 0.5 = 72 W into heat inside itself. This fixed r is a simplified picture, since a real source's internal resistance changes as it works. When the slope equation is too hard to solve, read it off the picture. Fold an open box from a 30 cm square sheet with squares of side x cut from its corners. Small cuts make a wide but shallow tray, big cuts a deep box on a small base, so the largest lies between: V = x(30 − 2x)² = 4x³ − 120x² + 900x, so dV/dx = 12x² − 240x + 900. The slope readout hits zero at x = 5 cm; check it by putting it back, 300 − 1200 + 900 = 0. The box holds 5 × 20 × 20 = 2,000 cm³, more than the 1,936 cm³ at 4 cm or the 1,944 cm³ at 6 cm. The slope is zero again at 15 cm, but that is a bottom, where no base is left. **Away from the ends, a top or a bottom of a smooth curve is where the derivative is zero,[^2] so set the derivative to zero to find it, then use the second derivative, or the slope just either side, to tell which it is.[^3]**"
