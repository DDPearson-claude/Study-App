Last lesson the speeding boat, f(x) = x² metres after x seconds, was doing 6 m per s at 3 s. Do the same at other moments and each gives one rate: 2 m per s at 1 s, 4 at 2 s, 6 at 3 s and 8 at 4 s, each the number its chords settle on. Plot each rate as a dot on a second graph below, at the same x, with the rate going up instead of the distance. The dots line up on a straight line through zero that rises 2 for every 1 across, so at x seconds the boat is doing 2x metres per second. That rule is a function in its own right, the [[derivative]] of f, written f′(x) and read 'f dash of x' (many books say 'f prime'). Like any function, it takes a number, x, and gives one back, here the slope of f at x. It gives the rate at each point of f where there is one.[^2] For f(x) = x², f′(x) = 2x.\n\nFour dots on a line suggest the rule, and a square shows why it holds at every x. Since x² is x × x, picture it as the area of a square of side x. Make the side longer by a small step h. The new square is the old one plus two thin strips, each x long and h wide, plus a tiny corner h by h, the same area model you used for brackets in Linear equations. So the area grows by 2xh + h². On the graph of y = x², moving from x to x + h raises y by exactly that growth, so the chord between them has Δy = 2xh + h² over Δx = h, and its slope is 2x + h. Shrink h and the h left over shrinks with it, so the slope settles on 2x. At x = 3 the chord's slope is 6 + h, which is why, in every column of last lesson's table, the amount above 6 was the gap itself: a gap of 1 gave 7 and a gap of 0.5 gave 6.5. Working out a derivative is called [[differentiating]], and textbooks call doing it from chords like this working from first principles.\n\nKeep the number and the rule apart. f′(3) = 6 is one number, the slope at x = 3, while f′(x) = 2x is the whole rule, which gives a slope for any x you put in. f′(3) = 6 does not put a point at (3, 6): the curve is at (3, 9), and the 6 is how steeply it climbs there, the height of the dot on the graph below. The rule holds on both sides of zero. The plain curve y = x² falls to the left of zero as x grows, so its slopes there are below zero: f′(−2) = −4, and the dots carry on along the same line below the axis. At x = 0 the curve is flat at the bottom and f′(0) = 0. Other functions have their own derivatives. A straight line has the same slope everywhere, so its derivative is that slope at every x, a flat line on the graph below: for the steady boat, f(x) = 4x, it is 4 m per s at every moment.
