  "text": "Read in its units, a derivative is a rate on the job: so much of one thing per unit of another, at one instant. Its unit is the top graph's unit per the unit across, and that unit tells you what it measures. Take charge. An ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point each second,[^1][^2] so if 3 C pass every second, the graph of charge passed against time is a straight line climbing 3 C each second, and the current is 3 A: the slope of the charge graph, in coulombs per second. When the current changes, the same holds at each instant, using the tangent's slope: the current is the derivative of the charge that flows, with respect to time,[^3] written i = dq/dt. That is the dy/dx from lesson 2 with letters that say what is measured, q for charge and t for time; writing it this way is quite common in engineering and physics.[^4] Say the charge passed after t seconds is q = 0.5t² coulombs, an example rule. The power rule gives dq/dt = 0.5 × 2t = t, so at 4 s the current is 4 A. At that moment the charge graph stands at 0.5 × 16 = 8 C, and the chord from the start gives 8 ÷ 4 = 2 A on average. Neither is the current now: the height says how much charge has passed, the chord how fast on average, and the tangent's slope how fast at that instant.\n\nA capacitor shows why the difference matters on the job. Textbooks add that the charge on a capacitor is its capacitance times the voltage across it, q = Cv. C is fixed, and a number in front stays when you differentiate,[^5] so i = dq/dt = C dv/dt: the current through a capacitor is its capacitance times the slope of its voltage graph.[^6] The units agree: q = Cv makes a farad a coulomb per volt, so farads times volts per second are coulombs per second, which is amps. So it is not how high the voltage is that sets the current, but how fast it is changing.[^6] Make the voltage on a 0.01 F capacitor climb a steady 5 V each second and the current is 0.01 × 5 = 0.05 A, 50 mA, the whole time it climbs. Hold the voltage at 20 V and its slope is 0, so the current is 0, however high it is held. Across the same resistor, a higher voltage means more current; for a capacitor, it is the change of voltage that drives the current. Now charge a capacitor from a steady supply through a resistor. The resistor has the supply's voltage less the capacitor's across it, so at the start, with the capacitor uncharged, the most current flows, and by i = C dv/dt the voltage climbs fastest. As the capacitor's voltage climbs, less is left across the resistor, so less current flows and the voltage climbs more slowly. So the voltage climbs quickly at first, then more and more slowly as it nears the supply's voltage, and the current is largest at the start, when the voltage is lowest, and falls away towards zero as the voltage levels off. A coil, an inductor, has the same pattern with voltage and current swapped: textbooks add that the voltage across it is its inductance times how fast its current changes, v = L di/dt. A steady current makes no voltage across it, and a current that falls fast makes a large voltage, the other way round to the one a rising current makes. These capacitor and coil rules are for ideal parts, a simplified picture: a real capacitor leaks a very little current at a steady voltage, and a real coil's resistance gives a small voltage at a steady current.\n\nMotion works the same way and adds a second step. Measure displacement, s, as how far something is from a starting point along its path, plus one way and minus the other. Differentiate it with respect to time and you get its [[velocity]] at that time,[^7] its speed with a sign for its direction, in metres per second. Velocity changes too, and its rate of change, in metres per second each second, is the [[acceleration]]; the Second Engineer syllabus asks for both, velocity from displacement and acceleration from velocity.[^8] Acceleration is a derivative of a derivative, the [[second derivative]] of displacement, which textbooks write d²s/dt².
