    "text": "The mean value of a graph over a stretch is the area under it divided by the stretch's length: the height of the flat rectangle with the same area.[^1] The RMS value says how big a changing current is for heating: square the graph, take the mean of the square, then take the square root.[^2] For a sine wave that comes to the peak ÷ √2,[^3] and you can see why without any trigonometry.\n\nRemember the current i = 3t² amps from 0 s to 2 s in lesson 8: the area under it, the charge, is 8 C. A steady current moving the same 8 C in the same 2 s would be 8 ÷ 2 = 4 A, a flat rectangle 4 A high and 2 s long with the same area. That height is the [[mean value]], the definite integral divided by the length of the stretch,[^1] the same move as the mean effective pressure in the last lesson. Where the curve rises above the rectangle, the extra area exactly makes up for where it sits below, because the two shapes hold the same 8 C. The mean is not the average of the end values, (0 + 12) ÷ 2 = 6 A, because this curve spends most of the 2 s low down and only shoots up near the end; on a straight line the two do agree, which is why the slip is easy to make. With equal bars the mean is plain averaging: each bar's area is its height times the same width, so the bars' total area ÷ their total width is the average of their heights.\n\nAn a.c. current flows one way, then back. Textbooks add that the a.c. from a generator is close to a sine wave, the shape drawn here with a peak of 10 A; its trigonometry waits for a later course. Over a whole cycle the backward half has the same area as the forward half, and area below the axis counts as minus, so the mean is zero.[^6] Yet the current warms a heater both ways, so for its size the mean is taken over half a cycle: the bars settle on 6.37 A, 0.637 of the peak.[^7] Heating needs something different again. A heater's power is I²R, so its heat goes with the square of the current: twice the current gives four times the heat, and a backward current heats just as well, because a minus times a minus is a plus. So the steady current that heats the same is the one whose square equals the mean of the squared current: square the graph, find its mean, then take the square root.[^2] That is the [[RMS value]], short for root mean square: the steady direct current that would give a circuit the same energy.[^8] Take a ramp, i = 3t amps from 0 s to 2 s, rising to 6 A and dropping back. Its mean is its triangle's 6 C ÷ 2 s = 3 A. Its square is 9t², which the reverse rule turns into 3t³, so its area from 0 s to 2 s is 24 and its mean is 24 ÷ 2 = 12 A². The RMS value is √12 = 3.46 A, more than the mean, because squaring makes the big currents count for more. Any ramp from 0 up to a peak A works the same way: its square's mean is A² ÷ 3, so its RMS value is A ÷ √3, and 6 ÷ 1.732 = 3.46 A.\n\nNow square the sine wave with its 10 A peak. The square runs between 0 and 100 A², never below the axis, and repeats every half cycle. Its humps above 50 A², half the peak squared, are the same shape as its dips below 50 A², turned upside down, so each hump lifts off and drops exactly into a dip, leaving a flat band at 50 A². One way to picture why: a sine wave is the height of a point going steadily round a circle whose radius is the peak. A quarter turn later its height is what its sideways distance was, and by Pythagoras height² + sideways distance² = 10² = 100 at every moment. So the squared wave and the squared wave a quarter cycle later always add up to 100: wherever one is above 50 by some amount, the other is below 50 by the same amount. The mean of the square is therefore 50 A², and the RMS value is √50 = 7.07 A, the peak ÷ √2, about 0.707 of the peak;[^3][^4] the other way round, the peak is 1.414 × the RMS value.[^5] That is more than the half-cycle mean of 6.37 A, and textbooks add that the RMS value ÷ the mean value is called the [[form factor]]: 7.07 ÷ 6.37 = 1.11 for a sine wave, and 3.46 ÷ 3 = 1.15 for the ramp. Like the work from a pV diagram, a mean or RMS value is an area under a graph, found by bars or the reverse rule, then divided by a length: one more way the integral turns a quantity that keeps changing into a single number you can use. **The mean value is the area under a graph ÷ its length;[^1] the RMS value is the square root of the mean of the squared graph,[^2] which for a sine wave is its peak ÷ √2.[^3]**"
