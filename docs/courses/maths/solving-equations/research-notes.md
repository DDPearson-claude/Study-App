### 1.6 What is core here (benchmark summary)

Every source above, school or Brilliant, builds the same chain: equality (both sides the same) then undoing one step then two steps then unknowns on both sides. Oak puts brackets after unknowns on both sides (the roadmap has them before). Bitesize and mathcentre treat changing the subject as the same process as solving. The chain that Dan's formulas need is the first half of it. Inference: by inspection, none of V = IR, P = VI, P = I²R, p1V1 = p2V2 or the voltage-drop rule needs brackets or an unknown on both sides to be rearranged.

### 2.4 Ohm's law and power: V = IR, P = VI, P = I²R

All sourced. The useful teaching fact: the three forms are one law and one power formula, joined by substitution, so I = V/R, R = V/I, and P = I²R are rearrangements or substitutions, not new facts.

- Sheffield's HELM engineering workbook names Ohm's law v = iR as a formula engineers meet early
  - Source: Formulae and Transposition, <https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/31930/download?attachment>
  - Quote: "you may have already seen Ohm’s law, v = iR"
- Transposition is called a basic engineering skill (University of Sheffield HELM workbook)
  - Source: Formulae and Transposition, <https://sheffield.ac.uk/media/31930/download?attachment>
  - Quote: "These are basic skills required in all aspects of engineering."
- Ohm's law and its rearrangement by "trivial algebraic manipulation" (UMass Amherst open textbook)
  - Source: 2.7 Resistors and Ohm’s Law – Applied Electrical Engineering Fundamentals, <https://openbooks.library.umass.edu/funee/chapter/2-7/>
  - Quote: "Ohm’s law stipulates that the voltage drop across and current through a resistor are related by"

  - Source: 2.7 Resistors and Ohm’s Law – Applied Electrical Engineering Fundamentals, <https://openbooks.library.umass.edu/funee/chapter/2-7/>
  - Quote: "or, by trivial algebraic manipulation,"

  - Source: 2.7 Resistors and Ohm’s Law – Applied Electrical Engineering Fundamentals, <https://openbooks.library.umass.edu/funee/chapter/2-7/>

## 3. Teaching: common mistakes and good visual models

### 3.1 The equals sign read as "the answer comes next"

- Equals sign read as "the answer" is a known operational misreading
  - Source: The Importance of - Equal Sign, <https://psych.wisc.edu/alibali/home/Publications_files/Knuth_etal_MTMS_2008.pdf>
  - Quote: "A response was categorized as operational if the student expressed the general idea that the equal sign means “add the numbers” or “the answer.”"
- Operations-on-the-left equations never demand the idea of equivalence
  - Source: The Importance of - Equal Sign, <https://psych.wisc.edu/alibali/home/Publications_files/Knuth_etal_MTMS_2008.pdf>
  - Quote: "In solving such “operations equal answer” equations correctly, it is not really necessary for students to think about the equal sign as a symbol of equivalence"
- "The answer comes next" misreading: add the left and ignore the right
  - Source: Full article: We Cannot Ignore the Signs: The Development of Equivalence and Arithmetic for Students from Grades 3 to 4, <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15248372.2023.2245507>
  - Quote: "may misinterpret the equal sign as “the answer comes next,” and proceed to add the numbers on the left side of the equation and ignore the numbers on the right side"
- Equals-sign and negative-sign misconceptions both lower equation-solving success
  - Source: Key Misconceptions in Algebraic Problem Solving - PACT Center, <https://pact.cs.cmu.edu/koedinger/pubs/BoothKoedingerCogSci2008.pdf>
  - Quote: "Results indicate that students who begin the lesson with misconceptions about the meaning of the equals sign or negative signs solve fewer equations correctly"

### 3.2 Sign errors with negatives

Roadmap reference [10] ("gave rise to many errors") checks out against the ERIC record of Vlassis (2002), quoted below. Its subject is the balance model, so it is also evidence about where the balance stops.

- Negative-sign errors are the most frequent error in one-step equations
  - Source: Persistent and Pernicious Errors in Algebraic Problem Solving, <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1059995.pdf>
  - Quote: "For 1-step equations, the most frequent were negative sign errors followed by fractions errors."
- Roadmap reference [10]: in one balance-model study negative numbers gave rise to many errors
  - Source: ERIC - EJ656398 - The Balance Model: Hindrance or Support for the Solving of Linear Equations with One Unknown., Educational Studies in Mathematics, 2002, <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ656398>
  - Quote: "Shows the presence of negative numbers gave rise to many errors."
- The same study weighs researchers who defend or reject the balance model
  - Source: ERIC - EJ656398 - The Balance Model: Hindrance or Support for the Solving of Linear Equations with One Unknown., Educational Studies in Mathematics, 2002, <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ656398>
  - Quote: "Discusses the relevance of the balance model and analyzes the arguments put forward by researchers who either defend or reject its use."
- Not making sense of the minus sign blocks success in solving
  - Source: errors and misconceptions of eighth-grade students, <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1294052.pdf>
  - Quote: "As seen, not being able to make sense of the negative sign prevents the success of solving equations"

### 3.3 Inverse operations, shortcuts, one side only

Sourced here: the wrong inverse ("other inverse" error and dividing instead of multiplying at the last step), the "change side, change sign" shortcut, and the advice to undo from the last operation back (Bitesize, in 1.3). UNCONFIRMED as measured errors in any source I found: "doing an operation to one side only" and "dividing only one term". Both are the opposite of the stated rule "same thing to both sides" (Khan, Bitesize and mathcentre all state it), but no source I read counts them as common errors. Oak says that for two-step equations the order of the two steps does not change the answer, so "wrong order" is a matter of efficiency (more fractions), not correctness, for numeric equations (NCETM raises the same efficiency point with 5x - 14 = 6). For formulas, undoing from the last operation back is the safe default (Bitesize).

- "Other inverse" error: undoing with the wrong operation
  - Source: errors and misconceptions of eighth-grade students, <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1294052.pdf>
  - Quote: "This error is called the other inverse error"
- Wrong last step: reaching x/4 = 160 correctly, then dividing instead of multiplying
  - Source: errors and misconceptions of eighth-grade students, <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1294052.pdf>

  - Quote: "you just grab a number, chuck it on the other side of the equals, and it changes sign"
- The shortcut works for many equations, so it survives until it fails
  - Source: Article - Misconceptions: Solving Equations - Mr Barton Maths Podcast, <https://podcast.mrbartonmaths.com/article-misconceptions-solving-equations>
  - Quote: "The problem is, of course, that the change side, change sign method works absolutely perfectly for a great number of equations."

  - Source: Article - Misconceptions: Solving Equations - Mr Barton Maths Podcast, <https://podcast.mrbartonmaths.com/article-misconceptions-solving-equations>

### 3.5 Where the balance model stops

Negative numbers are the clear limit (LibreTexts, NCETM on tiles, Vlassis). Unknowns on both sides are handled by scales and tiles by taking matching items off both sides (NCETM tile example in 3.4; Brilliant "Balancing with Unknown Weights" in 1.1). Rearranging formulas moves from weights to letters, which is where Bitesize switches to the function machine. Inference: a lesson sequence that keeps the scales for positive numbers only, and hands over to an "undo" flow diagram at two-step equations and for all formulas, matches what these sources do.

- Balance scales have limits, especially for negative numbers
  - Source: 4.6: Solving Equations Using the Balance Scale Representations - Mathematics LibreTexts, <https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Northern_Illinois_University/Conceptual_Mathematics_in_Society/04%3A_Algebra/4.06%3A_Solving_Equations_Using_the_Balance_Scale_Representations>
  - Quote: "Using balance scales can be tricky and has some limitations. For instance, representing negative numbers with balance scales can be challenging."
- Tiles with negative terms need careful consideration and can be dropped for symbols
  - Source: Mastery materials writing frame, <https://www.ncetm.org.uk/media/dj5o223w/ncetm_ks3_representations_algebra_tilespdf.pdf>
  - Quote: "As with expanding brackets, the appropriateness of using algebra tiles when factorising expressions that include negative terms may need to be carefully considered."

## 4. My view of the core lessons

Dan has cut the last two courses to the core. The roadmap's nine lessons shrink to six, because the core is one idea (do the same to both sides, undo in reverse) applied to equations, then to the formulas college uses.

1. **The balance and one-step equations.** Equality first, then undoing one step. Predict, then play, with scales (Brilliant, Oak and Khan open this way). Numbers only positive, so the model does not break. Merges roadmap lessons 1 and 2.
2. **Two-step equations: undo in reverse.** The function machine / flow diagram arrives here and stays for the rest of the course. Uses order of operations from Negative numbers lesson 8 (the undo order is the build order reversed). Negatives are allowed now, handed to the flow diagram and the number line, since the balance is poor at them. This is roadmap lesson 3.
3. **Unknowns on both sides.** Take the same unknown off both sides (scales, matching items off each side). Short. Brackets fold in as the last two or three screens, or move to Linear equations (Brilliant's Linear Equations course repeats this ground and the roadmap already plans such a course). Roadmap lessons 4 and 5. If Dan wants five lessons, this is the one to cut to a single puzzle set inside lesson 2, because none of his sourced formulas needs it (Inference).
4. **From a situation to an equation, and using formulas.** Write an equation from data (the GES I line "Form linear equations consistent with data provided"), then put numbers into V = IR, P = VI, P = I²R and solve for the unknown number. Roadmap lessons 6 and 7. This is the bridge from numbers to letters.
5. **Rearranging formulas I.** Make a different letter the subject by undoing a function machine drawn from the formula: V = IR to I = V/R, P = VI to I = P/V, and one sum form (for example v = u + at style, or E = V + Ir). This is the skill SQA assumes on entry. Roadmap lesson 8.
6. **Rearranging formulas II.** P = I²R to I = the square root of P/R, with the square-root warning ("squaring both sides may introduce extra solutions", HELM); p1V1 = p2V2 to V2 = p1V1/p2 with absolute pressures (the Negative numbers lesson 3 link); the voltage-drop rule solved for the longest run only once its source is confirmed (UNCONFIRMED, see 2.6), or derived from the unit as the lesson's own rule. Roadmap lesson 9.
