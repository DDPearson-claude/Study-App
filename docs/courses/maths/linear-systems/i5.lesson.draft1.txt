    "text": "A circuit with two loops has two unknown currents, and Kirchhoff's two laws turn it into two equations for them, which you solve as in Elimination. Take two batteries connected side by side to one load, each with a resistance of its own inside it.[^8] That is its [[internal resistance]], and its drop, current × resistance, takes more off the voltage at the battery's terminals as the current rises.[^7] Say, as example values, battery 1 is 12 V with 1 Ω inside, battery 2 is 12 V with 2 Ω inside, and the load is 2 Ω. Battery 1 sends a current I₁, battery 2 a current I₂, and the question is how big each one is.\n\nThe first law is about currents. At a junction, the currents flowing in add up to the currents flowing out.[^1] This is the [[junction law]]. At the top junction I₁ and I₂ flow in and the load's current I₃ flows out, so I₃ = I₁ + I₂. Write I₁ + I₂ wherever the load's current appears, which is Substitution, and only two unknowns are left. The junction at the bottom gives the same equation again, so it adds nothing new.[^4]\n\nThe second law is about voltages. Walk once round a loop, adding each rise and each drop of voltage as you pass it, and when you are back where you started the total is zero.[^2] This is the [[loop law]]. One way to picture it: each point in the circuit sits at one voltage level, like a height on a hill, so a walk that ends where it began has dropped exactly as much as it has climbed. Across a resistance the drop is I × R, because the current through it is proportional to the voltage drop across it.[^6] Textbooks add the signs for the walk: crossing a battery from − to + is a rise of its voltage, and crossing a resistance in the direction of its current arrow is a drop of I × R, which counts as a rise when you walk against the arrow. Walk loop 1, through battery 1 and the load: 12 − 1 × I₁ − 2 × (I₁ + I₂) = 0, which tidies to 3I₁ + 2I₂ = 12. It is the volt drops round a circuit adding up to the supply, written for one loop. A guess shows what the equation does: with I₁ = 1 A and I₂ = 1 A the walk ends at 12 − 1 − 4 = 7 V, not 0, so that pair is wrong. Loop 2, through battery 2 and the load, gives 12 − 2 × I₂ − 2 × (I₁ + I₂) = 0, which tidies to 2I₁ + 4I₂ = 12.\n\nEach law you apply gives one equation, and as many independent equations as unknowns is enough to solve them.[^3] Double loop 1 to get 6I₁ + 4I₂ = 24, and take loop 2 away: 4I₁ = 12, so I₁ = 3 A. Put that into loop 2: 6 + 4I₂ = 12, so I₂ = 1.5 A, and the load carries I₃ = 4.5 A. Walk each loop with these currents and both end at 0: 12 − 3 − 9 = 0 each time. On a grid with I₁ across and I₂ up, each loop's equation is a straight line, and the answer is where the two cross, as in Where two lines meet. Battery 1 always gives more current because it is the better battery. You guess each current's direction when you draw its arrow, and a wrong guess just comes out with a minus sign, meaning that current flows the other way.[^5] **The junction law joins the currents and each loop gives one equation from the loop law, so a two-loop circuit gives two equations in two unknown currents,[^3] which you solve together.**"

Add a battery's voltage when you cross it from − to +, take it away from + to −. Take away I × R when you cross a resistance along its arrow, add it when you cross against the arrow. Set the total to 0.\n- Tidy each loop into the form aI₁ + bI₂ = c.\n- Solve the pair by substitution or elimination.\n- A minus answer means that current flows against its arrow. Keep the minus in all later working.\n- Check: walk each loop again with your answers. Each should end at 0, and the junction should balance.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** one equation for each unknown. The junction law turns three currents into two unknowns, and two loops give the two equations.\n\n**Worked example:** with example values, a 12 V battery and a 9 V battery, each with 1 Ω inside, feed a 1 Ω load.
