Draw each wave's turning arrow, lay the second arrow's tail on the first arrow's tip, and the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second is the sum's arrow: its length is the sum's peak and its angle is the sum's phase.\n\nRemember From circle to wave: a wave's height at any instant is the height of its turning arrow's tip. Lay arrow B's tail on arrow A's tip, and B's tip sits B's height above A's tip, so the end of the chain is at A's height plus B's height. The sum arrow, from the centre to that end, therefore has the two heights added at every instant: the sum of the up parts of the separate arrows is the up part of the sum arrow.[^5] Both arrows turn at the same speed, so the angle between them never changes, and the chain turns as one rigid shape, like a bent bar spun about one end. The sum arrow keeps its length and turns at the same speed, so its height traces a sine wave of the same frequency, which is why phasors are good for summing sine waves of one frequency.[^6] Textbooks call each turning arrow a [[phasor]] and draw it frozen at the instant t = 0, because the whole picture just turns from there.\n\nTo measure the sum arrow, split each arrow as you split a force in Finding angles: the cosine gives the floor shadow and the sine the wall shadow,[^7] so an arrow of length E at angle θ has floor shadow E cos θ and wall shadow E sin θ.
