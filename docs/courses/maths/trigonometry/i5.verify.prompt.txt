The angle is the turn between where the arm starts and where it stops, as in Round the circle. Lift the arm off as a bendy rod, exactly one radius long, and lay it round the edge from where the arm starts, turning anticlockwise. The angle it spans at the centre is one radian.[^1] Now make the circle twice as big. The radius doubles, so the rod doubles. A circle twice as big is the same shape drawn at twice the scale, as lesson 1's triangles were, so every length in it doubles, the arc between the same two arms as well as the radius. Rod and arc grow together, so the rod still spans the same angle. That is why an angle's arc keeps the same relation to the radius whatever the radius is,[^3] and why the angle in radians can be worked out as arc ÷ radius. Both are lengths, so their units cancel. The radian is a unit of angle, as the degree is, but its figure is one length over another, so it carries no metres, and the word \"radians\" is often left off.\n\nNow lay the rod all the way round. The edge is the circumference, 2πr from Geometry fundamentals, so it holds 2πr ÷ r = 2π rods: six whole rods and 0.28 of a seventh. A full turn is 360°, so 360° = 2π radians, and halving both, 180° = π radians.[^2] One radian is therefore 180° ÷ π, about 57.3°, a slice a little under a sixth of the circle, not the whole circle. To turn degrees into radians multiply by π ÷ 180, and to turn radians into degrees multiply by 180 ÷ π.
