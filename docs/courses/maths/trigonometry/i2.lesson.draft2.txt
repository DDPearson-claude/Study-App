Which angle has a sine of 0.5? The calculator's sin⁻¹ key answers that: sin⁻¹ 0.5 = 30°. It is read [[inverse sine]], because it undoes sin. An inverse takes the output and does the opposite to get back to the input:[^1] sin turns 30° into 0.5, and sin⁻¹ turns 0.5 back into 30°. cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ undo cos and tan in the same way. Choose the key as you chose the ratio in lesson 1, from the two sides you have: sine is opposite over hypotenuse, cosine adjacent over hypotenuse, and tangent opposite over adjacent,[^5] so those pairs take sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹. A slope is the last kind. As in Straight-line graphs, its gradient m is rise ÷ run. The rise is the side opposite the slope's angle and the run is the side next to it, so tan θ = m, and the line y = 0.5x, drawn with the same scale on both axes, climbs at tan⁻¹ 0.5 = 26.6°. In a right-angled triangle θ is between 0° and 90°, and that is the angle these keys give; a later lesson finds a second angle with the same sine. You need an inverse key only when you want an angle. A side comes from plain sin, cos or tan and a rearrangement, as in lesson 1.\n\nOn sin, cos and tan, ⁻¹ means undo. That is not how Powers and roots read it, where 2⁻¹ is 1 ÷ 2, so it is worth saying twice: sin⁻¹ 0.5 is an angle, 30°, while 1 ÷ sin 30° is a number, 2.0. One over a ratio has a name of its own. 1 ÷ sin θ is the [[cosecant]] of θ, written cosec θ, and it is lesson 1's conduit multiplier, 2.0 at 30° and 1.414 at 45°.[^9] Textbooks add that 1 ÷ cos θ is the secant, sec θ, and 1 ÷ tan θ the cotangent, cot θ. A sine is opposite ÷ hypotenuse, and the opposite side is never longer than the hypotenuse, so no angle has a sine above 1, and sin⁻¹ 45 gives an error. Two triangles give exact ratios without a calculator. Cut a square with sides of 1 from corner to corner and each half has two sides of 1, a 45° angle, and by Pythagoras a hypotenuse of √2, so sin 45° = cos 45° = 1/√2, which is 0.7071.[^9] An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and three equal angles, and the angles make 180°, so each is 60°. Take one with sides of 2 and cut it down the middle. The cut halves the top angle into two of 30° and the base into two of 1, so each half has a hypotenuse of 2, a base of 1, and a height of √(4 − 1) = √3. So sin 30° = 1/2, cos 30° = √3/2, which is 0.866, and the two swap for 60°.\n\nA force has a size and a direction, so draw it as an arrow: its length is the size and its tilt the direction. Stand it at θ above the floor, and its shadow on the floor and its shadow on the wall are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle, with the arrow as the hypotenuse. The floor shadow is next to θ, so it is F cos θ, and the wall shadow is opposite θ, so it is F sin θ. For a force at an angle above the horizontal, the cosine gives the horizontal part and the sine the vertical part.[^2] A 100 N force at 30° has 100 × cos 30° = 86.6 N along the floor and 100 × sin 30° = 50.0 N up the wall.[^3] These two parts are the force's [[components]], and textbooks add that the two together push exactly as the one force does. Turn the arrow up and the floor shadow shortens while the wall shadow grows, as more of the arrow's length goes into its height. Resolving a force into horizontal and vertical components, for angles from 0° to 90°, is in the MCA's General Engineering Science syllabus.[^4] Textbooks split a launch speed at an angle the same way, into a speed across and a speed up, which is the launch Quadratics and polynomials left for here.
