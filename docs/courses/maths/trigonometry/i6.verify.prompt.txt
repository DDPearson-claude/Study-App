  "text": "Set lesson 3's arm turning at a steady speed, carry its tip's height across to a graph of height against time, and the trace is a sine wave, the shape of an AC supply's voltage: the arm's length sets how tall it is, its turns a second set how many waves fit in a second, and the angle it starts from sets where the wave starts.\n\nRemember Round the circle: an arm of length 1 has its tip at (cos θ, sin θ), so its height is sin θ.[^2] An arm of length E is the same picture drawn E times bigger, so its tip's height is E sin θ. Turning at a steady speed means the angle grows by the same amount every second, so equal steps of time are equal steps of angle, and the height plotted against time takes the same shape as sin θ plotted against the angle: up to E at a quarter turn, back to 0 at a half turn, down to −E at three quarters, and 0 again at a full turn, where it starts over. So a sine wave can be thought of as the height of an arrow turning at a constant speed, like a clock's second hand, whose length is the wave's height, its [[amplitude]].[^3] For an AC voltage, the up part of the turning arrow gives the voltage at each instant.[^4]\n\nOne turn draws one whole wave, so the turns a second are the waves a second: the [[frequency]], the number of cycles in one second,[^5] counted in hertz. UK low-voltage supplies are declared at 50 hertz unless otherwise agreed in writing,[^1] so each wave takes 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 seconds, 20 ms, a time called the period. Radians count the same turning in radius-lengths: one turn, 360°, is 2π radians,[^6] so f turns a second is 2πf radians a second, the arm's angular speed ω. After t seconds the arm has turned ωt radians from pointing right, so its height is E sin(ωt) = E sin(2πft).[^7] At 50 Hz, ω = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s. Because ωt is in radians, the calculator goes in radian mode.\n\nIf the arm starts at an angle φ instead of pointing right, it has a head start: after t seconds it is at ωt + φ, so the wave is E sin(ωt + φ).
