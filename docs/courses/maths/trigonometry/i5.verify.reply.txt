A [[radian]] measures turning with the circle's own ruler instead: it is the angle at the centre whose [[arc]], the stretch of edge between the two arms, is one radius long.[^1] So an angle in radians is a count of radius-lengths round the edge, the same count on any circle, and a full turn is 2π of them, about 6.28.[^2]\n\nPicture lesson 3's turning arm, now on a circle of any size. The angle is the turn between where the arm starts and where it stops, as in Round the circle. Lift the arm off as a bendy rod, exactly one radius long, and lay it round the edge from where the arm starts, turning anticlockwise. The angle it spans at the centre is one radian.[^1] Now make the circle twice as big. The radius doubles, so the rod doubles. A circle twice as big is the same shape drawn at twice the scale, as lesson 1's triangles were, so every length in it doubles, the arc between the same two arms as well as the radius. Rod and arc grow together, so the rod still spans the same angle. That is why an angle's arc keeps the same relation to the radius whatever the radius is,[^3] and why the angle in radians can be worked out as arc ÷ radius. Both are lengths, so their units cancel. The radian is a unit of angle, as the degree is, but its figure is one length over another, so it carries no metres, and the word \"radians\" is often left off.\n\nNow lay the rod all the way round. The edge is the circumference, 2πr from Geometry fundamentals, so it holds 2πr ÷ r = 2π rods: six whole rods and 0.28 of a seventh. A full turn is 360°, so 360° = 2π radians, and halving both, 180° = π radians.[^2] One radian is therefore 180° ÷ π, about 57.3°, a slice a little under a sixth of the circle, not the whole circle. To turn degrees into radians multiply by π ÷ 180, and to turn radians into degrees multiply by 180 ÷ π. The arc follows from the definition: each radian of angle holds one radius of edge, so θ radians on a circle of radius r hold an arc s = rθ, with θ in radians. Textbooks add that an angle written with no degree sign, as in cos 10, is taken to be in radians, so the calculator must be in radian mode: cos 10 is −0.839 for 10 radians and 0.985 for 10°.\n\nTurning speed is counted the same way. A shaft turning N times a minute turns through N × 2π radians a minute, and a minute is 60 seconds, so its [[angular speed]], ω, is N × 2π ÷ 60 radians a second. At 400 rev/min that is 41.9 rad/s. Textbooks add that a shaft's power in watts is its torque in newton metres times ω in radians a second, and that calculus with sine and cosine comes out simplest with angles in radians, which is one reason engineering formulas use them.
