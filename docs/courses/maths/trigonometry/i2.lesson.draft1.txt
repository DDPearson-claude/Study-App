    "text": "Lesson 1 went from an angle to a side. This lesson runs it backwards, from two sides to the angle, and then uses the same triangle to split a force into two parts. Remember the 150 mm offset with 300 mm between the bends. The offset depth is the opposite side and the distance between bends the hypotenuse,[^5] so sin θ = 150 ÷ 300 = 0.5. Which angle has a sine of 0.5? The calculator's sin⁻¹ key answers that: sin⁻¹ 0.5 = 30°. It is read [[inverse sine]], because it undoes sin. An inverse takes the output and does the opposite to get back to the input:[^1] sin turns 30° into 0.5, and sin⁻¹ turns 0.5 back into 30°. cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ undo cos and tan in the same way. Choose the key as you chose the ratio, from the two sides you have: opposite and hypotenuse take sin⁻¹, adjacent and hypotenuse cos⁻¹, opposite and adjacent tan⁻¹.[^4] A slope is the last kind. As in Straight-line graphs, its gradient m is rise ÷ run. The rise is the side opposite the slope's angle and the run is the side next to it, so tan θ = m, and the line y = 0.5x climbs at tan⁻¹ 0.5 = 26.6°. In a right-angled triangle θ is between 0° and 90°, and that is the angle these keys give; a later lesson finds a second angle with the same sine. You need an inverse key only when you want an angle. A side comes from plain sin, cos or tan and a rearrangement, as in lesson 1.\n\nThe ⁻¹ means undo, not one over. One over a ratio has a name of its own. 1 ÷ sin θ is the [[cosecant]] of θ, written cosec θ, and it is lesson 1's conduit multiplier, 2.0 at 30° and 1.414 at 45°.[^6] Textbooks add that 1 ÷ cos θ is the secant, sec θ, and 1 ÷ tan θ the cotangent, cot θ. So sin⁻¹ 0.5 is an angle, 30°, while 1 ÷ sin 30° is a number, 2.0. Two triangles give exact ratios without a calculator. Cut a square with sides of 1 from corner to corner and each half has two sides of 1, a 45° angle, and by Pythagoras a hypotenuse of √2, so sin 45° = cos 45° = 1/√2, which is 0.7071.[^6] An equilateral triangle with sides of 2 has three equal angles of 180° ÷ 3 = 60°. Cut it down the middle and each half has a hypotenuse of 2, a base of 1, angles of 60° and 30°, and a height of √(4 − 1) = √3. So sin 30° = 1/2, cos 30° = √3/2, which is 0.866, and the two swap for 60°.\n\nA force has a size and a direction, so draw it as an arrow: its length is the size and its tilt the direction. Stand it at θ above the floor, and its shadow on the floor and its shadow on the wall are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle, with the arrow as the hypotenuse. The floor shadow is next to θ, so it is F cos θ, and the wall shadow is opposite θ, so it is F sin θ. For a force at an angle above the horizontal, the cosine gives the horizontal part and the sine the vertical part.[^2] A 100 N force at 30° has 100 × cos 30° = 86.6 N along the floor and 100 × sin 30° = 50.0 N up the wall.[^3] These two parts are the force's [[components]], and textbooks add that the two together push exactly as the one force does. Turn the arrow up and the floor shadow shortens while the wall shadow grows, as more of the arrow's length goes into its height. Resolving a force into horizontal and vertical components, for angles from 0° to 90°, is in the MCA's General Engineering Science syllabus.[^7] Run it backwards and the shadows give the angle: tan θ = wall ÷ floor, so tan⁻¹(50 ÷ 86.6) = 30°, and Pythagoras gives the arrow back, √(86.6² + 50²) = 100 N. Tilt the floor into a slope at angle α and the same split works along the slope and square to it. A block's weight W still points straight down. Tilting the floor by α tilts the line square to it by α as well, so the weight is α away from that line. Its part pressing into the slope is the side next to α, W cos α, and its part pulling down the slope is the side opposite, W sin α: for 500 N on 20°, 469.8 N and 171.0 N.\n\nWhen a second step uses the angle, keep the whole angle in the calculator.
