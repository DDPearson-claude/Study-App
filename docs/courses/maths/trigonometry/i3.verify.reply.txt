Textbooks measure the angle θ anticlockwise from an arm pointing right, along the across axis, and a full turn is 360°.[^1] Lay lesson 1's triangle under the arm with θ at the centre. The arm is the hypotenuse, the upright drop from the tip is the side opposite θ, and the stretch along the across axis is the side next to it. Sine is opposite ÷ hypotenuse and cosine adjacent ÷ hypotenuse,[^2] and here the hypotenuse is 1, so sin θ is simply the tip's height and cos θ is how far across the tip is. So the tip sits at the point (cos θ, sin θ):[^3] cosine across, sine up. At 30° that is (0.866, 0.5). A circle of radius 1 used this way is called the [[unit circle]].\n\nNow turn the arm past 90° and keep the same rule: cos θ is the tip's across coordinate and sin θ its up coordinate, signs and all. Between 0° and 90° this gives lesson 1's values, and it needs no triangle to work, so beyond 90° it is how sine and cosine are defined, and it is what the calculator works out. At 150° the tip is 0.866 left of the centre and 0.5 up, so cos 150° = −0.866 and sin 150° = 0.5. The axes cut the circle into four quarters, called [[quadrant]]s, which textbooks number anticlockwise from the top right. In the first quadrant the tip is right and up, so both are positive. In the second it is left, so the cosine is negative. In the third it is left and down, so both are negative. In the fourth it is down, so the sine is negative. That is why sine, cosine and tangent are positive or negative depending on the quadrant.[^4] Tangent was opposite ÷ adjacent, and on the arm those sides are sin θ and cos θ, so tan θ = sin θ ÷ cos θ: the arm's slope, rise over run. It is positive where sine and cosine share a sign, in the first and third quadrants, and at 90° the run is 0, so tan 90° has no value, like the gradient of an upright line in Straight-line graphs. Textbooks add that an angle between 180° and 360° is called a reflex angle; the arm treats it like any other.\n\nTwo more facts come straight from the picture. Squaring takes the signs away, so it holds in every quadrant: at 150°, 0.75 + 0.25 = 1. On an axis one is 0 and the other 1 or −1, so it holds there too.
