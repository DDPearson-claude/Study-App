Roll a circle once and the track it leaves, the [[circumference]], is just over 3 diameters long, a number called π, about 3.14. Bitesize gives it as C = πd,[^1] and since d = 2r, that is also C = 2πr. For the area, cut the circle into thin wedges and lay them top to tail. NCTM's lesson notes that the rough rectangle they make is half the circumference long, or πr,[^2] and it stands one radius tall, so the area is πr × r, which is πr².\n\nThe area rule needs the radius, so halve a diameter first; for the way round, use πd with a diameter or 2πr with a radius. NCTM lists difficulty using C, d and r,[^3] and the slip costs a lot. A solid round wire 2 mm across has r = 1 mm, so its cross-section is π × 1² = 3.14 mm², but π × 2² gives 12.57 mm², four times too much. Because the radius is squared, a solid round wire twice as wide across has four times the cross-section, as the Bureau of Standards' copper wire tables put it, cross section varies with the square of the diameter.[^4] A ring, or [[annulus]], such as a pipe's end, is the whole circle less the hole, so work out each with πr² before taking one from the other. To go back from an area to a width, divide by π, take the square root to get r, then double it.\n\n**Circumference is πd, or 2πr from the radius, and area is πr², which needs the radius, so halve a diameter first.
