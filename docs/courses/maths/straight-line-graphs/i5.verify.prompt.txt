  "text": "When readings from a test fall on a straight line, the line's rule is a [[law]] for what you measured, and its two numbers are real quantities with units. The gradient is how fast one quantity changes with the other, and the intercept is how much there is when the other is 0. In many materials, how strongly the material resists current changes with temperature in approximately a straight line.[^1] So take an example motor winding read at 20, 40, 60 and 80 °C, giving 10.8, 11.6, 12.4 and 13.2 Ω. These made-up readings sit exactly on a line, an ideal case; real readings scatter a little, and laying a line through them is the next lesson.\n\nPut a step triangle on the line from 20 °C to 60 °C. Its run is 40 °C and its rise is 1.6 Ω, so the gradient is 1.6 Ω ÷ 40 °C = 0.04 Ω per °C.[^2] The rise is in ohms and the run in degrees, so dividing one by the other gives ohms per degree: a gradient's unit is y's unit per x's unit. Stretch the triangle to 80 °C and the run is 60 °C and the rise 2.4 Ω, still 0.04 Ω per °C, because a straight line climbs the same amount for each 1 along. So the winding gains 0.04 Ω for every degree it warms.\n\nThe intercept is the value of R where the line crosses the R axis,[^3] where T is 0. In R = 0.04T + c the 0.04T part is 0 there, which leaves c, so c is R at 0 °C, in ohms. Carry the line back 20 °C from the first reading: 10.8 − 0.04 × 20 = 10.0 Ω. The law is R = 0.04T + 10. 0 °C lies below the readings, so the 10 Ω trusts the line to stay straight down there. Engineering syllabuses write the same law as y = ax + b, with a the gradient and b the intercept.[^4][^5] Each reading's ohms include that 10 Ω, so one reading divided by its temperature is not the gradient: 10.8 Ω ÷ 20 °C = 0.54 and 13.2 Ω ÷ 80 °C = 0.165, a different answer at each reading. That shortcut works only for a line through the origin, where c is 0. Use the law both ways: at 50 °C, R = 0.04 × 50 + 10 = 12.0 Ω, and a reading of 12.8 Ω means 12.8 − 10 = 0.04T, so T = 2.8 ÷ 0.04 = 70 °C.\n\nHeight and steepness are different things. A winding with R = 0.02T + 12 sits above one with R = 0.05T + 8 everywhere from 0 to 100 °C, yet the lower one gains 0.05 Ω for each degree, 2.5 times as fast. The same reading works on other laws. Plot a resistor's voltage up the side against its current along, and the gradient is its resistance,[^6] in volts per amp.[^7] Example readings of 6 V at 0.5 A and 24 V at 2 A give 18 V ÷ 1.5 A = 12 Ω. Swap the axes, current up the side, and the gradient flips to 1.5 A ÷ 18 V, about 0.083 A per V, which is not the resistance, so read the axis labels before you name a gradient. With force up the side and a spring's extension along, the gradient gives the spring constant,[^8] the force for each millimetre of stretch: 10 N at 4 mm and 30 N at 12 mm give 20 N ÷ 8 mm = 2.5 N per mm. **A straight-line law's gradient is how fast y changes with x, in y's unit per x's unit, and its intercept is the value of y when x is 0, in y's unit.[^5]**"
