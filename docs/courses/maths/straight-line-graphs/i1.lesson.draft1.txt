    "text": "A straight line's [[gradient]], m, is how much y changes for each 1 you go along. To find it from two points on the line, divide the difference in their y-coordinates by the difference in their x-coordinates.[^1] Picture a step triangle between the two points. Its [[run]] is the arrow straight across, x₂ − x₁, and its [[rise]] is the arrow up or down, y₂ − y₁. For A (1, 3) and B (4, 9), the run is 4 − 1 = 3 and the rise is 9 − 3 = 6, so m = 6 ÷ 3 = 2. The line climbs 2 for each 1 along.\n\nAny two points on the same line give the same m. A straight line climbs the same amount for each 1 along wherever you are on it. A run of 3 is three steps of 1, so its rise is three of those climbs, and dividing by 3 gives back the climb for one step. Slide A and B along the line to (2, 5) and (3, 7), and the triangle shrinks to a run of 1 and a rise of 2, which is m itself. The gradient is always a measure of how steep the line is.[^2]\n\nThe arrows' directions give the sign. Going from A to B, the run is positive when B is to the right, and the rise is negative when B is lower. So a line that falls from left to right has a negative gradient. For A (1, 7) and B (4, 1), m = (1 − 7) ÷ (4 − 1) = −6 ÷ 3 = −2. Put brackets round a negative coordinate, because taking away a negative adds. From (−1, −3) to (2, 3), m = (3 − (−3)) ÷ (2 − (−1)) = 6 ÷ 3 = 2. A level line has a rise of 0, so its gradient is 0. A line straight up has a run of 0, and nothing can be divided by 0, so its gradient is undefined.[^3]\n\nOn a real graph the rise and run carry the axes' units, so m is in y's unit per x's unit. For a resistor, a plot of voltage against current is approximately a straight line, and its slope is the resistance.[^5] Resistance is in volts per amp,[^4] so example readings of 10 V at 2 A and 25 V at 5 A give 15 V ÷ 3 A = 5 Ω.
