    "text": "When readings from a test fall on a straight line, the line's rule is a [[law]] for what you measured, and its two numbers are real quantities with units. Take an example winding read at 20, 40, 60 and 80 °C, giving 10.8, 11.6, 12.4 and 13.2 Ω. In many materials the dependence on temperature is approximately a straight line,[^3] and these example readings sit exactly on one.\n\nPut a step triangle on the line from 20 °C to 60 °C. Its run is 40 °C and its rise is 1.6 Ω, so the gradient is 1.6 Ω ÷ 40 °C = 0.04 Ω per °C.[^12] The units divide just as the numbers do, so the gradient is in Ω per °C. Stretch the triangle to 80 °C and the run is 60 °C and the rise 2.4 Ω, still 0.04 Ω per °C. So the winding gains 0.04 Ω for every degree it warms.\n\nThe intercept is R at 0 °C.[^7] Carry the line back 20 °C from the first reading: 10.8 − 0.04 × 20 = 10.0 Ω. The law is R = 0.04T + 10. Engineering syllabuses write it y = ax + b, with a the gradient and b the intercept.[^5][^9] Use it both ways: at 50 °C, R = 0.04 × 50 + 10 = 12.0 Ω, and a reading of 12.8 Ω means T = 2.8 ÷ 0.04 = 70 °C.\n\nPlot a resistor's voltage against current and the gradient is the resistance,[^1] in volts per amp.[^2] With force against a spring's extension the gradient is the spring constant.[^4] **A straight-line law's gradient is how fast y changes, in y's unit per x's unit, and its intercept is y when x is 0, in y's unit.[^9]**"
