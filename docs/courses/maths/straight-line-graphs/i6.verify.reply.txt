This lesson turns that line into a law. Lay it so about as many readings sit above it as below, even if it passes through none of them,[^1] then take m and c from the line itself, not from the readings.\n\nHow strongly many materials resist current changes with temperature in approximately a straight line.[^2] Take eight example readings of a winding, one every 10 °C from 10 °C to 80 °C, rising from 10.5 Ω to 13.3 Ω. Each one strays a little from the trend, some up and some down, so a line through their middle follows the trend and not any one reading's stray. Draw a thin stick straight up or down from each reading to the line. The line R = 0.04T + 10 leaves four above and four below, and no stick is longer than 0.2 Ω. Take its gradient from two points on the line far apart: 10.8 Ω at 20 °C and 12.8 Ω at 70 °C give 2.0 Ω ÷ 50 °C = 0.04 Ω per °C. Far apart matters because you can read a height off a line only so closely. Say you read each one to within 0.1 Ω. Then a rise of 2.0 Ω could come out anywhere from 1.8 to 2.2 Ω, a gradient of 0.036 to 0.044, while between 40 and 50 °C the rise of 0.4 Ω could come out from 0.2 to 0.6 Ω, a gradient anywhere from 0.02 to 0.06. Two readings are worse still, because each brings its own stray: 10.6 Ω at 20 °C and 11.4 Ω at 30 °C give 0.8 Ω ÷ 10 °C = 0.08 Ω per °C, twice the line's. Then c comes from one point on the line, as in lesson 4: 10.8 − 0.04 × 20 = 10.0 Ω. So the law is R = 0.04T + 10, with 10.0 Ω the line carried back to 0 °C below the readings. The first reading, 10.5 Ω, is not c, because it was taken at 10 °C and has its own stray.\n\nCounting is a check, not the whole rule. Pin the line through (0, 0) and it can only swing about that point. Swung to R = 0.26T it still leaves four readings above and four below, yet the sticks grow to 7.9 Ω at 10 °C and 7.5 Ω at 80 °C, and all four above sit on the left while all four below sit on the right. A pattern like that means the line is tilted, and here the pin is to blame: the winding still has resistance at 0 °C, so its line has no reason to pass through zero. Pin a line through zero only when the physics says y is 0 when x is 0. An ordinary spring is such a case, since with no force there is no stretch; one wound with its coils pressed tight needs a pull before it stretches at all, so its line misses zero. Up to its [[limit of proportionality]], a graph of force against extension is a straight line through the origin.[^3] Example readings of a spring, from 5.2 N at 2 mm to 29.9 N at 12 mm, scatter round F = 2.5x, three above and three below, and its gradient, 30 N ÷ 12 mm = 2.5 N per mm, is the spring constant.[^4] Past 12 mm these example readings bend away below the line, to 33.2 N at 14 mm and 35.0 N at 16 mm, so fit the straight part only. Those last two alone give 1.8 N ÷ 2 mm = 0.9 N per mm, which is not the spring constant.\n\nThat is how the course's puzzle is answered: plot the winding's readings, lay one line through their middle, and read its two numbers as ohms per degree and ohms at 0 °C. The readings here are made-up examples and the line is laid by eye, a simplified picture: two careful people lay slightly different lines, and textbooks add that a method called least squares calculates the line instead. **Lay one straight line through the middle of the readings, about as many above as below,[^1] with short sticks spread along it, take its gradient from two points on the line far apart, and pin it through zero only when the physics says so.**"
