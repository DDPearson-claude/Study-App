Random error throws each reading a different way, so averaging repeats shrinks it. A steady offset throws every reading the same way, so averaging leaves it exactly where it was. Say a charger holds a battery bank at 27.00 V, and each reading has a random error of 0.04 V, its standard deviation. Most single readings land within about 0.08 V of the true value. Now make each reading the mean of four. Their random errors point different ways, so they partly cancel. They are random, so they rarely cancel fully. When each reading's random error is independent of the others, the spread of a mean of n readings is the spread of one reading divided by √n.[^3] Four readings halve it, to 0.02 V. Two readings cut it only to about 0.028 V. So to halve the spread again you need four times as many readings, not twice as many.\n\nThe same rule says how well the mean of fifty readings is pinned down. Divide the 0.04 V spread by √50, about 7.07, and double it. That gives about 0.011 V. Textbooks add that this doubled bracket, the mean ± 2 × the standard deviation ÷ √n, is the usual one. It catches where the meter centres about 95% of the time. A stated [[uncertainty]] needs both parts: a width and how confident you are, such as 20 cm ± 1 cm at 95%.[^1] So fifty readings give a result of 27.00 ± 0.011 V. That bracket is far narrower than the 0.08 V either side where most single readings land. The result says how well the mean is known. The scatter says how far one reading wanders.\n\nNow say the meter reads 0.02 V high. The same influence lands on every reading, so the whole pile shifts across. That is a [[systematic error]].
