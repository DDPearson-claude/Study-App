    "text": "Equipment does not fail on a timetable. Each unit fails at its own random time. A [[failure rate]] says how often, as failures each year for each unit still working. For most of a product's useful life, if all goes well, that rate stays roughly steady.[^2] A steady rate sets the share of units still working at any age. It also means far fewer reach the average lifetime than you might expect.\n\nHere is why. Call the rate λ. Over a short spell, every working unit has the same small chance of failing, λ times the spell. So the stock loses the same fraction of what is left in every spell. Losing the same fraction each step gives the decay curve you met with a capacitor discharging. The share still working is the [[reliability]], R = e^(−λt). One over the rate, 1 ÷ λ, is the mean time to failure, usually called the [[MTBF]] in power work.[^3] Put t = 1 ÷ λ into the rule, and R = e^(−1), about 0.37. Most units fail before the mean, because a few long-lived ones pull it up. At 0.1 a year the MTBF is 10 years, yet half have failed by about 7.\n\nTwo units together follow two rules, if each fails on its own. In series, the pair works only while both work. So its R is R × R, and the failure rates add. In parallel, the pair works while at least one works. It fails only if both fail, with chance (1 − R) × (1 − R). So its R is 1 − (1 − R)². A part the two share, such as one supply, can stop both at once. Then the parallel rule promises too much.\n\nReal units are not steady for their whole lives. Units with hidden faults fail early, then the rate settles low, and late on wear-out makes it climb. That shape is called the bathtub curve, and the steady model fits only its flat middle. There a failure says nothing about age, while a climbing rate is wear. It is the same question as a Zs reading 0.02 Ω higher than last year: wear, or chance?\n\n**With a steady failure rate λ, the share of units still working after a time t is e^(−λt), so only about 37% reach the MTBF of 1 ÷ λ.[^4]**"

At half the MTBF about 61% are, and at a tenth of it about 90%.\n\n**Worked example:** take the example UPS, with failure rates of 20, 10 and 20 per million hours in series.
