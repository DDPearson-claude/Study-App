    "text": "No single reading is the true value. Every reading carries two kinds of error, and they behave differently when you repeat. Random error throws each reading a different way, so averaging repeats shrinks it. A steady offset throws every reading the same way, so averaging leaves it exactly where it was. Say a charger holds a battery bank at 27.00 V, and each reading has a random error of 0.04 V, its standard deviation. A single reading can easily land 0.08 V from the true value. Now make each reading the mean of four. Their random errors point different ways, so they partly cancel. The spread of a mean of n readings is the spread of one reading divided by √n.[^4] Four readings halve it, to 0.02 V. Two readings cut it only to about 0.028 V. So to halve the spread again you need four times as many readings, not twice as many.\n\nThe same rule says how well the mean of fifty readings is pinned down. Divide the 0.04 V spread by √50, about 7.07, and double it. That gives about 0.011 V. Textbooks add that this doubled bracket, the mean ± 2 × the standard deviation ÷ √n, is the usual one. It catches where the meter centres about 95% of the time. A stated [[uncertainty]] always needs both parts: a width and how confident you are, such as 20 cm ± 1 cm at 95%.[^6] So fifty readings give a result of 27.00 ± 0.011 V. That bracket is far narrower than the 0.08 V either side where single readings land. The result says how well the mean is known. The scatter says how far one reading wanders.\n\nNow say the meter reads 0.02 V high. The same influence lands on every reading, so the whole pile shifts across. That is a [[systematic error]]. Repeating teaches you nothing extra about it.[^2] Make each bead the mean of four and the pile tightens round 27.02 V, where the meter centres. The result, 27.02 ± 0.006 V, now misses the true 27.00 V. Yet the meter is still within its accuracy, the most its maker allows it to be out. Accuracy is written as a percentage of the reading plus a few digits, each digit one step of the display. An accuracy of ±(2% + 2) on a reading of 100.0 V allows anything from 97.8 V to 102.2 V.[^5] That display step is the meter's [[resolution]], the finest change it can show. A finer step shows more detail, but it does not move where the meter centres. Only a check against a known reference finds the offset.\n\nThis matters most against a limit. If the whole bracket sits on one side of the limit, the reading shows which side the value is on. If the bracket overlaps the limit, the true value could be on either side, so the reading shows neither a pass nor a fail. The bracket from repeats covers only the random part. A full uncertainty also takes in the meter's accuracy and every other influence. Working those together is an uncertainty budget, and this simplified picture leaves it out.\n\n**Averaging repeat readings shrinks random scatter by the square root of how many you take, but no number of repeats removes a steady offset.[^2] So a result is a value plus or minus its uncertainty, at a stated confidence.**"

    "text": "- Take several readings the same way: same leads, same points, same range.\n- Find their mean and standard deviation.\n- Divide the standard deviation by the square root of how many readings you took, then double it. That is the random part of the result's bracket, at about 95%.\n- Work out the meter's accuracy at that reading: the percentage of the reading, plus the digits times one step of the display.\n- Remember that repeats cannot shrink the accuracy. Check the meter against a known reference to find any offset.\n- Set the whole bracket against the limit. If it overlaps the limit, the reading shows neither a pass nor a fail. Take more readings or use a better meter.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** four times the readings halves the random bracket. No number of readings removes an offset.\n\n**Worked example:** ten readings of a battery bank's float voltage, with an example meter rated ±(0.1% + 2 digits) reading to 0.01 V. Their mean is 27.04 V and their standard deviation 0.03 V. The random part is 2 × 0.03 ÷ √10, and √10 is about 3.16, so about 0.02 V. The accuracy is 0.1% of 27.04 V, 0.027 V, plus two digits, 0.02 V, so 0.047 V. Adding both straight, the true voltage could be anywhere from about 26.97 V to about 27.11 V. Against an example maximum of 27.15 V, the whole bracket is below, so it shows a pass. With fewer than about 30 readings, textbooks raise the factor of 2 a little. Here that would not change the answer.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Quoting one reading as the value, with no bracket.\n- Expecting more readings to cancel a meter that reads high.\n- Taking the last digit for the accuracy. The display step is the resolution, and accuracy is usually wider.\n- Dividing by the number of readings instead of its square root.\n- Reading the result's bracket as where single readings land.\n- Calling a pass when the bracket touches the limit."
