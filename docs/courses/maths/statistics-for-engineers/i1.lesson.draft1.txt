    "text": "Read the same circuit fifty times and you get fifty slightly different numbers. They pile up round a middle, and the middle and the spread of that pile are what the reading really is. Drop each reading as a bead on a beam at its value. The [[mean]], all the readings added and divided by how many, is where the beam balances. Each bead turns the beam by its distance from the pivot, and at the mean the distances above add to exactly the same as those below. Take 0.79, 0.80, 0.80, 0.81 and 0.85 Ω. They add to 4.05 Ω, so the mean is 0.81 Ω. Below it sit 0.02 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.04 Ω of distance, and above it 0.04 Ω, so it balances. Line the readings up in order and the one in the middle is the [[median]], here 0.80 Ω. With an even count it is halfway between the middle two.\n\nThe two part company when a reading goes astray. Say a slipped probe turns one of the fifty into 1.80 Ω, 1.00 Ω too high. The total rises by 1.00 Ω, shared over fifty readings, so the mean rises by 0.02 Ω. The median only counts places in the order. The slipped reading jumps to the top of the order, so the middle moves by one place at most, which in this pile is half a meter step, 0.005 Ω, or nothing. Extreme values distort the mean but not the median, because the median is based on ranks.[^2]\n\nThe middle alone cannot tell a steady meter from a jumpy one. Halve the random error and the pile shrinks to half its width and grows nearly twice as tall, yet it balances at the same point. The [[standard deviation]] measures how far readings typically sit from the mean. Take each reading's distance from the mean, square it, average the squares and take the square root.[^1] The squaring is needed because the distances always add to zero, which is the very reason the beam balances. It is the RMS recipe you know from AC, run on the distances. For a handful of readings, textbooks add that you average the squares by dividing by one fewer than the count. The mean was found from those same readings, so they sit a little closer to it than to the true value, and the smaller divisor makes up for that. For 0.77, 0.79, 0.81, 0.81 and 0.82 Ω the mean is 0.80 Ω. The distances are −0.03, −0.01, +0.01, +0.01 and +0.02 Ω. Their squares add to 0.0016, divided by 4 that is 0.0004, and its root is 0.02 Ω. Squaring makes a far reading count heavily, so the slipped probe lifts the fifty readings' standard deviation from about 0.02 Ω to about 0.14 Ω. The range, highest minus lowest, is a quick look, but it uses only the two end readings. So two piles can share a range and still differ in spread. Ten repeat readings of one circuit give that circuit's value, and their spread is the wobble of the reading. One reading each on ten circuits gives a typical circuit, and their spread is mostly how the circuits differ. Before you call this year's 0.02 Ω rise a change, you need the standard deviation of repeat readings of that circuit to measure it against.\n\n**Repeat readings pile up round a middle: the mean is where the pile balances and the median is its middle reading. The standard deviation, the root of the mean of each reading's squared distance from the mean, measures how far they typically sit from it.[^1]**"

    "text": "- Take repeat readings the same way each time: same leads, same points, same instrument.\n- Write every reading down in the order you took it.\n- Find the mean: add them and divide by how many.\n- Find the median: put them in order and take the middle one, or halfway between the middle two.\n- Find the standard deviation: each reading's distance from the mean, squared, added, divided by one fewer than the count, then the square root. On a calculator it is usually the key marked s or σn−1.\n- If the mean and the median disagree, look for a stray reading and find its cause before you trust the mean.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** judge spread by how far readings sit from the mean, not by the range or by how bumpy the pile looks.\n\n**Worked example:** five example RCD trip times of 18, 20, 21, 22 and 24 ms. They add to 105 ms, so the mean is 21 ms, and the middle reading, the median, is 21 ms too. The distances are −3, −1, 0, +1 and +3 ms. Their squares, 9, 1, 0, 1 and 9, add to 20. Divided by 4 that is 5, and the root of 5 is about 2.2 ms. So these trip times typically sit about 2.2 ms from 21 ms.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Forgetting the square root at the end, which leaves an answer in squared units.\n- Judging spread by the range alone. One stray reading sets it, and it ignores where the rest sit.\n- Averaging in a slipped reading without asking why it is so far out.\n- Mixing repeat readings of one circuit with readings of different circuits, then reading their spread as the meter's wobble."
