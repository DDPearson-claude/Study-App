A [[failure rate]] says how often, as failures each year, or each hour, for each unit still working. For most of a product's useful life, if all goes well, that rate stays roughly steady.[^1] A steady rate sets the share of units still working at any age. It also means far fewer reach the average lifetime than you might expect.\n\nHere is why. Call the rate λ. Over a short spell, every working unit has the same small chance of failing, λ times the spell. So the stock loses the same fraction of what is left in every spell. That gives the decay curve you met with a capacitor discharging. The share still working is the [[reliability]], R = e^(−λt). One over the rate, 1 ÷ λ, is the mean time to failure, usually called the [[MTBF]] in power work.[^2] Put t = 1 ÷ λ into the rule, and R = e^(−1), about 0.37. Lifetimes pile up short, with a long tail of units that last far longer. That tail pulls the mean up, so most units fail before it. At 0.1 a year the MTBF is 10 years, yet half have failed by about 7.\n\nTwo units together follow two rules, if each fails on its own. In series, the pair works only while both work. A share R of first units still works, and a share R of those have a working partner. So the pair's R is R × R. That is e^(−λt) × e^(−λt) = e^(−2λt), so the failure rates add. In parallel, the pair works while at least one works. It fails only if both fail, with chance (1 − R) × (1 − R). So its R is 1 − (1 − R)². A part the two share, such as one supply, can stop both at once. Then the parallel rule promises too much.\n\nReal units are rarely steady for their whole lives. Textbooks add that units with hidden faults often fail early, and that wear-out makes the rate climb late on. That shape is called the bathtub curve, and the steady model fits only its flat middle. There a failure comes by chance, not age, while a climbing rate is wear.
