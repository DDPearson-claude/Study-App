    "text": "Repeat readings often pile into a bell: lots near the mean, fewer and fewer further out. Because of that shape, counting how many standard deviations a reading sits from the mean tells you how unusual it is. Here is why a pile takes that shape. Each simulated reading is the true value plus twelve small random nudges. To land far out, most of its nudges must push the same way. That is rare, because most mixes of pushes partly cancel. So most readings land near the middle, and fewer land the further out you go. Real repeat readings often pile into a similar bell. The smooth curve drawn to fit such a pile is the [[normal curve]]. It is a model, and it does not fit every pile. The insulation resistance readings in the first lesson leaned into a long tail.\n\nThe standard deviation sets the width of a normal curve. Every normal curve is the same shape, only stretched. So the share of readings within 1, 2 or 3 SD of the mean is the same for every bell, whatever the units. Textbooks give about 68% within 1 SD. About 95% sit within 2 SD.[^1] About 99.7% sit within 3 SD.[^2] A share of the time is a chance. So one honest reading has about a 95% chance of landing within 2 SD. In fifty honest readings, expect about 34 within 1 SD and about 2 or 3 beyond 2 SD. Remember the bracket of most beads in the last lesson, the mean ± 2 SD? Now you can say how many it holds.\n\nTo judge one reading, measure its distance from the mean in SDs. Subtract the mean, then divide by the SD. That count is the reading's [[z-score]]. Say a UPS output's readings have a mean of 115.0 V and an SD of 0.3 V. A reading of 115.6 V is 0.6 ÷ 0.3 = 2 SD high. That is unusual, but honest readings do it, about one time in forty. A reading of 116.0 V is 1.0 ÷ 0.3, about 3.3 SD high. Honest readings land that far out very rarely.\n\nSo a reading far out is more likely a slip, a loose lead or a real change. One common choice calls any reading beyond about 3.3 SD an [[outlier]].[^2] That number is a convention. The habit matters more: ask why the reading happened before you set it aside.[^3] Deleting it unasked could throw away the one reading that shows a fault. Honest readings also land past limits now and then, so one reading out does not prove a fault. Test again.\n\n**Count a reading's distance from the mean in standard deviations. In a bell, about 95% of honest readings land within 2, so one far beyond 3 is rare enough to ask why before you trust it or delete it.[^3]**"

    "text": "- Gather past readings taken the same way: same meter, same point, same load if you can.\n- Find their mean and standard deviation. Leave out the reading you are judging, so it cannot stretch its own ruler.\n- Subtract the mean from the new reading, then divide by the SD. That is its z-score.\n- Within 2 SD: an ordinary reading.\n- Between 2 and 3 SD: unusual, but honest readings land there now and then. Note it and watch the next one.\n- Beyond about 3 SD: rare by chance. Check the leads and the connection, test again and look for a cause.\n- Set a reading aside only once you know why it is wrong. Keep it in the record either way, with the reason.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** for readings that pile into a bell, about 68% land within 1 SD, 95% within 2 and 99.7% within 3.\n\n**Worked example:** a UPS output's past readings on rounds have a mean of 115.0 V and an SD of 0.3 V. The first reading was most likely a poor connection, not the UPS.
