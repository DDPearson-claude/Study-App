Because of that shape, counting how many standard deviations a reading sits from the mean tells you how unusual it is. Here is why a pile takes that shape. Each simulated reading is the true value plus twelve small random nudges. To land far out, most of its nudges must push the same way. That is rare, because most mixes of pushes partly cancel. So most readings land near the middle, and fewer land the further out you go. Textbooks add that real repeat readings often pile into a similar bell. The smooth curve drawn to fit such a pile is the [[normal curve]]. It is a model, and it does not fit every pile. The insulation resistance readings in the first lesson leaned into a long tail.\n\nThe standard deviation sets the width of a normal curve. Every normal curve is the same shape, only stretched. So the share of readings within 1, 2 or 3 SD of the mean is the same for every normal curve, whatever the units. Textbooks add that about 68% sit within 1 SD. About 95% sit within 2 SD.[^1] About 99.7% sit within 3 SD.[^2] A share of the time is a chance. So one honest reading has about a 95% chance of landing within 2 SD. In fifty honest readings, expect about 34 within 1 SD and about 2 or 3 beyond 2 SD. Remember the bracket of most beads in the last lesson, the mean ± 2 SD? Now you can say how many it holds.\n\nTo judge one reading, measure its distance from the mean in SDs. Subtract the mean, then divide by the SD. That count is the reading's [[z-score]]. Say a UPS output's readings have a mean of 115.0 V and an SD of 0.3 V. A reading of 115.6 V is 0.6 ÷ 0.3 = 2 SD high. That is unusual, but honest readings land that high or higher about one time in forty. A reading of 116.0 V is 1.0 ÷ 0.3, about 3.3 SD high. Honest readings land that far out very rarely.\n\nSo a reading far out is more likely a slip, such as a loose lead, or a real change.
