That difference is a quadratic, so its graph is an upside-down U. It is zero where the two parts are equal, and highest halfway between those two roots.\n\nTake a ball thrown straight up at 19.6 m/s, an example speed. With no gravity it would rise in step with time, 19.6t metres after t seconds. That is the straight-line part. Gravity adds about 9.8 m/s of downward speed every second, so after t seconds it has added 9.8t. Its average downward speed over that time is halfway from 0 to 9.8t, which is 4.9t, and 4.9t for t seconds pulls the ball 4.9t² metres below the line. That 4.9 is half of gravity's roughly 9.8 m/s².[^1] The fall is squared because two things grow with time together, the downward speed and the time it has had to act. So h = 19.6t − 4.9t². Now say a 12 V battery has 0.5 Ω inside it, example figures. Its voltage with nothing connected, E = 12 V, drives a current I and makes EI = 12I watts, the straight-line part. Its internal resistance r drops I × r volts while passing I amps, so it burns I²r = 0.5I² watts inside, the I²R heat of lesson 6. The load gets what is left, P = 12I − 0.5I², and again two things grow together, the current and the volts it loses inside. Textbooks add that a beam's [[bending moment]] at a point is the turning effect, force times distance, of everything on one side of it, which tries to bend the beam there. It is measured in newton metres, like a torque setting, here in kilonewton metres, kN·m. Say a 6 m beam rests on two simple supports, which let it turn, and carries 2 kN on every metre, 12 kN in all. The load is spread evenly, so each support pushes up half of it, 6 kN. At a point x metres from the left support, that push turns with a lever arm of x, giving 6x kN·m. The 2x kN of load on those x metres acts as if at their middle, x ÷ 2 away, and turns the other way with 2x × x ÷ 2 = x² kN·m. So M = 6x − x², with the load and its lever arm both growing with x.\n\nThe roots are where the two parts are equal, so the gap is zero. 19.6t = 4.9t² at 0 s, the throw, and 4 s, the landing. 12I = 0.5I² at 0 A, nothing connected, and 24 A, a short circuit, E ÷ r.[^3] 6x = x² at 0 m and 6 m, the two simple supports, where the bending moment must be zero.[^4] A parabola is the same either side of its line of symmetry, so its peak sits in the middle of its roots:[^5] 19.6 m at 2 s, 72 W at 12 A, and 9 kN·m at 3 m. The middle of the roots is also why the battery's best current is exactly half its short-circuit current, E ÷ 2r:[^6] the peak sits halfway between 0 and E ÷ r. At 12 A the load has 12 − 0.5 × 12 = 6 V across it, so its resistance is 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 Ω, the same as r, which is the condition for the most power to reach the load,[^6] called [[maximum power transfer]].\n\nRead every root and peak in its job's terms.
