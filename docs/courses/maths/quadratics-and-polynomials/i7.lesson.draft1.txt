    "text": "A thrown ball's height, the power a battery gives a load and the bending in a loaded beam all follow one pattern: a straight-line part minus a squared part. That difference is a quadratic, so its graph is an upside-down U. It is zero where the two parts are equal, and highest halfway between those two roots.\n\nTake a ball thrown straight up at 19.6 m/s, an example speed. With no gravity it would rise in step with time, 19.6t metres after t seconds. That is the straight-line part. Gravity pulls it below that line by 4.9t² metres, where 4.9 is half of gravity's roughly 9.8 m/s².[^1] The fall is squared because two things grow with time together: the downward speed gravity adds, 9.8t, and the time it has had to act. In Calculus fundamentals a speed that grows steadily like that added up to ½ × 9.8 × t². So h = 19.6t − 4.9t². Now say a 12 V battery has 0.5 Ω inside it, example figures. At current I it makes EI = 12I watts, the straight-line part. Its internal resistance r drops I × r volts while passing I amps, so it burns I²r = 0.5I² watts inside, the I²R heat of lesson 6. The load gets what is left, P = 12I − 0.5I², and again two things grow together, the current and the volts it loses inside. Textbooks add that a beam's [[bending moment]] at a point is the turning effect, force times distance, of everything on one side of it, which tries to bend the beam there. Say a 6 m beam on two supports carries 2 kN on every metre, 12 kN in all. The load is spread evenly, so each support pushes up 6 kN. At a point x metres from the left support, that push turns with a lever arm of x, giving 6x kN·m. The 2x kN of load on those x metres acts as if at their middle, x ÷ 2 away, and turns the other way with 2x × x ÷ 2 = x² kN·m. So M = 6x − x², with the load and its lever arm both growing with x.\n\nThe roots are where the two parts are equal. 19.6t = 4.9t² at 0 s, the throw, and 4 s, the landing. 12I = 0.5I² at 0 A, nothing connected, and 24 A, a short circuit, E ÷ r.[^4] 6x = x² at 0 m and 6 m, the two supports, where a bending moment must be zero.[^6] A parabola is the same either side of its line of symmetry, so its peak sits in the middle of its roots:[^3] 19.6 m at 2 s, 72 W at 12 A, and 9 kN·m at 3 m, which matches the wL²/8 at midspan that engineers use, 2 × 6² ÷ 8.[^7] At 12 A the load has 6 V across it, so its resistance is 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 Ω, the same as r, which is the condition for the most power to reach the load,[^5] called [[maximum power transfer]]. But the same 12 A burns 72 W inside the battery, so only half of the 144 W it makes reaches the load. Read every root in its job's terms. A ball thrown up at 4.9 m/s from a deck 9.8 m above the water has h = 9.8 + 4.9t − 4.9t², zero at 2 s and at −1 s. A negative time is before the throw, so you discard it,[^14] and the ball hits the water at 2 s. And the load's power is a parabola in the current, not in the load's resistance. P = I²R grows in step with R only while the current stays the same: double this load to 1 Ω and the current falls to 12 ÷ 1.5 = 8 A, so the power falls to 8² × 1 = 64 W.\n\nEach job here is a teaching model: the ball ignores the air, the battery keeps E and r fixed, and the beam carries only an even load, so real curves come out a little different. **Take a squared part from a straight-line part and you get an upside-down U, zero where the two parts are equal and highest halfway between those two roots.**[^3]"
