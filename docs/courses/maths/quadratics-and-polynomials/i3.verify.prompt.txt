On the picture, one pointer feeds both brackets, because both hold the same x. At x = 3 the bars read 1 and −2, and the product is −2. At x = 2 the x − 2 bar is 0, so the product is 0 even though the other bar reads −3, and at x = 5 it is the x − 5 bar's turn. Values of x that make an equation true are its [[root]]s, so this equation has two roots, 2 and 5. Each bracket is zero at the number that cancels it, so the root has the opposite sign to the number in its bracket: x + 2 is zero at x = −2, and (x + 2)(x + 5) = 0 gives x = −2 or x = −5. The dot rides the graph of y = (x − 2)(x − 5), a U-shaped curve called a [[parabola]].[^2] Its height is the product, and on the axis the height is zero, so the dot meets the axis exactly at the two roots.\n\nThe rule needs a zero on one side. If (x − 2)(x − 5) = 10, the product is 10 at x = 0, where the brackets are −2 and −5, and at x = 7, where they are 5 and 2, so neither bracket is 10. Two numbers can multiply to 10 in endlessly many ways, so a 10 says nothing about either bracket alone, and x − 2 = 10 gives x = 12, where the product is 10 × 7 = 70.
