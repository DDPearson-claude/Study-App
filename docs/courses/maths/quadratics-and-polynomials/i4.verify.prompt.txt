On the picture, x² + 6x is an x by x square with a strip 6 wide beside it. A square needs both its sides lengthened by the same amount, so the strip is shared equally: cut it into two strips 3 wide and move one round to the bottom. The pieces now nearly fill a square with sides x + 3. Only a 3 by 3 corner is missing, because (x + 3)² is x² + 6x + 9, the perfect square from lesson 2. So x² + 6x is that square less its corner: x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9. Adding the corner and taking it off again changes nothing, which a number confirms: at x = 1, 1 + 6 = 7 and 16 − 9 = 7. Leave the corner on and (x + 3)² gives 16 there, not 7. The 2 on the end just comes along: x² + 6x + 2 = (x + 3)² − 9 + 2 = (x + 3)² − 7. Whatever the number in front of x, half of it goes on each side, so the corner is (b ÷ 2)², and x² + bx + c = (x + b ÷ 2)² + c − (b ÷ 2)². That is the case with no number in front of x² of the general rule.[^2] The move is called [[completing the square]], and (x + 3)² − 7 is the quadratic's [[completed-square form]]. It also finishes the x² + 4x + 1 that lesson 2 could not factorise: x² + 4x + 1 = (x + 2)² − 4 + 1 = (x + 2)² − 3.\n\nThe square is what shows the lowest value.
