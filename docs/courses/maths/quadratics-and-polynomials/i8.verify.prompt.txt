  "text": "A [[polynomial]] is a rule that adds up whole-number powers of x, each with a number in front, such as x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6.[^1] That one is a cubic, because its highest power is x³. Call the rule P(x), so P(4) is its value at x = 4. Every number a that makes it zero gives it a bracket (x − a). So you factorise one with a higher power by finding a root, dividing out its bracket and factorising what is left. It is lesson 3 run backwards: there two brackets gave two roots, and here each root gives its bracket.\n\nDividing by a bracket works like long division with numbers: divide the front term, multiply back, take away, bring down the next term, and repeat. You stop when what is left has no x for the bracket's x to divide into, so the remainder is a plain number. Divide x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 by (x − 4) and you get x² − 2x + 3 with 6 left over. So x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 4)(x² − 2x + 3) + 6, for every x. Now put x = 4. The bracket x − 4 is 0, so the whole product is 0 and only the 6 is left. So P(4) is the remainder: 64 − 96 + 44 − 6 = 6. The same steps work for any number a, so putting a into the rule gives the remainder from dividing by (x − a) without any dividing; textbooks call this the [[remainder theorem]]. When P(a) = 0 the remainder is 0, so (x − a) divides exactly and is a factor. That is the [[factor theorem]].[^2] At x = 1 the rule gives 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor; dividing leaves x² − 5x + 6, which splits into (x − 2)(x − 3) because −2 and −3 add to −5 and multiply to 6.[^3] The root and its bracket carry opposite signs: the root 2 gives (x − 2), and the root −2 gives (x + 2), because that is the bracket the root makes zero.\n\nEach root is where the curve meets the x-axis, and each brings its own bracket. Three brackets already multiply out to x³, and a fourth would make x⁴, so a cubic meets the axis at most three times. Bring two roots together and their bracket comes twice: x³ − 5x² + 8x − 4 = (x − 1)(x − 2)². A square is never negative, so near 2 the curve comes down to the axis and goes back up on the same side. It touches instead of crossing, at a repeated root. Some cubics meet the axis only once: x³ + 8 is 0 at x = −2, so it is (x + 2)(x² − 2x + 4), and that quadratic's discriminant, 4 − 16 = −12, is negative, so it has no real roots. Textbooks add that its two missing roots are complex numbers, which the Complex numbers course takes up. The course's power rule, 12I − 0.5I² watts for the load, is a polynomial in I too: it is 0 at I = 0 and at I = 24, so I and (I − 24) are both factors, 12I − 0.5I² = −0.5I(I − 24), and the best current sits halfway between those roots. **If putting x = a into a polynomial gives 0, then (x − a) is one of its factors.**[^2]"
