**The main pictures** (one per lesson, each moved with your finger):
- **The two-bracket rectangle (lesson 1).** A rectangle whose sides are x + a and x + b. Drag the
  split on each side and the rectangle cuts into four tiles, x², ax, bx and ab, which add up to the
  expanded rule. Make the two sides the same and the two middle tiles are the 2xy that x² + y² leaves out.
- **Tiles into a rectangle (lesson 2).** An x² tile, some x-strips and some unit squares. Drag them
  into a rectangle. It closes only when the unit squares split into a pair that adds up to the
  number of strips. The rectangle's two sides are the two brackets.
- **Two brackets and their product (lesson 3).** Slide x along a line. Two bars show the two
  brackets' values and a third shows their product, while a dot rides the parabola below. The
  product is zero exactly where one bar is zero, and that is where the dot sits on the axis.
- **Finish the square (lesson 4).** x² + bx drawn as a square with a strip. Drag half the strip
  round to the other side and a corner is missing. That corner is the number you add, and the
  parabola beside it shows the lowest point the finished square gives.
- **Centre and spread (lesson 5).** A parabola you drag up and down. Its axis sits at −b ÷ 2a and two
  equal arrows reach out to the roots. Lift it and the arrows shrink, meet, and vanish, while the
  number under the square root goes from positive to zero to negative.
- **Three clues and a line (lesson 6).** Set a, b and c. The y-intercept, the roots and the turning
  point light up as three clues to the sketch. Then lay a straight line across it and count the
  crossings: two, one or none.
- **One curve, three jobs (lesson 7).** A switch between a ball thrown up, a supply feeding a load,
  and a loaded beam. Each curve is drawn as a straight-line part minus a squared part, and their
  difference is the parabola. Drag along it and its roots and its peak read in that job's units.
- **Roots on a string (lesson 8).** A cubic with three root handles on the x-axis. Drag them and the
  bracketed rule and the expanded rule rewrite. Push two handles together and the curve touches the
  axis instead of crossing. A "try x =" slider shows the remainder, and where it reads 0 a bracket drops out.

## 1. The overall topic
A quadratic is a rule whose highest power is x², such as x² + 5x + 6 (Bitesize: quadratic
expressions "contain 𝑥²", notes 2.2). Its graph is a U or an upside-down U called a parabola (notes
4.7). This course is about three ways of writing the same quadratic, and what each one tells you:
- **The expanded form, ax² + bx + c**, which says where the graph crosses the y-axis.
- **The factorised form, (x − p)(x − q)**, which says where it crosses the x-axis.
- **The completed-square form, a(x + p)² + q**, which says where it turns.

It then solves quadratic equations three ways (factorising, completing the square, the formula),
counts their roots with the discriminant, and puts them to work on a thrown ball, the power a supply
gives a load, and a loaded beam. It ends on polynomials with higher powers: their roots, the factor
theorem and dividing by a bracket.

**It builds on:**
- **Linear equations:** like terms, single brackets and factorising out a common factor (lessons 1
  to 3). Lesson 1 here grows one bracket into two, and lesson 2 starts every factorising with the
  common factor.
- **Negative numbers:** signs in products, which Bitesize points to before factorising quadratics
  (notes 2.2).
- **Geometry fundamentals:** the area of a rectangle and a square, which is the picture for lessons
  1, 2 and 4.
- **Powers and roots:** squares and square roots, √ meaning the positive root, x² = 36 having two
  answers, and simplifying surds such as √28 = 2√7 (its lesson 3). P = I²R and "twice the current,
  four times the heat" (its lesson 7).
- **Functions and their graphs:** the family y = x², and shifting and stretching a graph, including
  which way f(x − 3) moves (its lessons 3, 5 and 6). Its brief leaves "the shifted parabola in
  vertex form" and "cubics with more than one bend" to this course (notes 5).
- **Straight-line graphs** and **Linear systems:** y = mx + c, and where two lines meet. Linear
  systems leaves "a straight line meeting a curve" here (notes 5).
- **Calculus fundamentals:** a turning point has slope zero (its lesson 6), and maximum power to a
  load from P = EI − I²r. Its brief read an equation like 12x² − 240x + 900 = 0 off the picture
  because you had no quadratics (notes 5). Lesson 3 here solves it.

**Not taught again:** single brackets and the common factor, like terms, signs in products, the two
square roots and simplifying surds, "twice the current, four times the heat", the y = x² family and
its shifts and stretches, y = mx + c, substitution, slope zero at a turning point. Each is used with
a one-line reminder, and only what is new is taught.

**Taught inline, because you have not met them:** the words quadratic, coefficient, binomial (the
MCA's word for a two-term bracket, notes 3.4), root of an equation (a value that makes it true, a
different job from a square root), turning point, line of symmetry, discriminant, polynomial,
degree, factor, quotient, remainder and repeated root. Long division with plain numbers, on one
screen in lesson 8, before it is done with brackets (Khan: a remainder "just like with integer
division!", notes 2.1). Adding two fractions with brackets underneath, only for lesson 8's stretch
screen.

**Edges.** Real roots only. Quadratics with small whole-number or decimal coefficients, a = 1 and
a ≠ 1. Cubics with at least one whole-number root, so the factor theorem can find it by trying.
A ball thrown straight up only. No complex roots, no cubic formula, no iteration, no synthetic division.

**Left for later courses:**
- **Exponentials and logarithms:** finding the power in a law from readings. Ship power is "roughly
  proportional to the cube of the speed", but doubling one boat's speed raised its power tenfold,
  not eightfold (USNA, notes 7.4). Finding its real power needs logs, which is where the Straight-line
  graphs brief also sends laws like this (my reading).
- **Trigonometry:** a projectile launched at an angle. The MCA's Chief Engineer list "Derives the
  equations for the horizontal and vertical components of the motion of a projectile" (notes 3.4),
  and splitting a launch into components needs sine and cosine. Lesson 7 says so.
- **Complex numbers:** the two complex roots of a negative discriminant (OpenStax, HELM, notes 4.6),
  which SQA's HN unit uses to bring complex numbers in (notes 3.1), and complex roots of a
  polynomial coming in pairs (HELM Workbook 10, notes 2.3, 4.9). Lessons 5 and 8 end by pointing there.
- **Partial fractions in full** (repeated and quadratic factors, Pearson's "rules of order", notes
  3.3): no later course on the shelf holds them. They are offered as a ninth lesson here.
- **Dropped, not deferred:** iteration for equations with no formula (Bitesize's seventh page, notes
  2.2; pump software uses Newton-Raphson for higher-order curves, notes 7.4). Lesson 8 names it on
  one screen as a college method. Also Brilliant's sums of nth powers and biquadratics (notes 1.2).

## 2. The lessons, in the order they build
Every source expands, then factorises, then solves by factorising (notes 2.5). Completing the square
comes next, so the formula can be derived from it, as Brilliant and Bird order it (notes 1.1, 2.4,
2.5). The research's core of eight lessons runs in this order (notes 8).

| # | Lesson | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Two brackets as an area | "Every term in the first bracket has to be multiplied by every term in the second" (Bitesize, notes 4.1), seen as the four tiles of a rectangle. (x + 3)(x + 2) = x² + 2x + 3x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6 (Bitesize, notes 4.1). The grid for signs: (x − 4)(x + 1) = x² + x − 4x − 4 = x² − 3x − 4, checked at x = 5 (1 × 6 = 6, and 25 − 15 − 4 = 6). The square of a bracket: (x + 5)² = x² + 10x + 25, not x² + 25. The picture shows the two missing tiles (Wikipedia's square of side X + Y, notes 4.1), and a number shows the slip is false: (1 + 4)² = 25, not 17 (notes 6.1). Sum times difference: (x + 3)(x − 3) = x² − 9, because the middle tiles cancel. A number in front: (2x + 1)(x + 3) = 2x² + 7x + 3. A quadratic named, and a binomial (MCA 2.1.11 to 2.1.13, notes 3.4). |
| 2 | Factorising quadratics | The reverse of expanding (Bitesize, SQA, notes 2.2, 4.2). x² + bx + c has no common factor, so it needs two brackets (Bitesize, notes 4.2). The two numbers add to b and multiply to c: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) (Bitesize, notes 4.2). With signs: x² − x − 12 = (x − 4)(x + 3), since −4 + 3 = −1 and −4 × 3 = −12. Look for a common factor first: x² + 2x = x(x + 2), and 2x² + 10x + 12 = 2(x + 2)(x + 3) (SQA 2024 report, notes 6.2). a ≠ 1 by the ac method: 2x² + 7x + 3 has ac = 6, so split 7x into 6x + x and group, 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (2x + 1)(x + 3) (Norton review, notes 6.2; MCA 2.1.17 and 2.1.18, notes 3.4). Perfect squares, x² − 6x + 9 = (x − 3)², and the difference of two squares, x² − 49 = (x + 7)(x − 7) (MCA 2.1.19, 2.1.20). Some will not factorise with whole numbers, such as x² + 4x + 1, which waits for lesson 4 (Bitesize, notes 4.4). Check by expanding back. |
| 3 | Solving by factorising | "If ab = 0, then a = 0 and/or b = 0" (Bitesize, LibreTexts, notes 4.3). A root is a value that makes the equation true (MCA 2.1.31, notes 3.4). (x − 2)(x − 5) = 0 gives x = 2 or x = 5, the two places the graph crosses the x-axis (MCA, notes 3.4). (x + 2)(x + 5) = 0 gives −2 and −5: the signs swap (Mr Barton, SECONDARY, notes 6.3). Get zero on one side first: x² − 5x = 6 becomes (x − 6)(x + 1) = 0, so 6 or −1, both checked. (x − 2)(x − 5) = 10 does not mean x − 2 = 10: it becomes x(x − 7) = 0, so 0 or 7 (the class slides' slip, SECONDARY, notes 6.3). x² = 6x: do not divide by x, factorise to x(x − 6) = 0, so 0 or 6 (Edexcel 2021, notes 6.3). 3x² = 48 gives x = ±4 (Bitesize, notes 4.3). x² − 6x + 9 = 0 has one root, 3, and the graph touches the axis there (Bitesize, notes 4.3). Calculus fundamentals' 12x² − 240x + 900 = 0 divides by 12 to (x − 5)(x − 15) = 0. Factorising an expression is not solving an equation (notes 6.3). |
| 4 | Completing the square | x² + 6x is a square and a strip. Split the strip in two, put the halves on two sides, and a 3 by 3 corner is missing. So x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9: the extra "has to be subtracted" (Bitesize, notes 4.4), the "added area" that "completes the square" (MathWorld, notes 4.4). x² + 6x + 2 = (x + 3)² − 7. Solve it: (x + 3)² = 7, x = −3 ± √7, about −0.35 or −5.65. A square cannot go below 0, so the lowest value is −7, at x = −3, which is also the line of symmetry (Bitesize, notes 4.4). That is y = x² slid 3 left and 7 down (Functions lesson 5). The slope 2x + 6 is zero at x = −3 too (Calculus lesson 6). A minus in front gives a maximum, with care over the signs (Brilliant wiki, notes 4.4): −x² + 4x + 10 = −(x − 2)² + 14, top 14 at x = 2. A number in front: 2x² + 12x + 5 = 2(x + 3)² − 13 (Brilliant level 14, SQA National 5's k(x + p)² + q, notes 1.1, 2.2). |
| 5 | The quadratic formula | Do lesson 4 with letters on ax² + bx + c = 0, and the formula comes out: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a (OpenStax, Bitesize, notes 4.5). Check it on x² + 6x + 2 = 0: b² − 4ac = 28, √28 = 2√7, so x = −3 ± √7, as in lesson 4. Read it as a centre, −b ÷ 2a, plus or minus a spread (OpenStax: the value halfway between the roots is −b ÷ 2a, notes 4.7). The fireworks equation 4.90t² − 14.3t − 20.0 = 0 (OpenStax, notes 7.2). The discriminant b² − 4ac counts the roots: positive gives two, zero gives one repeated root, negative gives no real roots (OpenStax, HELM, SQA, notes 4.6). x² + 2x + 5 = 0 has a negative discriminant and its graph "does not cross or touch the x-axis" (Bitesize, notes 4.6). Say the nature of the roots in SQA's words (notes 4.6). Choose the method: factorise, complete the square or the formula (Booth, notes 6.8). Last screen: no real roots now, and Complex numbers later. |
| 6 | The parabola from its rule | Three clues for a sketch. The sign of a: positive is a U with a minimum, negative an upside-down U with a maximum (Bitesize, notes 4.7). c is where it crosses the y-axis, (0, c) (Bitesize, notes 4.7). The roots are the x-axis crossings (OpenStax, notes 4.7). The turning point sits halfway between them (OpenStax, notes 4.7). y = x² − 2x − 8 = (x − 4)(x + 2): crosses at −2 and 4, crosses the y-axis at −8, turns at (1, −9). a is not a slope: in a table of y-values the steps between them change by the same amount, 2a, each time (MathBits, notes 4.7; Ellis and Grinstead, notes 6.6). Read the three forms, each for what it tells you (Khan, Childers, notes 4.7, 6.6). A line meets the curve: y = x² and y = x + 2 meet at (−1, 1) and (2, 4), y = 2x − 1 touches at (1, 1), and y = x − 1 misses, with a negative discriminant (MCA 2.1.35, notes 3.4). |
| 7 | Quadratics at work | Each curve is a straight-line part minus a squared part, and the roots and the peak mean something. A ball thrown up: h = 19.6t − 4.9t² (example figures, the metric form h = −4.9t² + v₀t + h₀ from notes 7.2). It lands at the positive root, 4 s; the root at 0 is the throw. The peak is halfway, 19.6 m at 2 s (Wikipedia, SECONDARY, notes 7.2). The fireworks' negative time "implies an event before the start of motion, so we discard it" (OpenStax, notes 7.2). Power in a load: P = EI − I²r is a downward parabola in I. With E = 12 V and r = 0.5 Ω (example figures) it is zero at 0 A and 24 A, so the peak is halfway, 12 A, giving 72 W, where the load equals r (Wikipedia, PMC, notes 7.1). Only half the source's power reaches the load there (Farside, notes 7.1). P against R is not a parabola: the quadratic is in the current (Physics SE, SECONDARY, notes 7.1). A beam with a uniform load: the bending moment is parabolic, zero at both supports, largest at midspan, wL²/8 (notes 7.3). |
| 8 | Higher powers | A polynomial adds up whole-number powers of x, and its degree is the highest one. (a + b)³ as (a + b)(a + b)² = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³ (MCA 2.1.14, 2.1.15, notes 3.4). A degree n equation has n roots, some repeated and some complex (HELM, SECONDARY, notes 4.9), so a cubic crosses the axis at most three times. The factor theorem: if P(a) = 0, then (x − a) is a factor (MathWorld, Bird, notes 4.9). P(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 gives P(1) = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor. Long division by (x − 1) leaves x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) (MCA, Pearson, notes 3.3, 3.4). The remainder theorem: dividing by (x − 4) leaves P(4) = 6, "we don't need to do any division" (Math is Fun, notes 4.9). x³ + 8 has root −2, so (x + 2) is a factor, leaving x² − 2x + 4 (MCA's a³ + b³, notes 3.4). A repeated root touches the axis (OpenStax, notes 4.9). Where cubics turn up: ship power roughly as speed cubed (USNA, notes 7.4). Optional stretch screen: partial fractions with two linear factors. |

## 3. Can it be taught mostly visually, through interaction?
**Yes**, for all eight (RUNBOOK section 0, item 3). Every lesson is a steps lesson of about 12
screens, SHOW then DO, ending on two or three mixed puzzles. The research asks for exactly this.
SQA's course notes say "Make connections with geometrical representations" and "Connect features of
graphs to the equations of quadratic functions" (notes 2.2). Norton's review found "a lack of
alternative pedagogies such as links to geometric models ... and the integrated use of graphs in
contexual settings" (notes 6.8). A 2026 systematic review is more careful: teaching completing the
square through geometric pictures is advocated, but "the empirical findings ... remain tentative"
(notes 6.8). So no lesson rests on a picture alone. Every area picture has its graph or a number
check beside it. Each lesson has one main picture you move with your finger, chosen because it shows
the cause, not just the answer:
- **Lesson 1, the two-bracket rectangle.** One side is x + a and the other x + b, with a handle at
  each split and a slider for x. The four tiles are labelled x², ax, bx and ab, with their areas, and
  the expanded rule adds them up underneath. Set a = b and the two middle tiles match: that is the
  2xy. For a minus sign, the rectangle becomes Bitesize's grid, its negative cells hatched, not red
  (notes 4.1). *Why:* "every term times every term" is not a rule to learn. It is the four pieces of
  one area. Booth and others list area models among the effective ways to show an expression (notes
  4.1). The picture shows exactly what (x + y)² = x² + y² leaves out (Wikipedia, SECONDARY, notes 4.1, 6.1).
- **Lesson 2, tiles into a rectangle.** An x² tile (two for 2x²), x-strips and unit squares. Drag
  them to build a rectangle. Its sides read off as the two brackets. With the wrong split, a gap or an
  overhang shows. Try (x + 3)(x + 3) for x² + 5x + 6 and the rectangle needs 9 squares and 6 strips,
  not 6 and 5. *Why:* factorising "is the reverse process of expanding brackets" (Bitesize, notes
  4.2), so it is lesson 1's rectangle run backwards. Algebra tiles link factorising to "the area model
  of rectangles and squares" (Howden, through the Norton review, SECONDARY, notes 4.1). The
  (x + 3)(x + 3) slip is the one a 2025 paper names (notes 6.2).
- **Lesson 3, two brackets and their product.** A pointer slides along the x-axis. Two bars show the
  values of (x − 2) and (x − 5), and a third shows their product. Below, a dot rides the parabola
  y = (x − 2)(x − 5). The product bar hits zero only where one bracket bar does, and the dot sits on
  the axis there. A switch sets the right-hand side to 10. The product reads 10 at x = 0 and x = 7, where the
  brackets are −2 and −5, then 5 and 2. Neither is 10, and neither is 0. *Why:* the zero-product rule is the whole step (notes 4.3). Students could factorise
  but "did not know how to apply the null factor law or did so incorrectly", and 24% did not see an
  equation was already factorised (Norton review, notes 6.3). Seeing why only zero works answers
  both, and ties each root to the crossing the MCA names (notes 3.4).
- **Lesson 4, finish the square.** x² + bx drawn as an x-by-x square and a b-by-x strip, with a slider
  for b. Drag half the strip round to the other side. The corner left empty is (b ÷ 2)², and it
  flashes as the number to add and take away again. Beside it, the parabola y = x² + bx + c shows
  its lowest point at x = −b ÷ 2, height c − (b ÷ 2)², moving with the slider. *Why:* this is
  al-Khwarizmi's square, "the added area 25 completes the square of side x + 5" (MathWorld, notes
  4.4). SQA's 2024 report says "Many candidates did not link these skills", completing the square
  and the turning point (notes 4.4), so one picture shows both at once.
- **Lesson 5, centre and spread.** A parabola with handles for a, b and c, which you can also drag up
  and down whole. A dashed line marks its axis at −b ÷ 2a, and two equal arrows reach from it to the
  roots, each √(b² − 4ac) ÷ 2a long. A readout shows b² − 4ac. Lift the parabola and the arrows shrink,
  meet at one root when the readout is 0, and vanish when it goes negative, where the readout says
  "no real roots" in ink. *Why:* the ± in the formula is the two arrows, so dropping it drops a root.
  The HELM tutors' guide says graphs suggest "two distinct solutions, a unique solution or no
  solutions; algebra can verify the suggestion" (notes 4.6), and Barnes linked graphs to the
  discriminant the same way (SECONDARY, notes 4.6).
- **Lesson 6, three clues and a line.** Set a, b and c with handles. The point (0, c), the roots and the
  turning point light up as markers, with a small table of y-values beside it whose steps change by 2a each time.
  Then a straight line with an m handle and a c handle, as in Straight-line graphs, lies across the
  parabola, and the crossings are counted. *Why:* the research names two risks, carrying line ideas
  across (two-thirds of students called a the "slope", Ellis and Grinstead, notes 6.6) and losing the
  link between the rule and the graph (Zaslavsky, Childers, notes 6.6). The clues tie each number in
  the rule to one feature of the graph. Showing a by its steps, not by rise over run, avoids the move
  Ellis and Grinstead say fed the slope idea (notes 6.6).
- **Lesson 7, one curve, three jobs.** A switch between three jobs. A ball thrown up with a time
  slider; a supply and a load with a current dial and a power readout; a beam on two supports with a
  uniform load and a point you drag along it. Each draws its straight-line part (19.6t, EI, wLx ÷ 2)
  and its squared part (4.9t², I²r, wx² ÷ 2), and the gap between them is the parabola. *Why:* the
  cause of each curve is that difference. The roots are where the two parts are equal, and the peak
  is halfway. OpenStax calls quadratics the model for "area and projectile motion" (notes 7.2), and
  Brilliant's level 12 ends on "Viable Solutions" (notes 1.1).
- **Lesson 8, roots on a string.** A cubic's graph with three root handles on the x-axis. Dragging
  them rewrites (x − p)(x − q)(x − r) and the expanded rule. Push two handles together and the curve
  touches the axis there instead of crossing. A "try x =" slider shows P(x) as a height and as the
  remainder of the division. Where it reads 0, the bracket (x − a) drops out and the rest of the rule
  is a quadratic. *Why:* the factor theorem is lesson 3 run backwards. Edexcel's examiners found
  candidates "unsure of the difference between a factor and a root" and confused "( x – 2) and the
  solution x =2" (notes 6.7). The picture shows the root on the axis and its bracket in the rule
  side by side.
- **Colour as before:** ink for curves, working and controls, amber for key terms, teal for small
  labels, red only for a slip, green only for finished. Negative cells and below-axis areas are hatched.

- **The mistakes to catch, each with a puzzle and its own hint.**
  - **Lesson 1, squaring a bracket term by term:** "Beginning students commonly make this error",
    (x + y)² as x² + y² (Wikipedia, SECONDARY), from assuming any operation "distributes linearly
    across terms" (OpenExamPrep, SECONDARY) (notes 6.1). A quadratics study also saw "adding unlike
    terms, and incorrect applications of index notation" (Norton review, notes 6.1).
  - **Lesson 1, signs in the grid:** Bitesize sends learners to negative numbers before factorising
    (notes 2.2), and Booth and others found the negative sign "problematic for learning to solve
    algebraic equations" (notes 5).
  - **Lesson 2, the wrong pair:** x² + 5x + 6 factored as (x + 3)(x + 3) (Persistent Misconceptions
    in Algebra, 2025, notes 6.2), and a student who "does not seek the correct factors (multiply to
    ac=24 and add to b=11)" (Norton review, notes 6.2).
  - **Lesson 2, missing the common factor:** some candidates "did not identify the common factor and
    applied an inappropriate method ... leading to two brackets" (SQA 2024, notes 6.2). x² + 2x is a
    common-factor job (blog, SECONDARY, notes 6.2).
  - **Lesson 3, the zero-product step:** able to factorise but not to apply the null factor law (Norton
    review; O'Connor and Norton through PMC, SECONDARY, notes 6.3). x(x + 9) = −20 solved as x = −20
    or x + 9 = −20 (class slides, SECONDARY, notes 6.3).
  - **Lesson 3, the lost x = 0:** "too many students ignored this solution" when x is a factor (Edexcel
    2021), and dividing both sides by x loses it (Vaiyavutjamai and Clements; OpenExamPrep, SECONDARY)
    (notes 6.3).
  - **Lesson 3, one square root:** "92% of students recognised the square root of 9 was 3, but none
    recognised it could have two solutions" (Norton review), and the single answer x = 3 was the
    expected error (Vaiyavutjamai and Clements) (notes 6.3).
  - **Lesson 3, signs of the roots:** "the factors contain +2 and +5, but the solutions are their
    negatives" (Mr Barton, SECONDARY, notes 6.3).
  - **Lesson 3, the two x's:** students may think two x's in one equation "should take different
    values" (Vaiyavutjamai and Clements, notes 6.3). One screen shows both brackets use the same x.
  - **Lessons 3 and 5, treating it as linear:** "Large proportions of the sample attempted to rearrange
    quadratic equations as if they were linear equations" (Norton review, notes 6.3), and "trying to
    solve the equation as if it were linear" (SQA 2023, notes 6.5).
  - **Lesson 3, factorising is not solving:** answers like "x = 0, 2, 10" to a factorising question
    (Pearson 2025 report on a copy host, SECONDARY, notes 6.3).
  - **Lesson 4, adding to one side only:** learners added "the square of half the coefficient of x on
    one side of the equation" (Grade 11 study, notes 6.4).
  - **Lesson 4, signs and the process:** completing the square "was often badly handled", with sign
    errors (Edexcel 2005), errors "in transformation and process skill" (Zakaria and others), and a
    negative leading coefficient needing care (Brilliant wiki) (notes 4.4, 6.4).
  - **Lesson 4, not linking the square to the turning point:** "Some candidates did not use their
    answer to part (a) to get the answer to part (b)" (SQA 2024, notes 4.4).
  - **Lesson 4, the turning point's sign:** reading (x − 3)² − 5 as turning at x = −3. Students
    "frequently" predict f(x + 3) moves right (OpenExamPrep, SECONDARY, notes 6.7). The Functions
    course taught the direction, so the puzzle checks it with a point.
  - **Lesson 5, the ± sign:** "the most common error here was to omit the ± sign" (Edexcel 2021), and
    "difficulties in replacing the positive and negative sign" (Zakaria and others) (notes 6.3, 6.5).
  - **Lesson 5, the discriminant:** errors "when carrying out the calculations", an "incomplete
    description of the nature of the roots", and "obtaining a negative discriminant and still finding
    roots" (SQA 2023, notes 6.5).
  - **Lesson 5, surds in the answer:** "the inability to extract the factor correct from within the
    square root sign" (Edexcel 2021, notes 6.5). √28 = 2√7 is a reminder from Powers and roots.
  - **Lesson 6, a as a slope:** "two-thirds of the students interviewed identified the parameter a as
    the 'slope' of the parabola" (Ellis and Grinstead, notes 6.6).
  - **Lesson 6, the vertex by setting y to 0:** "this led some students to solve the original quadratic
    function set equal to 0" (Childers, notes 6.6).
  - **Lesson 6, a parabola drawn like a line, and one form only:** some students drew parabolas "similar
    to the linear function" (Turkish study, notes 6.6). Students "preferred to work with the standard
