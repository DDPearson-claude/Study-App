    "text": "A bracket keeps what is inside it together as one number, so 3(x + 5) means 3 lots of the whole of x + 5. [[Expanding]] the bracket writes it without the bracket, and the number outside, the [[factor]], multiplies each term inside, not just the first.[^1] Picture x + 5 as an x-bar with 5 squares on its end. Stack three of them and you have a rectangle 3 high and x + 5 long. Its left part is 3 rows of x, which is 3x, and its right part is 3 rows of 5, which is 15. So 3(x + 5) = 3x + 15. At x = 4, 3(4 + 5) = 3 × 9 = 27, and 3x + 15 = 12 + 15 = 27.\n\nThe common slip is to multiply the x and forget the 5, writing 3x + 5. At x = 2 that makes 11, while 3(x + 5) makes 21. A letter outside works the same way. R = R₀(1 + αΔT) gives the resistance R after a temperature change ΔT, where R₀ is the original resistance.[^2] Its bracket expands the same way,[^1] to R₀ + R₀αΔT. The first part is the original resistance and the second is the extra resistance from warming. For a winding of 2.0 Ω cold and 2.4 Ω after a run, that extra part is 0.4 Ω.\n\nA minus in front of a bracket multiplies every term inside by −1, so every sign inside changes: −(6x − 5) = −6x + 5. The rectangle cannot show this, because an area is never negative, so try a number instead. At x = 2, 6x − 5 makes 7, so −(6x − 5) makes −7, and −6x + 5 makes −12 + 5 = −7. The slip −6x − 5 makes −17. In the same way x − (a − b) is x − a + b, because the minus must be multiplied into both terms in the bracket.[^3]\n\n**Expanding multiplies the factor outside by every term inside the bracket,[^1] so 3(x + 5) = 3x + 15, and a minus in front changes every sign inside.[^3]**"
