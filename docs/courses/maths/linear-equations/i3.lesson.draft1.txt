    "text": "[[Factorising]] is expanding run backwards.[^1] Expanding turned 3(2x + 5) into 6x + 15. Factorising starts from 6x + 15 and finds the bracket again. Picture the rectangle from Expanding brackets with its two parts given, a 6x part and a 15 part, and its sides missing. The parts sit side by side, so they have the same height, and that height has to divide both of them. 6x is 3 rows of 2x and 15 is 3 rows of 5, so a height of 3 fits both, and the rectangle is 3 high and 2x + 5 long. So 6x + 15 = 3(2x + 5). A factor that divides every term like this is a [[common factor]].\n\nSome terms share more than one factor, and you take out the biggest, the largest factor that divides evenly into all of them.[^2] 12x and 18 share 2, 3 and 6. 2(6x + 9) is true, but 6x and 9 still share a 3, so it is not finished. Take out 6 and you get 6(2x + 3), since 12x ÷ 6 = 2x and 18 ÷ 6 = 3. A minus stays with its term, so 6x − 15 = 3(2x − 5). To check, expand it back, multiplying the factor outside by each term inside,[^4] and you should get the start again: 3 × 2x = 6x and 3 × −5 = −15. Expanding does not change the value of an expression,[^3] so trying a number checks it too. At x = 4, 6x − 15 makes 9 and 3(2x − 5) makes 3 × 3 = 9.\n\nWhen the factor is a whole term, dividing that term by it leaves 1, and the 1 must stay. 3x + 3 = 3(x + 1), because 3 is 3 × 1. Without it, 3(x) is only 3x, which loses the 3. Letters work the same way. R = R₀(1 + αΔT) gives the resistance R after a temperature change ΔT, where R₀ is the original resistance.[^5] Expanded, it was R₀ + R₀αΔT. Both terms have R₀ in them, and R₀ is R₀ × 1, so taking R₀ out leaves 1 + αΔT. For an example winding of 2.0 Ω cold and 2.4 Ω after a run, 2.0 + 0.4 = 2.0 × 1.2. In the same way, with a current I through resistors R₁, R₂ and R₃ in series, V = IR₁ + IR₂ + IR₃ = I(R₁ + R₂ + R₃).[^6] With an example 2 A through 3, 5 and 2 Ω, the three parts make 6 + 10 + 4 = 20 V, and I(R₁ + R₂ + R₃) makes 2 × 10 = 20 V.\n\n**Factorising is the reverse of expanding:[^1] take the biggest common factor out of every term,[^2] put what is left of each term in the bracket, keeping any 1, and expand it back to check.**"

    "text": "- Find the biggest factor that divides every term, numbers and letters.\n- Write it outside the bracket.\n- Divide each term by it and write what is left inside, sign and all. A term equal to the factor leaves 1.\n- Look inside: if the terms there still share a factor, take that out too.\n- Expand it back, and try a number in the start and the result.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** If expanding your answer does not give the start again, it is wrong.\n\n**Worked example:** 18x − 12. 18 and 12 share 2, 3 and 6, so take out 6. 18x ÷ 6 = 3x and −12 ÷ 6 = −2, so 6(3x − 2). Expand it back: 6 × 3x = 18x and 6 × −2 = −12. As a check, with x = 2 the start makes 36 − 12 = 24, and 6(3x − 2) makes 6 × 4 = 24.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Dividing only the first term, so 4x + 20 becomes 4(x + 20).
