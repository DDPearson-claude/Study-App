    "text": "A job in words becomes an equation when you write what one part is and then how many parts there are, so a job made of identical two-part pieces gets a bracket. Say it in one sentence first:[^1] two cable runs, each x metres plus a 3 m tail, use 40 m in all. One run is x + 3, and the bracket keeps that run together, so two runs are 2(x + 3), and 2(x + 3) = 40. Writing 2x + 3 would count the tail once, though each run has its own. Undo it as in Equations with brackets: 40 ÷ 2 = 20, then 20 − 3 = 17, so x = 17. Put it back in to check:[^5] 2(17 + 3) = 2 × 20 = 40. An invoice works the same way. Two jobs, each a £60 call-out plus h hours at £40 an hour, come to £360, so 2(60 + 40h) = 360. Halve it to £180 for one job, take off the £60 call-out to leave £120, and £120 ÷ £40 is 3 hours.\n\nA formula with a bracket is undone in the same way. R = R₀(1 + αΔT) gives the resistance R after a temperature change ΔT, where R₀ is the original resistance.[^3] Expanded, it is R = R₀ + R₀αΔT, so each degree adds α × R₀. α is the [[temperature coefficient]], and for copper it is 3.9 × 10⁻³ per °C, which is 0.0039.[^6] As a flow diagram, ΔT is multiplied by α, then 1 is added, then the whole bracket is multiplied by R₀. Say a 2.0 Ω winding warms by 50 °C: 0.0039 × 50 = 0.195, the bracket is 1.195, and R = 2.0 × 1.195 = 2.39 Ω. The bracket is one number, the factor the resistance grows by. Handling a process like this as one object is a shift many learners find hard,[^2] and here the object is the whole bracket. To make R₀ the [[subject]], the letter on its own on one side, divide both sides by the whole bracket: R₀ = R ÷ (1 + αΔT), and 2.39 ÷ 1.195 = 2.0. Dividing by the αΔT part alone leaves out the 1, which R₀ multiplied too.\n\nTo make ΔT the subject, undo the flow diagram from the right. Divide both sides by R₀, which leaves R ÷ R₀ = 1 + αΔT. Take 1 from both sides, then divide by α: ΔT = (R ÷ R₀ − 1) ÷ α. A winding that reads 2.0 Ω cold and 2.4 Ω after a run gives 2.4 ÷ 2.0 = 1.2, then 1.2 − 1 = 0.2, and 0.2 ÷ 0.0039 is about 51 °C. Taking the 1 off first is the slip to watch, because R₀ multiplies the 1 as well: (2.4 − 1) ÷ 2.0 = 0.7 gives about 179 °C. The rule fits changes of about 100 °C or less,[^7] and 51 °C is inside that.\n\nThe same shape turns up in thermal expansion. A rod or pipe warmed from t₀ to t₁ grows to L₁ = L₀ + L₀α(t₁ − t₀),[^8] where t₁ − t₀ is the temperature change, like ΔT, and α is now how much the metal grows for each degree, a different number. L₀ is a common factor, so it is L₁ = L₀(1 + α(t₁ − t₀)), and L₀ = L₁ ÷ (1 + α(t₁ − t₀)), just as for R₀. The course's puzzle is exactly the winding worked above, so its 2.0 Ω and 2.4 Ω answer as a warm-up of about 51 °C. **Write one part of the job and multiply it by how many parts there are, undo a formula from the outside in with its bracket divided as one whole thing, and put the answer back in to check it.[^5]**"
