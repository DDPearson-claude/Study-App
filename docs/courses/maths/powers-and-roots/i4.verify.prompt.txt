  "text": "A power that is a fraction is a root, for any positive number. The bottom number of the fraction says which root to take and the top number says what power to raise it to, so 8^(1/3) is the cube root of 8, which is 2, and 27^(5/3) is the cube root of 27, which is 3, raised to the power 5, which is 243.\n\nStart from lesson 1's adding law. On the power ladder, rungs 1, 8, 64 and 512 are 8⁰, 8¹, 8² and 8³, and each step up multiplies by 8. Now cut one step into three equal small steps, each adding ⅓ to the power. Adding ⅓ to a power multiplies by 8^(1/3), whichever rung you start from, so every small step multiplies by the same number. Three of them make one whole step, because multiplying adds the powers and ⅓ + ⅓ + ⅓ = 1, so 8^(1/3) × 8^(1/3) × 8^(1/3) = 8¹ = 8.[^1] So 8^(1/3) is the number that, used three times in a multiplication, makes 8. That is 2, because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, so 8^(1/3) = ∛8 = 2, the cube root of lesson 3. The power-of-a-power law agrees: (8^(1/3))³ = 8^(⅓ × 3) = 8¹, which is why a fraction as a power is defined as a root.[^2][^3] Cut a step into two instead and 4^(1/2) × 4^(1/2) = 4¹, so 4^(1/2) = √4 = 2. Cut it into four and you get the [[fourth root]]: 16^(1/4) = 2, because 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. The bottom of the fraction counts the equal small steps in one whole step, so it is the root.\n\nThe top number counts the small steps you climb. 8^(2/3) is two small steps of × 2, so 8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 4.
