## What the interactive must show

The rule (the app shows it just above the frame, under "What you're looking at"): A short lesson in thirteen small screens on the staircase of powers. Each stair is one power of a base, and beside it the amount is drawn as unit squares with its value written under it, as in the factor tiles of lesson 1, where 4³ is three 4s multiplied. On the first screen a stepper runs the power down from 3 to −3 with base 4. 4³ is 64 squares, 4² is 16 and 4¹ is 4: each step down takes one 4 out of the multiplying, so it divides by 4, and the sum ÷ 4 is shown beside every step. At 4⁰ one whole square is left, because 4 ÷ 4 = 1. Below that the single square is cut into 4 equal parts with one shaded, which is 1/4, then into 16 with one shaded, 1/16, then into 64, 1/64, as the Fractions course draws a fraction. The amount shrinks at every step but stays above nothing, so a negative power of these bases is a small amount, not a negative one. Other screens use base 2, 3, 7 or 10 the same way; with base 10 the stairs show numbers only, because 1000 squares, or a square cut into 1000 parts, are too small to see. On screen 9 a jump arrow counts the steps a multiplication takes: × 2⁻⁵ divides by 2 five times, so it is five steps down. Screens 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 show an idea, screens 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 ask you to try it, and screens 11 to 13 mix them. A puzzle offers three answers, and a wrong pick draws its amount beside the right one, or puts 2 in for x, so the two can be seen to differ.
What the model leaves out (the app shows this in its own panel; do not repeat it on the page): At the top of the staircase the squares are drawn smaller so that 64 fit on the screen, and below 4⁰ the one square is drawn larger so its parts can be seen; the values under them are exact. With one, the answer can be negative, as in (−2)⁻¹ = −1/2, but that minus comes from the base, not the power. Squares cannot show a base between 0 and 1, such as a half: dividing by a half doubles, so its stairs climb as the power goes down, and a negative power gives a large amount, though still not a negative one. A base of zero has no stairs below 0¹, because each step down would divide by zero. A power between whole numbers, such as a half, sits between the stairs, and lesson 4 finds what it means. The puzzles' numbers are examples.

## The explanation Dan reads after playing (use the same words for the same things)
Each step down in power divides by the base, so a zero power is 1 and a negative power is one over the matching positive power: 4⁻² is 1/16, a small amount, not a negative one. Start from the tiles of lesson 1. 4³ is three 4s multiplied, 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. Take one 4 out of the multiplying and you have 4² = 16, because taking out a factor of 4 divides by 4. So walking down the staircase of powers, 4³, 4², 4¹, gives 64, 16, 4, dividing by 4 at every step.

Now keep walking, dividing by 4 each time. 4¹ is 4, and 4 ÷ 4 = 1, so 4⁰ = 1, one whole square. The dividing law from lesson 1 agrees: 4² ÷ 4² is 16 ÷ 16 = 1, and taking the powers away gives the power 2 − 2, which is 4⁰. So any base to the power of zero equals 1, as long as the base is not zero, because the step down divides by the base and you cannot divide by zero. It is not 0: from 7¹ = 7 the step down divides by 7, leaving 1, where 0 would mean taking all 7 away. One more step: 1 ÷ 4 = 1/4, so 4⁻¹ = 1/4. The next is 1/4 ÷ 4 = 1/16, so 4⁻² = 1/16. Since 16 is 4², that is 1 over 4². This is the rule: a negative power gives the reciprocal, one over the number with the matching positive power. The dividing law agrees again: 4² ÷ 4⁴ is 16/256 = 1/16, and 2 − 4 = −2.

Dividing a positive amount by 4 makes it smaller but never takes it below nothing, so the minus in the power does not make the answer negative. It is a common slip …

## The house kit (complete API reference)
You write the BODY of one page: HTML, an optional `<style>`, then one inline `<script>` ending
with `K.ready()`. It runs in a sandboxed iframe on Dan's phone (about 340 px wide, touch) and
laptop, with the global `K` and the kit's stylesheet. No network or storage: no web
addresses, `fetch`, `localStorage`, `alert` or `prompt`.

### Page shape that works
1. `<p class="lead">`: one line saying what is drawn (the app adds title and action).
2. The main visual with its controls right under it: `K.stage('#scene', '#controls')` keeps the
   pair on one phone screen. A choice that re-sorts the picture may sit above it.
3. `<p class="say">`: one sentence that changes with the state and says why. Then
   `<div class="k-readouts">` for numbers the drawing does not label.
4. Secondary figures and anything that grows go below.
5. `<p class="caption">`: one short sentence, at most 20 words: where the numbers come from ("A

### Keep the answer hidden until Dan moves
Dan predicts before he plays: open on a setting the question is not about, and hide whatever
gives the answer away until his first move:
- class `k-after-move` on any element (a mark, an arrow, a sentence): hidden, its space kept.
  Hide the answer, not the controls or a whole figure (a blank hole fails the test).
- `K.readout({..., afterMove: true})` shows "?" until then; plot series and marks take `afterMove: true` too.
- `K.moved` is true from then on: in `K.update`, write `K.moved ? answer : question`.

### The pipeline
Controls register themselves; their values form `params` = `{id: value}`.
```
K.model(fn)        fn(params) -> outputs {name: number | string}. Pure: no DOM, no lists or objects.
K.update(fn)       fn(params, outputs): draw everything here. More than one is allowed.
K.at(over)         -> outputs of the model at those params (others take each control's starting value)
K.check(label, fn, {source?})   fn() must return exactly true
K.ready()          call once, last: draws the opening state and runs the checks
K.complete()       at the lesson's end
K.params()  K.outputs()  K.refresh()   (refresh reruns the pipeline after you change your own state)
```
A readout whose id is an output key updates itself.

### Controls
Every control takes `into` (a selector or element). Ids are unique; use the lesson's ids exactly.
```
K.control({id, label, min, max, step, value, unit?, prefix?, fmt?, log?, snap?, hint?, into}) -> {el, get(), set(v)}
K.choice({id, label, options: [value | {value, label}], value, hint?, into}) -> {el, get, set}
K.toggle({id, label, value: false, hint?, into}) -> {el, get, set}
```

`control` is a big slider with − / + buttons. Keep `unit` short; `fmt(v)` returns the whole text;

### Example
```html
<p class="lead">Lengthen the string and watch each swing take longer.</p>
<div id="plot"></div>
<div class="k-controls" id="controls"></div>
<p class="say" id="say"></p>
<div class="k-readouts" id="outs"></div>
<p class="caption">Small swings only; g = 9.81 m/s² is gravity at the Earth's surface.</p>
<script>
  const g = 9.81;
  const period = (L) => 2 * Math.PI * Math.sqrt(L / g);
  K.control({ id: 'L', label: 'String length', min: 0.1, max: 3, step: 0.05, value: 1, unit: 'm', snap: [2], into: '#controls' });
  K.stage('#plot', '#controls');
  K.readout({ id: 'T', label: 'One full swing takes', unit: 's', decimals: 2, into: '#outs' });
  const plot = K.plot('#plot', { x: { min: 0, max: 3, label: 'Length (m)' }, y: { min: 0, max: 4, label: 'Period (s)' } });
  K.model((p) => ({ T: period(p.L) }));
  K.update((p, o) => {
    plot.draw({ series: [{ fn: period }], marks: [{ x: p.L, y: o.T, guides: true }] });
    document.getElementById('say').textContent = !K.moved ? 'Make the string four times longer: how much longer is each swing?'
      : 'A ' + K.fmt(p.L, { decimals: 2 }) + ' m string swings there and back every ' + K.fmt(o.T, { decimals: 2 }) + ' s. Four times the length only doubles that.';
  });
  K.check('No length, no swing time', () => K.near(period(0), 0));
```

  const ELEMENTS = [

    mass: { groupOf: (e) => e.mass < 30 ? 'Lighter atoms' : 'Heavier atoms', order: ['Lighter atoms', 'Heavier atoms'] },
    column: { groupOf: (e) => 'Column ' + e.column, order: ['Column 1', 'Column 17', 'Column 18'] },
  };
  const groupsFor = (by) => SORTS[by].order.map((g) => ({ name: g, members: ELEMENTS.filter((e) => SORTS[by].groupOf(e) === g) }));
  // Pairs of group-mates, and how many of them share a behaviour.
  function pairs(by) {

    }[p.by];
