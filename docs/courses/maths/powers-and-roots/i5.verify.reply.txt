   "now": "Multiplying by a power of ten slides the digits past a fixed decimal point, one place for each 10, and each power of ten you are likely to meet that is a multiple of 3, other than 0, has a short name in front of the unit, so 0.0047 A is 4.7 × 10⁻³ A, or 4.7 mA. Start from place value. In 4.7 the 4 stands for four units and the 7 for seven tenths. Multiply by 10 and each digit is worth ten times as much, which is exactly what the place on its left is worth, so every digit slides one place left and the decimal point stays where it is. 10³ is three 10s multiplied, as in lesson 1, so 4.7 × 10³ slides the digits three places left, to 4700, and zeros hold the empty places. A negative power gives one over the number,[^3] so 10⁻³ is one over 1000, a thousandth, and 4.7 × 10⁻³ slides the digits three places right, to 0.0047.\n\nBecause the digits can slide either way, one number can be written many ways: 0.0047 is 47 × 10⁻⁴, 4.7 × 10⁻³ and 0.47 × 10⁻². Make the front number 10 times bigger and the power must drop by one to keep the same number, so a bigger front number goes with a smaller power. Move the power the same way as the front number and the number changes: 470 × 10⁻⁵ is 0.0047, but 4.7 × 10⁻⁷ is 0.00000047, ten thousand times smaller. [[Standard form]] settles on one of these: a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten.[^4] Textbooks add that 1 is allowed and 10 is not, so 0.0047 is 4.7 × 10⁻³, and 47 × 10⁻⁴, with two figures before the point, is the same number but not standard form.\n\nIn electrical work the power is usually kept to a multiple of 3, because each of those you are likely to meet, other than 0, has a [[prefix]], a letter in front of the unit that stands for that power of ten. Milli, m, is a thousandth, 10⁻³,[^1] so 4.7 × 10⁻³ A is 4.7 mA. Textbooks add that kilo, k, is 10³, mega, M, is 10⁶ and micro, µ, is 10⁻⁶, and further out giga is 10⁹, nano 10⁻⁹ and pico 10⁻¹². NIST's guide to the SI reports a common recommendation: use only the prefixes for powers of ten that are multiples of 3, chosen so the number in front is between 0.1 and 1000.[^2] Textbooks call writing a number with the power a multiple of 3 and 1 up to 1000 in front [[engineering notation]]. So the prefix changes only at every third place: 4.7 mA, 47 mA, 470 mA, then 4.7 A. Centi, as in cm, is 10⁻²: a prefix, but not one engineering notation uses. A prefix is a power of ten, so lesson 1's laws work on it. Multiplying adds the powers:[^5] 2 mA × 5 kΩ is 2 × 10⁻³ × 5 × 10³ = 10 × 10⁰, and any base to the power of zero equals 1,[^6] so that is 10 V. Dividing takes the powers away: 9 V ÷ 1.5 kΩ is 9 ÷ 1.5 = 6 with 10⁰ ÷ 10³ = 10⁻³, so 6 × 10⁻³ A = 6 mA. Adding is different, because only the front numbers add, and only when the powers match: 3 × 10⁴ + 2 × 10³ = 30 × 10³ + 2 × 10³ = 32 × 10³ = 3.2 × 10⁴, which is 30,000 + 2,000 = 32,000. Adding the powers instead gives 5 × 10⁷, which is 50,000,000.\n\n**Multiplying by 10ⁿ slides the digits n places, left for a positive n and right for a negative one, and each power you are likely to meet that is a multiple of 3, other than 0, has a prefix, so 0.0047 A = 4.7 × 10⁻³ A = 4.7 mA.[^1]**"
