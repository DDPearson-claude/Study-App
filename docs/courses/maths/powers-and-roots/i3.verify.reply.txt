The square root asks the same question backwards: given the area, what side made it? The root sign writes that side exactly.[^2] Areas such as 1, 4, 9, 16 and 25, each a whole number times itself, are [[square number]]s, and their roots are whole. Between them the side still exists. Take a 2 by 2 square of four unit squares and cut each one along a diagonal, so the cuts make a tilted square in the middle. The tilted square keeps half of each unit square, so its area is half of 4, which is 2, and its side, one unit square's diagonal, is √2. A calculator gives √3 as 1.73 to two places, but 1.73 × 1.73 = 2.9929, not 3, so the decimal is close but not exact.[^1] Textbooks add that the decimals of √2 and √3 never end and never repeat, so no decimal is exactly right. Writing the side as √3 m is exact, and an expression with a root sign in it like this is a [[surd]].[^3] The same works in three directions: a cube of volume 8 has edges of 2, because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, so the [[cube root]] of 8, ∛8, is 2.\n\nA surd can often be made simpler by taking out a square-number factor. 48 = 16 × 3, so a square of area 48 can be cut into 16 small squares of area 3, set 4 by 4. Each small square has a side of √3, and four of them line each edge, so √48 = 4√3. Squaring checks it: a bracket powers every factor (lesson 1), so (4√3)² = 4² × (√3)² = 16 × 3 = 48. Only a square-number factor comes out of a square root, because only a square number of equal small squares, 4, 9, 16 and so on, can be set out as a square: 48 = 8 × 6 would need 8 small squares, and 8 cannot make a square.
