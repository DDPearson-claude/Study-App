A square millimetre is a square 1 mm along each side, and its area is side times side, 1 mm × 1 mm. Each side is 10⁻³ m, a thousandth of a metre,[^1] so the area is 10⁻³ m × 10⁻³ m. Multiply the numbers and the units separately. The numbers are powers of the same base, so their indices add,[^4] and −3 add −3 makes −6, giving 10⁻⁶. The units multiply too, and m × m is m². So 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², a millionth of a square metre.\n\nZoom into a square metre and you can count it. Along one side 1000 mm fit, so the [[length factor]], how many small units fit along one metre, is 1000. The square fills with 1000 rows of 1000 small squares, so 1 m² = 1,000,000 mm², which is 10⁶ mm². You met the same count for 1 cm² = 100 mm² in Geometry fundamentals, 10 rows of 10; the same count works for any prefix. The length factor counts in two directions, across and down, so an area conversion squares it, and a cube adds a third direction, depth, so a volume conversion cubes it.[^5] Centi is 10⁻²,[^2] so 1 cm³ = (10⁻² m)³ = 10⁻⁶ m³: 100 cm along each edge of a cubic metre, and 100 × 100 × 100 = 1,000,000 small cubes.\n\nTwo easy slips come from using the length factor only once. One says a square metre holds 1000 mm², when 1000 is only the count along one edge: 1000 mm² is the first row alone, a strip 1 mm wide, and 999 more rows are still to fill. The other reads cm² as a hundredth of a square metre, as if the centi sat outside the square. NIST's guide to the SI rules that out: the prefix and the unit are one inseparable symbol, and the power belongs to both.[^3] So 1 cm² is (10⁻² m)² = 10⁻⁴ m². The rule holds for a negative power too,[^3] which is how a unit you divide by is written, as dividing is multiplying by one over. A load of 1 N/mm² is 1 newton on every square millimetre.
