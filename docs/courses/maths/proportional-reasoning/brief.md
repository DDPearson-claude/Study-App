## 1. The overall topic
Comparing amounts by multiplying, not by subtracting. It covers:
- a ratio, and its order
- scaling a ratio up and down
- sharing an amount in a ratio
- a rate per one of something
- the two ways two quantities can move together: in step (direct) or one up as the other comes
  down (inverse)

It is taught with blocks, tables and number lines, before any algebra. Brilliant, Khan, Oak,
NCETM and Bitesize all teach proportion this way, and algebra comes later (research notes 1.1 to 1.5).

**It builds on:**
- Fractions: equivalent fractions become equivalent ratios.
- Percentages: a multiplier scales an amount.
- Negative numbers lesson 3: absolute = gauge + atmospheric, at 1.013 bar.

**Left for later courses:** graphs of proportion and y = kx (The coordinate plane), and solving
for an unknown (Solving equations).

## 2. The lessons, in the order they build
| # | Lesson | The skill Dan leaves with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ratios as blocks | Read 3 : 2 as blocks: three of one for every two of the other. The order matters (a 10 : 1 transformer is not 1 : 10), and both parts need the same units. Tell part : part (3 : 2) from part : whole (3 out of 5). |
| 2 | Equivalent ratios and scaling | Scale a ratio by multiplying both parts by the same number on a ratio table, never by adding the same number. Simplify by dividing. A scale drawing at 1 : 100 (1 cm to 1 m) is the worked context. |
| 3 | Sharing in a ratio | Share an amount in a ratio with a bar model: add the parts for the total number of blocks, find one block, then each share. |
| 4 | Unit rates | Turn a rate into "per one": per hour, per kWh, per turn. Then use it to scale. A rate holds only where it is steady: an engine's fuel per kWh rises at low load. |
| 5 | Direct proportion | Spot when two amounts are in direct proportion (double one, the other doubles) and use the multiplicative link on a table. A transformer's secondary voltage follows its primary. Not every link that rises steadily is proportional: a fixed charge plus a rate is not. |
| 6 | Inverse proportion | One up, the other down, with the product fixed: double one and the other halves. Boyle's law (pressure × volume stays the same, at absolute pressure) is the main context. A transformer's current is the inverse of its voltage (10 : 1 on voltage is 1 : 10 on current), and a reduction gearbox is a ratio of speeds. |

**Folded in, not lessons of their own:**
- **Scale drawings** go into lesson 2.
- **Ratios in machines** become worked cases in lessons 1, 2, 4 and 6: a transformer's turns ratio, a diesel's compression ratio (a ratio of two volumes), a gearbox's reduction and fuel per kWh.

**If you want it shorter:** lessons 3 and 5 are the ones to merge or drop.

## 3. Can it be taught mostly visually, through interaction?
Yes. Steps lessons, as before, with minimal writing:
- **Blocks:** drag to add blocks to one side and watch the ratio and the total change. In lesson 3 the
  bar becomes a whole cut into equal blocks, and each share is read off it.
- **A ratio table:** tap ×2 or ×3 to scale both rows at once. Adding the same number to both
  rows visibly breaks the mix, which shows the additive mistake for itself.
- **A double number line:** two scales locked together, used for scale drawings, rates and direct
  proportion.
- **The fixed-area rectangle:** drag one side and the other follows so the area stays the same.
  The same picture shows a gas cylinder with its piston: halve the volume and the absolute pressure doubles.
  It drives lesson 6.
- **Colour as before:** the two quantities in a ratio are told apart by solid ink and a light tint,
  with their names on the blocks; never red or green, which keep their meanings.

## 4. What else
- **Benchmark (notes 1).**
  - Brilliant's course has 36 lessons in 8 levels. It sets up ratios, then equivalent ratios, then
    scaling, then unit rates, and leaves graphs and equations until levels 6 and 7. Its lesson list
    has no inverse proportion; that is read from the list, not stated by Brilliant.
  - Khan puts ratios and rates at unit 3, with proportional relationships at unit 9.
  - Oak's Year 7 runs multiplier, then double number line, then ratio table, then sharing.
  - Bitesize teaches KS3 direct proportion as "multiplicative links" and inverse as
    "multiplicative reasoning".
- **Starting point.** Dan already works with ratios as a matter of course: transformer ratings,
  drawings, mixes. So the course moves quickly through what a ratio is, and spends its time on the
  two slips (adding, and inverse treated as direct).
- **His aim (notes 2.1).** The MCA General Engineering Science I syllabus asks him to "Solve problems on
  ratio, proportion and percentage". It also asks for "efficiency, mechanical advantage and velocity
  ratio" and gauge-to-absolute conversion. GES II names Boyle's law. These syllabuses belong to the
  MCA's certificates of competency, not the HNC/HND itself; no HN unit line naming ratio was found.
- **Where it is used (each cited before a lesson uses it).**
  - **Transformer turns ratio:** it equals the voltage ratio, and 10 : 1 on voltage is 1 : 10 on current (notes 2.2).
  - **Marine reduction gearboxes:** "usually between 2:1 and 3:1" on small marine diesels (Yachting Monthly, a magazine). There is also a US Navy manual case: 1300 rpm in, 280 rpm out (notes 2.3).
  - **Compression ratio:** a ratio of volumes (Britannica), with diesels at 14 : 1 to 24 : 1 (notes 2.4).
  - **Specific fuel consumption:** 155 to 225 g/kWh at good load (a consultancy blog, so labelled as such; notes 2.5).
  - **Boyle's law:** it uses absolute pressure (Engineering ToolBox, and a Cambridge University Press worked step; notes 2.7).
  - **Scale drawings:** 1 : 100 is 1 cm to 1 m (Bitesize). Their use on electrical or ship drawings is not sourced, so no screen claims it.
  - **Mixing ratios:** the trade use is not sourced, so lesson 3 uses mixes without saying "on site you will...".
- **The mistakes to catch, each with a puzzle and its own hint.**
  - **Lesson 1, order:** reading a ratio backwards. Only the electrical source names this, for turns ratios (notes 3.4).
  - **Lessons 1 and 3, the whole:** part : part taken as part : whole. "Confusion about the whole" was the most frequent misconception in one study (notes 3.2). Reading 1 : 4 as four parts in all is the likely form, but no source names it, so it is a check rather than a sourced mistake.
  - **Lesson 2, adding instead of multiplying:** the best-documented slip. NCETM says even older students "think additively by default" (notes 3.1).
  - **Lesson 5, a straight line that is not proportional:** treating it as if it were. "It is a common misconception that all linear relationships are proportional" (NCETM, notes 3.3).
  - **Lesson 6, inverse treated as direct:** a listed error, and pupils do worse on inverse tasks (notes 3.3).
- **Size and cost.** 6 lessons of about 12 screens on the agreed settings, at about 1.05M tokens
  a lesson (measured on Percentages), so about 6.3M. Add about 0.3M for the plan, and the course
  research, which can start from this brief's 21 saved search results.
- **Gaps to close at build time.**
  - A source that a reduction gearbox raises torque as it lowers speed. It is not sourced yet, so lesson 6 uses speed only unless one is found.
  - An HN maths unit line naming ratio or proportion.
  - A maker's datasheet for fuel per kWh, if a better source than the blog is wanted.
