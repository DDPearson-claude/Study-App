### 1.6 What is core (consensus across the five)
All five agree on these skills, in about this order (my reading; the list is not a quote):
1. What a percentage is (per 100) and its link to fractions and decimals (Brilliant L1/L3, Khan unit 4, Bitesize "What are percentages?", Oak 1-2, NCETM Y6).
2. A percentage of an amount (all five).
3. One number as a percentage of another, "what percent is it?" (Khan "Finding percents", Bitesize GCSE page 3, Oak 5, NCETM 3.1.5.1).
4. Increase and decrease, with the single multiplier (Brilliant L2/L4, Bitesize, Oak 6-8, NCETM 3.1.5.2-3).
5. Percentage change from start and finish (Khan 7th grade, Bitesize, Oak 11, NCETM 3.1.5.5).
6. Reverse percentages (Brilliant L4, Bitesize, Oak 9-10, NCETM 3.1.5.4).
7. Repeated change / compound (Brilliant L5, Bitesize GCSE "multiplier method"). NCETM lists no repeated-change key idea in 3.1.5.

Simple/compound interest appears in Khan, Bitesize and Brilliant, but it is finance, not core for this route.
Not found as a separate lesson in any of the five: percentage points. It shows up only as a misconception (section 3).

### 2.1 Voltage drop limits, 3% lighting and 5% other uses (BS 7671)
- **Status: PARTLY CONFIRMED.** BS 7671 itself is paywalled, so I could not read the regulation text. The best original-publisher page I found is an IET forum thread (IET's own site, but a member's post, not the IET's published guidance).
- **Source:** Volt Drop - Wiring and the Regulations BS 7671 - IET EngX. https://engx.theiet.org/f/wiring-and-regulations/21643/volt-drop
  Quote (member "broadgage"): "The total voltage drop should not normally exceed 5% for power and 3% for lighting. It is up to the designer as to how this is apportioned between the sub main and the final circuits."
  Quote (member "AJJewsbury"): "if your submain fed lighting you might need to achieve a maximum of 3% voltage drop overall - so you might for example have to share the 3% (or 6.9V) between the submain and final circuit - perhaps 1% for the final circuit and 2% for the submain"
  Quote (same member, a nuance worth teaching): "the 3%, 5% etc in the regulations are simple 'deemed to comply' values - it is permitted to use other (larger) values if you know the appliances will cope with the resulting (low) voltage."
  Quote (same member, base): "use the single phase voltage drop figures against the percentage of 230V."
- **Source (SECONDARY, not original):** Voltage Drop Calculator BS 7671 | ecalpro.com. https://ecalpro.com/it/calculators/voltage-drop/bs-7671
  Quote: "Lighting circuits: 3% of nominal voltage (6.9V at 230V). Other circuits (power): 5% of nominal voltage (11.5V at 230V)."
  This site says Regulation 525.1 and Appendix 4; another secondary site (ecalpro blog) says Appendix 12; they disagree, so **the exact BS 7671 clause number is UNCONFIRMED**. Do not print a clause number in the course until it is checked against the book. The 6.9 V and 11.5 V figures are just 3% and 5% of 230 V, so the course can compute them from the shown rule.
  The same site says "Regulation 525.201 allows increased limits where the supply is from a private LV supply (e.g., generator): 6% for lighting and 8% for other uses". **UNCONFIRMED**, one secondary source; relevant to ships' generators, so worth checking before use.

### 2.5 Propeller slip

## 3. Teaching: common mistakes and good visual models

### 3.1 Common mistakes

**a) A 20% rise then a 20% fall is "no change".**
- **Source:** Common ways percentages can mislead you. Math Open Reference. https://mathopenref.com/percenterrors.html
  Quote: "Percentages do not cancel out. If I increase something by say 20% then reduce it by 20% you might expect to get back to the number you started with. But you don't!. For example, if I have 100 items and reduce that by 20% I get 80 items: Now if I increase 80 by 20% I get 96: So I end up with 96 items, not 100. Why? Because in the first percentage I am taking 20% of 100 which is 20. In the second percentage I am taking 20% of 80 which is 16."
- **Source:** Applications of Percentages. Brilliant Wiki (archived). https://brilliant.org/wiki/applications-of-percentages. Quote: "If a man's wages is increased by 10% and afterwards decreased by 10%, what is the total decrease of his wages? The correct answer is: 1%"
- **Source (SECONDARY):** Common Percentage Mistakes, Explained. calculate.studio. https://calculate.studio/en/blog/percentage-mistakes-everyone-makes. Quote: "a 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease returns to 75% of the original value, not 100%, because each percentage is calculated on a different base." Blog, not an education publisher.
- Maths check (computed, not quoted): 100 x 1.2 x 0.8 = 96; to undo +20% you need -1/6, about -16.7%.

**b) Percentage change found from the wrong base.**
- **Source:** Finding the percentage change. Oak National Academy. https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/maths-secondary-ks3/units/understanding-multiplicative-relationships-percentages-and-proportionality/lessons/finding-the-percentage-change
  Quote (Common misconception): "Forgetting to find the percentage change. Emphasise the word change. Encourage pupils to write down the 2 percentages with their relevant values. Go back to the question and calculate the actual change they should notice that this is neither of the 2 values but the difference between them."
  Quote (Teacher tip): "a bank account had a balance of £100, it now has a balance of £120. By how much has the amount changed?' and then move onto percentages."
- **Source:** Percentage Change - BBC Bitesize. https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/articles/zdwntrd. Quote: "Percentage change is the amount that a quantity has changed, expressed as a percentage of the original value."
- **Source:** Percentage change - BBC Bitesize (KS4). https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/articles/zxghrmn. Quote: "Percentage change is calculated by writing the actual change as a fraction of the original amount, and converting to a percentage."
- "Dividing by the new value instead of the original" is named as an error by a commercial calculator site (howdeedo.com: "Common mistake: Dividing by $65 instead of $50. Always divide by the original (base) value."), **SECONDARY**. No education publisher found stating it in those words. Oak and Bitesize teach the right base but do not list this slip.

**c) Reverse percentages done by taking the percentage off the new amount.**
- **UNCONFIRMED as a named misconception.** I found no education publisher that states "taking the % off the new amount" as the common error. What the sources do say is the correct idea:
  - Bitesize: https://www.bbc.co.uk/bitesize/articles/zxghrmn. Quote: "The original amount always represents 100%. If the original amount has increased by 5%, the new amount represents 105% of the original. If the original amount has decreased by 5%, the new amount represents 95% of the original."
  - Oak (lesson "Finding the original amount after an increase"): https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/maths-secondary-ks3/units/understanding-multiplicative-relationships-percentages-and-proportionality/lessons/finding-the-original-amount-after-an-increase. Quote (Key learning): "In all of these representations a single multiplier can be used to find the original amount after a percentage increase". Its listed misconception is a different one (see d).
  - Math Open Reference (see a): needing 33.3% to get back from -25%, shown as "To get back to $1000, I need to know what percentage $250 is of $750, which is one third, or 33.3%." (search excerpt).
  - The mistake itself can still be taught: a screen can show the wrong route (take 20% off 72) next to the check (60 x 1.2 = 72). Mark as "common slip, our observation" in the course, not as a sourced claim.

**d) Wrong multiplier: dividing by 10, not 100.**
- **Source:** Increase by a percentage. Oak National Academy. https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/maths-secondary-ks3/units/understanding-multiplicative-relationships-percentages-and-proportionality/lessons/increase-by-a-percentage
  Quote (Common misconception): "A single digit percentage is incorrectly worked out by dividing by 10 and not 100 e.g 3% = 0.3. This error continues when increasing amounts e.g increase 40 by 3% has a multiplier of 1.3. Remind pupils that to covert a percentage into a decimal we divide by 100. This applies with increase too e.g 120% has a multiplier of 1.2"
  Useful for Dan: the same slip turns 3% of 230 V into 69 V instead of 6.9 V.

**e) Percentage points versus percent.**
- **No education or statistics authority found** (ONS, Bank of England, NCETM, Oak did not turn up in this run). **SECONDARY** sources only:
  - percently.io, 7 Common Percentage Mistakes. https://percently.io/blog/common-percentage-mistakes. Quote: "Saying 'interest rates fell from 5% to 3%, a 2% decrease.' ... This is a 2 percentage point decrease, but a 40% decrease in the rate itself."
  - calculate.studio (as above). Quote: "a rate rising from 5% to 7% is a 2 percentage point increase but a 40% relative increase."
- A clean example from an original source: NEMA says "IEC nameplate efficiency would be 0.3% higher than NEMA motor of same design" (the NEMA table shows 95.8 against 96.1, which is 0.3 percentage points). The NEMA wording itself uses "%" loosely, which is the point. Source in 2.2.
- Percentage points are not a lesson in any benchmark course; treat it as a short note inside the "percentage change" lesson.

## 4. What this suggests for the brief (my inference, not sourced)

The core that every benchmark shares fits five lessons. A first draft:
1. What a percentage is (per 100) and a percentage of an amount, using the 10 by 10 grid and the percentage bar.
2. What percentage is it? (part over whole x 100; efficiency, tolerance, slip as examples).
3. Increase and decrease with a multiplier (x1.2, x0.85; voltage drop limit, VAT).
4. Percentage change, with the base and percentage points as a short note.
5. Reverse percentages and repeated changes (+20% then -20% gives 96).

The roadmap's draft lesson 8 (efficiency, error and slip) can be folded in as the worked contexts for lessons 2 and 4, so the course stays at five. Percentage points have no benchmark lesson, so give them one screen, not a lesson.
