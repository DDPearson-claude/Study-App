  "text": "In the last lesson, e^(−t) kept 0.368 of what was there after each unit of time. A capacitor emptying through a resistor does the same, and the circuit sets the unit of time: R × C, called the [[time constant]]. Charging the capacitor, and a hot part cooling to the room, are the same shrinking gap seen from another side.\n\nStart with the discharge: say a capacitor charged to 10 V, with a resistor across it. The current through the resistor is the voltage left ÷ R, by Ohm's law. Textbooks add that a capacitor's charge is C × its voltage, so its voltage falls by the charge that flows away ÷ C. Halve the voltage and you halve the current, so the voltage falls half as fast. The fall always keeps in proportion to what is left, so each equal stretch of time takes away the same share of it, not the same number of volts: the same factor on every equal step, as in the first lesson. It is the downhill version of growth whose rate is proportional to the current value.[^2] A bigger R lets less current through at the same voltage, and a bigger C must lose more charge for each volt it drops, so both slow it down and the pace is set by their product.
