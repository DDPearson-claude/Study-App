## The idea
Topic: Exponentials and logarithms
Idea: Growth by multiplying (Multiplying by the same factor each equal step, such as × 1.05 for 5% a year, gives A × bˣ, which shrinks instead when b is below 1.)
Kind: quantity
Dan knows a little about this topic. Show an equation on the page only if every symbol in it is labelled on the picture; otherwise say the rule in words.

## What the interactive must show
Brief: The one thing you should see is the multiplying column's rises growing every step while the adding column's stay the same, so it starts behind and then races past, when you step both columns forward.
Title (the app shows it above the frame, so do not repeat it): Add or multiply
The rule (the app shows it just above the frame, under "What you're looking at"): A short lesson in thirteen small screens on two columns of bars over the same equal steps, numbered 0, 1, 2 and on along the bottom. Both columns start from the same bar. The left column adds a fixed amount each step, and each new bar is labelled with the sum that made it (+ 2, + 2, …). The right column multiplies by a fixed factor each step, labelled the same way (× 2, × 2, …). A step button moves both on together, and the rise from each bar to the next is marked, so the left column's rises stay equal while the right column's grow, because each one is a fixed share of a bigger bar. Each bar drops a dot onto a graph below, steps along the bottom and the value up the side: the adding dots lie on a straight line and the multiplying dots curve. Sliders set the amount and the factor, and the rule is written beside them as A × bˣ, where A is the start, b the growth factor and x the number of steps. Set the factor below 1 and the right column shrinks each step, by less each time, towards the bottom line but never onto it, because a share of a positive bar is still positive. A switch fills in the curve between the dots, where x is a fraction of a step: half a step multiplies by the square root of the factor, because two half steps must make one whole step. Screen by screen: 1 shows the two columns from 1, adding 2 and doubling, up to step 4. 2 asks you to guess the doubling column at step 10 before it runs there. 3 drops the dots onto the graph and builds A × bˣ, with both sliders free. 4 asks for the bar one step after 48 at × 1.5, with no formula on screen. 5 turns 5% a year into × 1.05 on a £1,000 pot over ten years, beside £50 added each year, and marks where it doubles. 6 asks for the factor for 5%. 7 sets the factor to 0.5 and halves the course's 400 V every 10 seconds. 8 asks what an £8,000 machine is worth after 2 years losing 25% a year. 9 turns on the fill-in switch. 10 asks for the value half a step on from 100 at × 9. 11 to 13 mix it all: which of x², 2ˣ and 10x is exponential, from their tables at x = 1, 2, 3 and 4; a count of 100 growing 9% a day, after 30 days; and when the 400 V passes 30 V. What the model leaves out (the app shows this in its own panel; do not repeat it on the page): The starts, amounts, factors, money, machine values and the capacitor's voltages are example values. Each screen draws its bars to one scale, so a bar too short to see beside one of 1,024 is still not zero. Real quantities rarely keep exactly the same factor for long: rates change, and growth in the world runs into limits. And a real amount made of whole pieces, such as coins or the electrons on a capacitor, stops fitting the rule once only a few pieces are left, while the rule itself never reaches zero.

## The explanation Dan reads after playing (use the same words for the same things)
Some things grow by adding the same amount on each equal step. Others grow by multiplying by the same number on each step, and that is exponential change: the output is multiplied by the same factor every time the input goes up by one, and that multiplier is called the growth factor. Start two columns of bars at 1. The left one adds 2 each step: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. The right one multiplies by 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. At first the doubling column is behind, 2 against 3 and 4 against 5, so here multiplying starts slowly. But each doubling adds as much as there already is, so its rises go 1, 2, 4, 8 while the left column's stay at 2. By step 10 the left column reads 1 + 10 × 2 = 21, and the right column, 1 doubled 10 times, reads 1,024. A slow start can hide it for longer. Say a count of 100 grows 9% a day. After a week it is about 183, close to the 163 that adding 9 a day gives, but after 30 days it is about 1,327 against 370.

That is the cause: each step's rise is a fixed share of what is there, so the more there is, the more it grows, and the rate of growth is proportional to the current value. Write the start as A and the factor as b. After one step you have A × b, after two A × b × b, and after x steps A multiplied by b x times, which is A × bˣ. That is a power, as in Powers and roots, but now the letter is in the power, counting the steps. A percentage change gives the factor just as…

## The house kit (complete API reference)
You write the BODY of one page: HTML, an optional `<style>`, then one inline `<script>` ending
with `K.ready()`. It runs in a sandboxed iframe on Dan's phone (about 340 px wide, touch) and
laptop, with the global `K` and the kit's stylesheet. No network or storage: no web
addresses, `fetch`, `localStorage`, `alert` or `prompt`.

### Page shape that works
1. `<p class="lead">`: one line saying what is drawn (the app adds title and action).
2. The main visual with its controls right under it: `K.stage('#scene', '#controls')` keeps the
   pair on one phone screen. A choice that re-sorts the picture may sit above it.
3. `<p class="say">`: one sentence that changes with the state and says why. Then
   `<div class="k-readouts">` for numbers the drawing does not label.
4. Secondary figures and anything that grows go below.
5. `<p class="caption">`: one short sentence, at most 20 words: where the numbers come from ("A
   sketch, not measured data") or the rule in the explanation's words. No new claim, no law or
   term the explanation does not teach. The app shows what the model leaves out.

### Keep the answer hidden until Dan moves
Dan predicts before he plays: open on a setting the question is not about, and hide whatever
gives the answer away until his first move:
- class `k-after-move` on any element (a mark, an arrow, a sentence): hidden, its space kept.
  Hide the answer, not the controls or a whole figure (a blank hole fails the test).
- `K.readout({..., afterMove: true})` shows "?" until then; plot series and marks take `afterMove: true` too.
- `K.moved` is true from then on: in `K.update`, write `K.moved ? answer : question`.
- `K.afterMove(fn)` runs `fn` once at that moment; `K.reveal()` marks it from your handlers.

### The pipeline
Controls register themselves; their values form `params` = `{id: value}`.
```
K.model(fn)        fn(params) -> outputs {name: number | string}. Pure: no DOM, no lists or objects.
K.update(fn)       fn(params, outputs): draw everything here. More than one is allowed.
K.at(over)         -> outputs of the model at those params (others take each control's starting value)
K.check(label, fn, {source?})   fn() must return exactly true
K.ready()          call once, last: draws the opening state and runs the checks
K.complete()       at the lesson's end
K.params()  K.outputs()  K.refresh()   (refresh reruns the pipeline after you change your own state)
```
A readout whose id is an output key updates itself.

### Controls
Every control takes `into` (a selector or element). Ids are unique; use the lesson's ids exactly.
```
K.control({id, label, min, max, step, value, unit?, prefix?, fmt?, log?, snap?, hint?, into}) -> {el, get(), set(v)}
K.choice({id, label, options: [value | {value, label}], value, hint?, into}) -> {el, get, set}
K.toggle({id, label, value: false, hint?, into}) -> {el, get, set}
K.stepper({id, steps: [title | {title, text}], value: 0, compact?, label?, into}) -> {el, get, set, setSteps(list)}
K.button({label, press, secondary?, into}) -> {el, press(), setLabel(text)}
K.drag(el, {control, toValue(x, y)}) -> {el}
```
`control` is a big slider with − / + buttons. Keep `unit` short; `fmt(v)` returns the whole text;
`log: true` spans decades; `snap: [values]` lands a drag exactly on a setting the prediction or a
check names. `choice`: 2-8 short named options; `value` is an option's value or its 0-based
index. `stepper` walks a process with Back / Next (`compact: true` when your diagram shows the
content). `button` is an action (Send it in): use it, not plain buttons. `drag` lets Dan move
the thing itself: `toValue` maps the pointer (viewBox units; px from `el`'s top-left for HTML)
to the control's value; the first touch counts as his move.

- A timeline runs forward: a slider rising left to right, its `fmt` showing the date (−500 as

  range, its `fmt` showing the year; years after the first date are for new controls. Each dated

### The self-test
Before Dan sees it, the app loads your body 340, 720 and 1040 px wide, runs every check, moves
every control, button, animation and drag, reveals the after-move parts, and looks again at Text

  K.model((p) => ({ T: period(p.L) }));
  K.update((p, o) => {
    plot.draw({ series: [{ fn: period }], marks: [{ x: p.L, y: o.T, guides: true }] });
    document.getElementById('say').textContent = !K.moved ? 'Make the string four times longer: how much longer is each swing?'
      : 'A ' + K.fmt(p.L, { decimals: 2 }) + ' m string swings there and back every ' + K.fmt(o.T, { decimals: 2 }) + ' s. Four times the length only doubles that.';
  });
  K.check('No length, no swing time', () => K.near(period(0), 0));
  K.check('A seconds pendulum (2 s per swing) is 0.994 m long', () => K.near(period(0.994), 2, 0.002),
    { source: 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seconds_pendulum' });
  K.check('Four times the length doubles the period', () => K.near(K.at({ L: 2 }).T / K.at({ L: 0.5 }).T, 2, 1e-9));

<script>
  // gamma: ratio of specific heats for air, the value NASA Glenn's Brayton page uses.
  const GAMMA = 1.4;
  const SOURCE = 'https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/brayton.html';
  const pct = (r) => 100 * (1 - Math.pow(r, -(GAMMA - 1) / GAMMA));

  const r = K.control({ id: 'r', label: 'Pressure ratio', hint: 'Pressure after the compressor ÷ pressure before it', min: 1, max: 50, step: 1, value: 8, unit: ':1', into: '#controls' });
  K.readout({ id: 'eff', label: 'Ideal efficiency', unit: '%', decimals: 1, big: true, into: '#outs' });

  K.stage('#plot', '#controls');   // stacked on a phone, side by side on a laptop

  const plot = K.plot('#plot', {
    x: { min: 1, max: 50, label: 'Pressure ratio' },
    y: { min: 0, max: 100, label: 'Ideal efficiency (%)' },
    label: 'Ideal efficiency rises steeply at low pressure ratios, then flattens',
  });

  // The pure model: pressure ratio -> ideal efficiency in percent.
  K.model((p) => ({ eff: pct(p.r) }));

  K.update((p, o) => {
    plot.draw({
      series: [{ fn: pct, label: 'Ideal efficiency' }],
      marks: [{ x: p.r, y: o.eff, guides: true }],   // its value is in the readout alone
    });
    document.getElementById('work').style.width = o.eff + '%';
    document.getElementById('split').setAttribute('aria-label', 'The filled part becomes work: ' + (o.eff < 50 ? 'under' : 'over') + ' half the heat');

    let s;
    if (p.r === 1) {
      s = 'With no squeeze at all (r = 1), the hot gas has no extra pressure to push the turbine with, so no work comes out: 0%.';
    } else if (p.r * 2 <= 50) {
      const more = pct(p.r * 2) - o.eff;
      s = 'At ' + p.r + ':1 an ideal engine turns ' + o.eff.toFixed(1) + '% of the fuel\'s heat into work. Doubling the squeeze to ' +
        p.r * 2 + ':1 would add ' + more.toFixed(1) + ' more points.';
    } else {
      const less = o.eff - pct(p.r / 2);
      s = 'At ' + p.r + ':1 an ideal engine turns ' + o.eff.toFixed(1) + '% of the fuel\'s heat into work. Halving the squeeze would cost only ' +
        less.toFixed(1) + ' points: the curve has flattened.';
    }
    // Until Dan moves the slider the sentence invites rather than answers his prediction.
    document.getElementById('say').textContent = K.moved ? s : 'Does doubling the squeeze double the work you get? Move the pressure ratio and see.';
  });

  // Known answers, worked out by hand from the formula.
  K.check('No squeeze (r = 1) gives 0% efficiency', () => K.near(K.at({ r: 1 }).eff, 0, 1e-9));
  K.check('r = 10 gives 48.2%, from the ideal Brayton formula', () => K.near(K.at({ r: 10 }).eff, 48.205, 0.001), { source: SOURCE });
  K.check('Efficiency rises with every step up in pressure ratio', () => {
    for (let x = 1; x < 50; x++) if (!(K.at({ r: x + 1 }).eff > K.at({ r: x }).eff)) return false;
    return true;
  });
  K.check('Diminishing returns: 1 → 2 adds 18.0 points, 20 → 40 adds only 7.6', () =>
    K.near(K.at({ r: 2 }).eff - K.at({ r: 1 }).eff, 17.97, 0.01) && K.near(K.at({ r: 40 }).eff - K.at({ r: 20 }).eff, 7.63, 0.01));
  K.check('Even at r = 50 the ideal stays below 100% (67.3%)', () => K.near(K.at({ r: 50 }).eff, 67.30, 0.01));

  K.ready();
</script>
