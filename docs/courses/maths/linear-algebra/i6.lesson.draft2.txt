  "text": "Some changes repeat: a pump that runs for an hour, every hour. Doing a matrix step n times means working out Aⁿ. Multiplying out powers is slow, and it hides where the steps are heading. The fix is to measure along the matrix's eigenvectors. On their grid each step only stretches each direction by its own eigenvalue. So n steps just raise each eigenvalue to the power n.[^1]\n\nTake A = [3 1; 0 2] again. Its eigenvectors are (1, 0), with eigenvalue 3, and (−1, 1), with eigenvalue 2. Use those two arrows as your basis. A basis made of eigenvectors is an [[eigenbasis]]. The arrow (1, 1) is 2 lots of (1, 0) plus 1 lot of (−1, 1), so on this grid its amounts are (2, 1). A matrix acts on a mix part by part. So one step turns 2 lots of (1, 0) into 2 lots of A(1, 0), and 1 lot of (−1, 1) into 1 lot of A(−1, 1). Each eigenvector is only stretched, so the first amount is multiplied by 3 and the second by 2. So on this grid A acts as D = [3 0; 0 2]. That has the eigenvalues down its diagonal and zeros everywhere else, a [[diagonal matrix]]. Three steps give the amounts 2 × 27 and 1 × 8, which is (54, 8). Back on your grid that is 54(1, 0) + 8(−1, 1) = (46, 8).\n\nRemember One arrow, two grids? Put the eigenvectors in as the columns of P = [1 −1; 0 1]. P turns amounts on the eigenvector grid into your numbers, and P⁻¹ = [1 1; 0 1] turns your numbers into amounts. So A seen on the eigenvector grid is P⁻¹AP = D. Turned round, A = PDP⁻¹. Read it right to left: into the eigenvector grid, stretch, back out. To [[diagonalise]] A is to find this P and D. Each eigenvalue sits in the column of its own eigenvector, so swapping P's columns swaps D's entries.\n\nNow repeat it. A³ is PDP⁻¹ PDP⁻¹ PDP⁻¹. Each P⁻¹P in the middle is I, which changes nothing, so A³ = PD³P⁻¹. In general Aⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹, and Dⁿ just raises each diagonal entry to the power n. Leave that power off and you get A itself, one step. Here Aⁿ = [3ⁿ 3ⁿ − 2ⁿ; 0 2ⁿ].   "text": "- Find the eigenvalues from det(A − λI) = 0, and an eigenvector for each.\n- Check you have as many independent eigenvectors as A has columns. If not, A cannot be diagonalised, so multiply out the powers instead.\n- Put the eigenvectors in as the columns of P. Put each eigenvalue in D, in the same column as its eigenvector.\n- Find P⁻¹. For a 2 by 2, swap the two diagonal entries, change the signs of the other two, and divide by the determinant.\n- Check that AP = PD. Each column of AP should be its eigenvalue times that column of P.\n- For n steps, raise each entry of D to the power n. Then work out PDⁿP⁻¹.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** read PDP⁻¹ right to left: into the eigenvector grid, stretch, back out. When one eigenvalue is bigger in size than the rest, its part takes over in the end, if the start has some of it. If every eigenvalue is smaller than 1 in size, every part dies away, and Aⁿ heads for zero. An eigenvalue of exactly 1 gives a part that never changes, a steady state. A negative eigenvalue flips its part every step.\n\n**Worked example:** the pumped tanks, A = [0.8 0.3; 0.2 0.7]. The eigenvectors are (3, 2) with 1 and (1, −1) with 0.5. So P = [3 1; 2 −1] and D = [1 0; 0 0.5]. det P = −3 − 2 = −5, so P⁻¹ = [0.2 0.2; 0.4 −0.6]. For three hours, D³ = [1 0; 0 0.125]. Then PD³ = [3 0.125; 2 −0.125], and PD³P⁻¹ = [0.65 0.525; 0.35 0.475]. So from 1000 L in tank 1, three hours later tank 1 holds 650 L and tank 2 holds 350 L. Over many hours 0.5ⁿ heads for 0, so Aⁿ heads for [0.6 0.6; 0.4 0.4]. Whatever the start, the water heads for a 60 : 40 share.\n\n**Where you meet it:** any rule of the form \"next = A × now\", stepped again and again. Textbooks add that symmetrical components, used to work out unbalanced faults on three-phase systems, rest on the same move. A change of basis turns a balanced system's matrix into a diagonal one, so each sequence can be worked on its own.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Leaving the power off D. PDP⁻¹ is A itself, one step.\n- Getting the order wrong, as in PAP⁻¹ or P⁻¹DP. A = PDP⁻¹, with P⁻¹ on the right, acting first.\n- Putting D's eigenvalues in a different order from P's columns.\n- Thinking a repeated eigenvalue always gives two eigenvectors. Count the independent lines, as with the shear.\n- Thinking a repeated eigenvalue always rules it out. 2I has 2 twice and is diagonal already.\n- Expecting the tanks to reach 60 : 40 exactly. The part left over halves every hour but never reaches zero."
