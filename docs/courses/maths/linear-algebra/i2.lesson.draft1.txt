    "text": "An arrow's numbers are not the arrow. They are the amounts of each basis arrow you lay nose to tail to reach its tip.[^1] Choose a different basis and the same arrow gets different numbers.[^2] A matrix whose columns are the new basis arrows turns one set of numbers into the other.\n\nYour grid uses i and j. So the arrow to (3, 3) means 3 lots of i and 3 of j. Now meet Jennifer. Her basis is b₁ = (2, 1) and b₂ = (−1, 1), written in your numbers. Two lots of b₁ reach (4, 2). One b₂ on the end reaches (3, 3). So to her, the same arrow is (2, 1). Her numbers mean nothing until you know her arrows. Now move b₁ to (1, 1). The arrow is 3 lots of the new b₁ and none of b₂. Her numbers read (3, 0), yet the arrow has not moved. Both numbers changed, though only b₁ moved. Each number depends on the whole basis.\n\nTo turn her numbers into yours, do what the How button does. Take 2 lots of b₁, add 1 of b₂, and you land on (3, 3). A matrix times a column is exactly that sum, a mix of its columns. So put her arrows in as columns. P = [2 −1; 1 1], and P times (2, 1) is (3, 3).[^3] This is the [[change-of-basis matrix]]. Its columns are her arrows written in your numbers. To go the other way, undo P with its inverse. Its determinant is 2 × 1 − (−1) × 1 = 3. That is also the area of one cell of her grid. So P⁻¹ = [1 1; −1 2] ÷ 3, and P⁻¹ times (3, 3) is (6, 3) ÷ 3 = (2, 1). The area is not 0 because b₁ and b₂ are independent. If her arrows lay on one line, P would have no inverse. Most arrows would then have no numbers on her grid at all.\n\nA move translates the same way. A quarter turn anticlockwise is M = [0 −1; 1 0] in your numbers. Jennifer wants it in hers. Start from her (2, 1). P turns it into your (3, 3). M turns that to (−3, 3). P⁻¹ brings it back to hers, (0, 3). Check: 3 lots of b₂ is (−3, 3). The first step goes on the right, as in any product of moves. So the turn, in her numbers, is P⁻¹MP. This [[change of basis]] can make a move far simpler. Take M = [3 1; 0 2] and the grid (1, 0) and (−1, 1). There P⁻¹MP = [3 0; 0 2]. On that grid the move only stretches each basis arrow. Finding such a grid is how the two masses on springs come apart into two simple swings.\n\n**An arrow's numbers are the amounts of each basis arrow. P, with the new basis arrows as its columns, turns new numbers into old, and P⁻¹ turns them back.**"
