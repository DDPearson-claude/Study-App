    "text": "Mix some arrows, so many of each laid nose to tail, and see where you can land. Every point you can reach that way is the arrows' [[span]].[^1] Arrows are [[linearly independent]] when each one adds a direction the others cannot reach.\n\nTake u = (2, 1) and v = (1, 3). Two lots of u and one of v land on (5, 5). Minus one u and one v land on (−1, 2), because a negative amount turns the arrow round. Whole amounts land on the corners of a slanted grid. Each cell of that grid is a parallelogram with u and v as its sides. Its area is the determinant, 2 × 3 − 1 × 1 = 5. Cells with real area tile the whole plane with no gaps. Fractions reach the points inside each cell. So every point in the plane is some mix of u and v, and their span is the whole plane.\n\nNow swing v round onto u's line, to (−4, −2). That arrow is −2 lots of u. So any mix of the two is just some amount of u, and it lands on the line through (2, 1). The cells thin to nothing, and the determinant is 2 × (−2) − 1 × (−4) = 0. The reach collapses to a line, because the second arrow added no new direction. The two arrows are dependent, the opposite of independent. It shows in a mix as well: 2u plus (−4, −2) is the zero arrow. That is the test. If one arrow is a mix of the others, moving it across gives a mix that is not all zeros landing on zero. For u and v, only 0u + 0v lands on zero.\n\nIn space it works the same with one more arrow. u = (1, 0, −1) and v = (0, 2, −1) span a flat sheet through the origin. A third arrow off the sheet, such as (1, 2, −1), makes a box of volume 2, and the three reach all of space. Lower it into the sheet, to w = (1, 2, −2), which is u + v, and the box flattens to volume 0. A third arrow on the sheet leaves the span as it was, trapped on that sheet.[^2] To test by hand, solve a u + b v + c w = 0 by elimination. a = b = c = 0 always works, so this system can never be inconsistent. The question is whether anything else works. Here c is free, and a = 1, b = 1, c = −1 lands on zero, so the three are dependent.\n\nA set of independent arrows that spans a space is a [[basis]] for it.[^3] i and j are one basis for the plane, and u = (2, 1) and v = (1, 3) are another. Any third arrow in the plane is a mix of two independent ones, so three arrows in a plane are always dependent. The two masses on springs will need this. Each of their simple swings becomes one arrow of a basis, and every shudder a mix of them.\n\n**The span is every point the mixes reach, and arrows are independent when none is a mix of the others, so their cells or boxes have real area or volume, while a dependent set crushes them to 0.**"

    "text": "- Two arrows in the plane: work out the determinant, p s − q r for (p, q) and (r, s). Not 0 means independent, and they span the plane. 0 means they lie on one line.\n- Three arrows in space: work out the 3 by 3 determinant. Not 0 means independent, and they span all of space. 0 means they lie in one sheet through the origin, or on one line.\n- Or use elimination. Put the arrows in as columns with zeros on the right, and run it to a staircase. A free letter means dependent. No free letter means only all zeros works, so they are independent.\n- To find the mix that lands on zero, set the free letter to 1 or −1 and work back up.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** More arrows than each arrow has parts are always dependent. A set holding the zero arrow is always dependent.\n\n**Worked example:** Are (1, 2, 0), (0, 1, 1) and (2, 5, 1) independent? No two are multiples of each other, so look harder. As columns they give the rows 1 0 2, 2 1 5 and 0 1 1. Take 2 lots of row 1 from row 2, giving 0 1 1. Take that from row 3, giving 0 0 0. So c is free. Set c = −1. Row 2 says b + c = 0, so b = 1. Row 1 says a + 2c = 0, so a = 2. Check: 2 lots of (1, 2, 0) plus (0, 1, 1) is (2, 5, 1), the third arrow. So the three are dependent and span only a flat sheet.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Reading the working wrongly, as in \"inconsistent, so independent\". The zeros on the right mean a = b = c = 0 always works. Ask whether anything else does.\n- Taking the span to be the arrows themselves, or the parallelogram between them. It is every point any mix reaches, with amounts of any size, negative too.\n- Checking only whether two of three arrows line up. In the worked example no two do, yet the third is a mix of the other two.\n- Reading a determinant of 0 as \"no reach\". The arrows still reach a line or a sheet, just not everything."
