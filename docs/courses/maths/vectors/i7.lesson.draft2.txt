    "text": "A phasor is drawn like a vector and adds like one. But its angle marks a moment in time, not a direction in space. And because it is a complex number, it can be divided.\n\nStart with the force arrow. A 3 kN pull on a ring points somewhere real. Its angle says which way the rope runs. Now take an arrow that turns round its tail once every cycle. Its height at each instant traces the sine wave of a voltage. Freeze it and you have the fixed arrow you write as r∠θ. On a 50 Hz supply one turn takes 20 ms. So a current 90° behind the voltage peaks a quarter turn later, 5 ms after it. The angle measures that delay. Nothing in the cable points that way.\n\nTurn two arrows together and their nose-to-tail sum turns with them. So the sum's height is the two heights added, at every instant. That is why phasors add nose to tail, exactly as forces do.[^1] 6 A in step with the voltage and 8 A a quarter turn behind give √(6² + 8²) = 10 A, not 14 A. This needs every arrow to turn at the same speed. A 150 Hz arrow turns three times as fast as a 50 Hz one. So the angle between them changes from one instant to the next, and they cannot share a diagram.[^5] Being a number gives a phasor one thing a vector lacks. You can divide phasors, but you cannot divide one vector by another.[^4] Divide the sizes and take away the angles. So 230∠0° V ÷ 10∠−30° A gives an impedance of 23∠30° Ω. Nor does the dot product of two phasors give another phasor.[^3]\n\nThree-phase is the ring of pulls again. Three equal currents 120° apart, laid nose to tail, close into a triangle. What flows out on the lines must come back. So the neutral carries whatever the three lines do not cancel. On a closed chain that is nothing. Now shrink L3 to 4 A, with L1 and L2 at 10 A. The chain falls short of closing, and the gap is the neutral current. Across, the parts give 10 − 5 − 2 = 3 A. Up, they give 0 − 8.66 + 3.46 = −5.20 A. So the neutral carries √(3² + 5.20²) = 6 A. This is the ideal case of pure sine waves at one frequency. Real loads add harmonics, and some of those add up in the neutral. So the board in the question sends 6 A back on the neutral, not 24 A, because its currents add as arrows.\n\n**A phasor adds like a vector, but its angle is a shift in time, and being a number it can be divided.**[^1]"

When two lines carry the same current, the neutral carries the difference between them and the third. With only one line loaded, the neutral carries all of its current.\n\n**Worked example:** Say L1 carries 20 A, L2 15 A and L3 10 A, at 0°, −120° and 120°. Across: 20 − 7.5 − 5 = 7.5 A.
