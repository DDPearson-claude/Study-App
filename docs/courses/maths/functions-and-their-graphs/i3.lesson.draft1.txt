    "text": "Five graph shapes turn up again and again: the line, the square, the cube, the one-over and the square root. Each is a family with a tell-tale look, and each is the shape of a law you use at work. Each shape comes straight from its rule, and the quickest way to see why is to double the input and watch the output.\n\nOn the line, y = x, every equal step across gives the same rise, so it is straight. The transmitter from the last lesson is a line, a straight-line rule of the kind instruments use.[^1] On the square, y = x², double the input and the output goes up four times, because 2 × 2 = 4, so the curve climbs faster and faster. A negative input gives the same output as the positive one, because a minus times a minus is a plus, so the left half mirrors the right and the curve is a U, called a [[parabola]]. Power lost in a cable depends on the square of the current,[^2] P = I²R: on a 1 Ω run, 3 A loses 9 W and 6 A loses 36 W. On the cube, y = x³, double the input and the output goes up eight times, because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. A negative input now gives a negative output, so the curve comes up from the bottom left, flattens through 0 and climbs to the top right. A ship's engine load goes with the cube of the propeller's speed,[^3] so at half speed it is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125, one eighth of full load.\n\nOn the one-over, y = 1/x, double the input and the output halves. Current from a fixed voltage is one: on 12 V, 3 Ω draws 4 A and 6 Ω draws 2 A. As the input shrinks towards 0 the output grows past any number you name, and dividing by 0 has no answer, so the curve never touches the up axis and has no point at 0. Plot a gas's pressure against its volume and you get this shape, called a [[hyperbola]].[^4] On the square root, y = √x, it takes four times the input to double the output, because √4 = 2. The curve starts at 0, rises steeply and then flattens. A flow meter's pressure difference goes with the square of the flow, so the flow is found from the square root of the pressure difference.[^5] Halfway up the pressure difference, √0.5 = 0.707, so a 12 mA signal from the cell is 50% of the pressure difference but 71% of the flow.[^9]\n\nThe families also differ in what a level line does. On the square, a level line above the bottom cuts the U twice, so two different inputs give the same output and it is many-to-one.[^8] On the line, the cube, the one-over and the square root, a level line cuts at most once, so each output comes from one input only, and the function is called [[one-to-one]].\n\n**Five shapes, the line, the square's U, the cube's S, the one-over's two curves and the root that starts at 0, each come from what the rule does to a doubled input, and each is the shape of a working law.**"

    "text": "- Make a short table: put in 1, 2 and 4, and a negative or two if the inputs can be negative.\n- See what each doubling does to the output.\n- Look for the tell-tale shape: straight, a U, an S, two curves hugging the axes, or a curve starting at 0.\n- Name the family, then check one more input against its rule.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** On the plain rules, doubling the input doubles the output on the line through 0, makes it four times on the square and eight times on the cube, and halves it on the one-over. On the root it takes four times the input to double the output.\n\n**Worked example:** A 12 V supply through 2 Ω draws 6 A, through 4 Ω draws 3 A and through 8 Ω draws 1.5 A. Each doubling of the resistance halves the current, so this is the one-over family, I = 12/R. Check: 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Treating every law as a line. Try a negative input: the square gives a positive output, the cube a negative one.\n- Reading a value at 0 off the one-over. It has no point there."
