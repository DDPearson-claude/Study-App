  "text": "A TDR sends a short, low-voltage pulse down a core and its screen and times the echoes. Wherever the impedance changes, part of the pulse comes back. The time tells you how far away the change is. The shape tells you what it is.\n\nThe pulse sees the cable as an impedance of its own, the [[characteristic impedance]]. The cable's inductance and capacitance per metre set it. While that stays the same, the pulse runs on and nothing returns. Where it changes, the share that comes back is (Z load − Z0) ÷ (Z load + Z0). An open end is infinite impedance, so the pulse comes back the right way up. A short is zero, so it comes back upside down. A fault from core to screen sits in parallel with the cable beyond it. Parallel paths give a lower impedance, so the reflection points down. The higher the fault's resistance, the less it lowers the impedance. On String B a 35 Ω fault sends back 0.28 of the pulse, but a 1000 Ω fault only 0.01. So a high-resistance fault can look like healthy cable.\n\nThe set measures time, and the time covers the trip there and back. So the distance is L = t × v ÷ 2. The insulation sets v, through its permittivity. It is often given as a [[velocity factor]], v divided by the speed of light. Every distance is worked with the same v, so a wrong v stretches them all by the same fraction. That is why the velocity is set on a known length, with the far end open. This picture is simplified: it draws clean reflections and leaves out the loss that makes far echoes smaller and wider.\n\n**Distance is time × velocity ÷ 2: a reflection up means the impedance rose, one down means it fell, and the velocity is set on a known length.**[^16]"
