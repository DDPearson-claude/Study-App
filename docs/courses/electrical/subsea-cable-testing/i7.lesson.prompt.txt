Research notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- LIRA is based on transmission line theory: it estimates and analyses the complex line impedance as a function of signal frequency, and measures the surge impedance over a wide range of frequencies. [1][2][3]
- Your notes say LIRA needs access to only one end, works on frequency-domain resonance effects of unmatched lines, and is sensitive to small changes in insulation permittivity. [4][5][6]
- Changes in dielectric permittivity change the cable impedance. A damaged segment differs mainly through a change in cable capacitance. [7][8]
- Joints, splices or defects create a deviation in the input impedance spectrum. The resonance frequencies give the cable length and the phase spectrum shows condition and locates joints and degradation. [9][10][11]
- Wirescan's patent gives the relative phase velocity from two consecutive resonance frequencies: vr = 2L(fk+1 − fk)/v0, where L is the length of the cable and v0 is the speed of light. [12][13][14]
- LIRA works with any termination except an exact match to the cable impedance, which makes the cable look endless. [15]
- DNORM indicates the severity of local impedance changes, and its sign helps determine the type of change. [16][17]
- DNORM normalises the spike difference from the terminal peak, and a local spot has a mirror image beyond the terminal peak from a second-order reflection. [18][19][20]
- Your notes give a negative DNORM for thermal degradation, water penetration and mechanical causes, and positive for bends and breaks in the shield. Colours run green mild, amber intermediate, red significant. [21][22][23]
- Your notes say tests are normally bi-directional, take about a minute each, run to 18 tests of core to screen and core to core, have no acceptance criteria, and need the raw trace data kept. [24][25][26][27][28]
- Wirescan's patent says LIRA can estimate the location of a degraded spot to within 0.5% of the cable length. [29]
- Both ends are earthed and discharged before a LIRA test so no energy is stored in the cable. [30]
- An open-ended line looks like a capacitor at low frequency, then resonates: the input impedance swings between a short and an open at frequencies set by length and velocity, spaced v divided by 2L apart (a quarter wavelength gives a short, a half wavelength an open). [31][32][33][34][35]
- A frequency sweep cannot say where a mismatch is by itself; transforming the reflection data from frequency to distance does. Reflections from a mismatch show as a ripple on the frequency response, and the Fourier transform of the reflection coefficient against frequency gives it against distance. [36][37][38]
- LIRA's patent finds each reflection's place from the power spectrum of the impedance after a transform: x = vr v0 f'' divided by 2, where f'' is the component in the transformed domain; the sweep runs from 0 to X MHz where X depends on the cable length; Wirescan's FAQ gives 5 000 to 30 000 discrete… [39][40][41]
- Wirescan injects low-amplitude sine waves, they travel to the far end and reflect back, and the spot signature is an FFT of the input-impedance phase spectrum, which is more sensitive to soft defects with a small impedance change. [42][43][44]
- DNORM severity is a bar chart of the features that exceed a DNORM threshold setting, colour-coded by severity; the direction (positive or negative) tells the type of impedance change, such as moisture ingress or thermal degradation. The threshold is a software setting (0 dB in one PNNL example), so… [45][46][47]
- Test from both ends for the best sensitivity (it is not a necessity); other loads may stay connected if they reflect enough; LIRA suits cables hundreds of kilometres long, which tests at working voltage do not. [48][49][50]
- Take an initial fingerprint at commissioning; measure at manufacturing milestones, factory acceptance tests, after transport and before, during and after installation; LIRA and TDR fingerprints at FAT, spooling and commissioning generate hundreds of reports, so the data must be kept in a usable for… [51][52][53][54]
- Online LIRA exists for energised cables: offline methods are restricted by safety, so the online system uses non-galvanic capacitive and inductive sensors with a band-pass of 100 kHz to 20 MHz; the one case study is a 12/10(24) kV wind-farm cable. - Test time: the notes say roughly 1 minute; a Fauske (US distributor) article says about three minutes depending on the cable length. [59]
- A TDR uses the radar principle: a pulse goes down the cable and changes in impedance send reflections back, which are displayed as a map of the cable. [60][61][62]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A positive reflection shows a higher impedance and a negative one a lower impedance, because conductors moving apart or closer together change the impedance. [63][64][65]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- An open conductor shows a large positive trace and a short circuit a negative trace. A joint shows a small positive followed by a small negative trace. [66][67][68][69][70][71]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A wet splice or water gives a short positive and negative trace, and your notes list a ragged trailing section for water ingress. [72][73]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The velocity factor is the ratio of the pulse speed in the cable to the speed of light, and it must be set for the cable. If unknown, test a known length and adjust it until the displayed end distance is correct. [74][75][76]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Megger lists a velocity factor of 0.54 to 0.62, which is 162 to 186 m per microsecond, for power XLPE cable. [77]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Distance comes from the time of the reflected pulse times the velocity, taking half of the time, because the pulse goes to the fault and back. Your notes write L = t × v ÷ 2. [78][79][80]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- If the velocity factor is wrong for the cable, the displayed distance will be wrong. [81]  (from an idea this one builds on)
