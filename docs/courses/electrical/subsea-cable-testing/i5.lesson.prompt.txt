Research notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Your notes say a mains-frequency HV withstand test needs very high power because of cable capacitance, and that lowering the frequency raises the capacitive reactance and cuts the power and current needed. [1][2]
- The power needed to energise a cable capacitance is proportional to test frequency. One source gives 500 times less energy at 0.1 Hz than at 50 Hz, another 600 times less power than at 60 Hz. [3][4][5]
- Traditional 50/60 Hz testing was impractical on site because of the strong capacitive nature of cables. [6]
- DC testing built up space charge that accelerated water tree growth in healthy XLPE. VLF reverses polarity, which reduces that build-up but is still a compromise. [7][8][9]
- IEEE 400.2 covers VLF below 1 Hz, most commonly 0.1 Hz, with recommended test voltages for installation, acceptance and maintenance. [10][11][12]
- The VLF test range is taught as contested: the notes give 1.5 to 2.5 U0, while the IEEE guide as reproduced by High Voltage Inc bases installation and acceptance levels on practice of up to 3 U0. - Recommended minimum time is 60 min at 0.1 Hz for installation or acceptance on new cable, and 30 min for a simple withstand on aged cable. The notes list the same durations. [18][19][20][21]

- Terminations may need to be added to avoid flashover for installation tests on cables rated above 35 kV. [24]
- Long cables need a lower frequency, a lower voltage or both, but lowering the frequency needlessly is to be avoided. A pass means the voltage held for the full duration with no breakdown. [25][26][27]

- VLF sets are rated in kV peak, and a sine wave's rms is 0.707 of its peak. IEEE 400.2 Table 3 gives the 69 kV class installation level as 75 kV rms (106 kV peak). The largest 0.1 Hz sets reach about 200 kV peak. [39][40][17]
- The defect picture: a defect big enough to be excited by the applied voltage grows to failure during the test, and lesser defects are not affected.
