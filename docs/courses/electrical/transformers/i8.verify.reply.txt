   "now": "Two hazards on the job share one cause: the core saturating. Switch a transformer on at the wrong moment and its flux overshoots the knee, so it draws a surge many times its full-load current. Open a current transformer's secondary while its primary carries current, and its core saturates every half-cycle. Tall voltage spikes then appear across the open terminals. A third point is about joins, not saturation: an auto-transformer's output is joined to its input, so it gives no isolation.\n\nStart from the rule that runs through every transformer. The supply voltage sets how fast the flux changes. So the flux is the running total of the voltage. Running normally, the flux swings between equal peaks either way. It rises while the voltage is positive and stops rising as the voltage reaches zero. So the flux peaks at each voltage zero. Now switch on at a voltage zero with an empty core. The flux should be at a peak there, but it starts from nothing. The next half-cycle of voltage still adds a full swing, two peaks' worth, so the flux climbs to twice its normal peak. Any flux left in the iron from last time, pushing the same way, adds on top. A core normally runs not far below its knee, so this takes it far past. Past the knee the iron is full and helps little more. Each extra bit of flux is then pushed through almost as if the core were air, and that takes a great deal of current. That surge is the [[inrush current]]. It comes mostly from the core saturating.[^1] Its peaks can reach 8 to 30 times the full-load current.[^2] Switch on at a voltage peak with an empty core instead, and the flux starts where it should be, so nothing overshoots. So two things set the surge: the moment on the wave, and the flux left in.\n\nA current transformer, a CT, turns the rule round. A power transformer is fed from a voltage, so the voltage fixes its flux. A CT's primary is a busbar carrying whatever current the circuit's load draws. Its 80-turn secondary drives 5 A round the meter, and those ampere-turns almost cancel the busbar's 400. The core then needs only enough flux to make the few volts that push 5 A round the meter circuit, its [[burden]]. Open the secondary and nothing cancels the busbar's ampere-turns. They drive the core into saturation.[^3] Saturated, the flux sits flat and makes no voltage. Near each current zero it flips to the other side in a moment, and that fast change makes a tall, narrow spike. With current in the primary, an open secondary can make high voltages.[^4] As much as 4000 V has been measured on large CTs left open.[^5] A voltage transformer, a VT, is fed from the system voltage like a power transformer. So it runs safely open, and a short on it is a fault.\n\nAn isolating transformer's two windings are not joined. An [[auto-transformer]] is one winding with a tap, so its output is joined to its input.[^6] Its two sides are one connected system, so an earth fault on either is an earth fault on both. It cannot make a separate insulated system. The supply's current flows straight on into the load, carrying 115 ÷ 440 of the power, 26%, at 440 V to 115 V. Transformer action carries the other 74%. So this is why an open CT is dangerous: with nothing to balance the busbar, its core saturates, and its flipping flux can drive high voltages across the open terminals, thousands of volts on large CTs. This picture is simplified: a sketched knee, a stiff supply, one phase, and a CT that keeps its ratio below the knee. **Core saturation explains both the surge at switch-on and an open CT's dangerous voltage, while an auto-transformer gives no isolation.**"
