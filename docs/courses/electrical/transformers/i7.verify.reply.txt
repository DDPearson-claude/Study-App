   "now": "A three-phase transformer works like three single-phase transformers sharing one core. How you join each side's three windings, star or delta, can turn its whole output round by whole steps of 30°. The [[vector group]], a name like Dyn11, records the joins and that turn as an hour on a clock. Two transformers may share a board only if their ratios match and their hours match as connected. Otherwise the gap between their outputs drives a large current round the pair, with no load connected.\n\nStart from what you know. On each limb, one flux threads an HV winding and an LV winding. So the LV winding's voltage keeps exact time with its HV partner. Now picture the clock. The HV line voltage A–B is the hand fixed at twelve. The LV line voltage a–b is the hour hand, and each hour is 30°.[^1] In a star, each line voltage runs 30° ahead of the voltage across its winding. Join both sides in star and that shift happens on both sides alike, so the hand stays at twelve: Yyn0. Now join the HV side in delta. Its winding on limb 1 then sits straight across lines A and B, so it carries A–B itself, at twelve. The LV winding a–n on that limb keeps time with it, at twelve too. The LV is still a star, so its line voltage a–b runs 30° ahead of a–n. That puts the hand one hour anticlockwise, at eleven.\n\nThe name spells this out. Capitals name the HV side and small letters the LV: D for delta, y for star, n for a neutral brought out, then the hour. So Dyn11 is an HV delta, an LV star with its neutral out, and 11 × 30 = 330°.[^2] That 330° is counted clockwise, the way the hours run. It lands where 30° anticlockwise does. Phasors turn anticlockwise, so the LV is 30° ahead of the HV.[^3] You may meet Dyn11 described as a lag, so read it from the clock. Close the delta across A and C instead and the hand goes to one o'clock, Dyn1, 30° behind. Swap the LV winding's two ends and every LV voltage flips half a turn, so Dyn11 becomes Dyn5. Each limb still follows the turns rule. A delta winding sees the full 440 V line voltage. A star winding on a 115 V side gives 115 ÷ √3 = 66.40 V. So a Dyn11 stepping 440 V to 115 V needs 440 ÷ 66.40 = 6.63 turns to one on each limb.\n\nNow put two transformers of the same size on one board, both 440 V to 115 V, with an open switch between their outputs. Each LV phase voltage is an arrow 66.40 V long, and the voltage across the switch is the gap between their tips. Two equal arrows make a triangle with two equal sides. Cut it down the middle and each half has half the gap opposite half the angle. So the gap is 2 × 66.40 × sin(half the angle). A Dyn11 beside a Yyn0 is one hour apart, so the gap is 2 × 66.40 × sin 15° = 34.37 V, 52% of the phase voltage. Matching ratios alone do not close it. Close the switch and that voltage drives a [[circulating current]] round the pair. Only the two transformers' impedances, and the cables to the board, hold it back. At 5% each, 52 ÷ 10 = 5.2 times rated current flows with no load at all. A Dyn11 against a Dyn5 is half a turn apart, so the gap is twice the phase voltage and 20 times rated flows, as into a dead short. Running generators together adds checks of its own, and those come with Generators and paralleling. This picture is simplified: ideal windings, a supply turning A, B, C, and only star and delta joins. **Transformers in parallel need equal vector groups, so that their outputs are in phase and no short circuit occurs.[^4]**"

Swapping a winding's two ends shifts it six hours. One hour apart leaves a gap of 52% of the phase voltage. Half a turn apart leaves twice the phase voltage, like a dead short. Units in parallel drop the same volts, so the one with the lower percentage impedance carries a bigger share of its rating.\n\n**Worked example:** Two 100 kVA units, each 440 V to 115 V with 5% impedance, feed one board.
