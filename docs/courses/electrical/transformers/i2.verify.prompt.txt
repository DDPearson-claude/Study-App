This lesson: i2 "The magnetic circuit: iron, saturation and loss" — Ampere-turns push flux round an iron core far more easily than through air, until past a knee the iron saturates and more current buys little. [quantity]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i3 Changing flux makes a voltage — Only a changing flux makes a voltage, in every turn it threads, so a transformer needs AC and its supply voltage fixes the core's flux.
- i4 The ideal transformer: turns, volts and amps — The same flux threads both windings, so the volts follow the turns ratio, the amps go the other way, and power in equals power out.
- i5 The real transformer: losses, tests and efficiency — A real transformer has a fixed iron loss while energised and a copper loss growing with load squared, and two tests measure each.
- i6 Regulation and percentage impedance — The windings' series impedance drops the output voltage under load, most at lagging power factor, and the same impedance sets the fault current.
- i7 Three-phase transformers and vector groups — Star and delta connections shift the output in 30° steps, named as a clock hour like Dyn11; only matching groups may be paralleled.
- i8 Transformers on the job — Core saturation explains the current surge at switch-on and the dangerous voltage of an open-circuited current transformer, while an auto-transformer gives no isolation.

Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Current times turns, in ampere-turns, is the magnetomotive force that drives flux round a magnetic circuit, much as voltage drives current round an electric one. [R1]
- Iron's permeability is far higher than air's; one source puts iron's relative permeability at about 5000, so the same ampere-turns give far more flux through an iron core. [R2]
- At saturation a rise in magnetic field intensity gives only a minimal rise in flux density, so more current buys little extra flux. [R3]
- Around a long straight wire carrying current, the field lines form circular loops centred on the wire. [R4]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A solenoid, a long coil of wire carrying current, makes a field that resembles a bar magnet's. [R5]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Magnetic flux measures how much of a magnetic field passes perpendicular through an area, and a change in it induces a voltage. [R6]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Round a long straight wire the flux density is B = μ0 I ÷ (2π r), where μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m/A is the magnetic constant (the permeability of free space); with B in mT and r in mm this is 0.2 × current in A ÷ distance in mm. [R7]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- An ideal transformer assumes all the magnetic field lines stay in the core, so the same flux links every turn of both windings. [R8]  (whole topic)
- A good real transformer can have losses as low as 1% of the transmitted power. [R9]  (whole topic)
- Transformers cannot change DC voltages, because they work by Faraday's law of induction. [R10]  (whole topic)

That push is the [[magnetomotive force]], in ampere-turns.[^1] It drives flux round the ring as volts drive current. What opposes it is the ring's [[reluctance]], as resistance opposes current. The push is spread round the whole path. So the push per metre, H = N I ÷ l, sets the flux density B. In air, B = μ₀ H, where μ₀ is the magnetic constant. Iron is full of tiny regions, each already a small magnet. The push swings them into line, and their fields add to the coil's. So on the steep part of its curve, iron gives about 5000 times air's flux density.[^2] Its reluctance is that much lower.\n\nBut the regions run out.
