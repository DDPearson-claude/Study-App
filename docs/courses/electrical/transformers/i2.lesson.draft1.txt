    "text": "A coil's current pushes flux round a core, as volts push current round a circuit. Iron carries that flux far more readily than air, until it fills up. Past a knee in its curve it saturates, and more current buys little more flux.\n\nWind N turns on a ring and pass a current I. Each turn adds its field, so the push round the ring is N × I. That push is the [[magnetomotive force]], in ampere-turns.[^1] It drives flux round the ring as volts drive current. What opposes it is the ring's [[reluctance]], as resistance opposes current. A longer ring takes more push, so the push per metre, H = N I ÷ l, sets the flux density B. In air, B = μ₀ H, where μ₀ is the magnetic constant. Iron is full of tiny regions, each already a small magnet. The push swings them into line, and their fields add to the coil's. So on the steep part of its curve, iron gives about 5000 times air's flux density for the same push.[^2] Its reluctance is that much lower.\n\nBut the regions run out. Once nearly all are in line, little is left to swing. So the curve of B against H bends round a knee and runs nearly flat. This is [[saturation]]: more push buys only a little more flux density.[^3] So iron's reluctance is not fixed. It climbs as the iron fills. On the ring in the picture, 200 turns on a 0.4 m path, 0.5 A reaches 1.21 T. Doubling to 1 A gives 1.54 T, and 2 A only 1.60 T. A gap of air in the ring adds reluctance. It takes more current for the same flux density, but it keeps the curve straight for longer.\n\nOn AC the iron is swung one way, then the other, every cycle.
