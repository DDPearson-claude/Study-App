Rearrange the same parts, and you get a steady higher DC instead.\n\nThe circuit is a switching transistor followed by a diode, an inductor and a capacitor.[^2] This is a [[buck converter]]. The share of each cycle the switch is on is the [[duty cycle]], D. While the switch is on, the inductor has the battery at one end and the output at the other. So it sees Vin − Vout, and its current climbs, as v = L di/dt says. When the switch opens, the inductor keeps its current going through the diode.[^3] The diode holds that end near 0 V. So the inductor now sees Vout the other way round, and its current falls.\n\nIn steady running the current must end each cycle where it began. So the voltage across the inductor must average zero.[^4] Drawn against time, the area above the line must equal the area below. That gives (Vin − Vout) × D = Vout × (1 − D).[^5] Multiply out, and Vout × D cancels from both sides, leaving Vout = D × Vin. So 48 V at 50% gives 24 V, and at 25% gives 12 V. If the output is too low, the area above is bigger, so the current gains a little each cycle. That extra current charges the capacitor until the areas balance. The switch is fully on or fully off, so it wastes little heat.[^1] The switch passes current only while it is on, so at 50% the battery gives 5 A on average for a 10 A load. So power in matches power out, less small losses. That is why switching supplies are more efficient than a transistor held part on.[^9] The current ripples by (Vin − Vout) × D ÷ (f × L) each cycle. A good first estimate for that ripple is 20% to 40% of the output current.[^6]\n\nA [[boost converter]] puts the inductor straight off the battery and the switch from its far end to 0 V. While on, the inductor sees the whole of Vin and stores energy.[^7] When off, it drives its current through the diode into an output above the battery.[^8] The same balance, Vin × D = (Vout − Vin) × (1 − D), gives Vout = Vin ÷ (1 − D). So 48 V at 50% gives 96 V. The battery then supplies more current than the load takes, 20 A for 10 A. A converter does not make energy. This is a simplified picture, with an ideal switch and diode and an inductor current that never falls to zero. **Chop DC through an inductor and a capacitor, and the inductor's zero average voltage sets the output: D × Vin for a buck, Vin ÷ (1 − D) for a boost.**"

While the inductor's current never falls to zero, the frequency sets the ripple, not the output voltage. As the battery voltage changes, D must change to hold the output.\n\n**Worked example:** a 48 V battery must feed a 12 V load drawing 10 A, switching at 50 kHz through 100 µH. D = 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25, so 25%. The battery supplies about 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 A, or 120 W, the same as the load's 12 V × 10 A. The ripple is 36 × 0.25 ÷ (50,000 × 0.0001) = 1.8 A, 18% of 10 A. That is a little under the 20% to 40% guide, so this inductor is on the large side.
