   "now": "A power transistor is a switch you work with a small signal on its control lead. In a converter it is kept fully on or fully off. Held half on, it would turn far more of the power into heat. Fully on or fully off, it controls the load while making little heat.[^5]\n\nThe heat in any switch is the voltage across it times the current through it. Take 600 V DC, close to a 440 V drive's DC link, feeding a 30 Ω load. Fully off, the whole 600 V sits across the switch. Only a tiny leakage current flows, so the heat is tiny. Fully on, about 20 A flows. A real transistor still keeps a small drop, say 2 V. So it makes about 2 × 20 = 40 W. Half on, it acts like a resistor in series with the load. It takes half the supply, 300 V, and passes half the current, 10 A. Now both are large at once, so it makes 300 × 10 = 3000 W. At each end one of them is near zero, so the heat peaks at half on. For a resistive load that peak is V² ÷ (4R), where V is the supply and R the load. Here that is 600² ÷ 120 = 3000 W. Small transistors behave the same way. Partly on, a small one might make 600 mW.[^3] As a switch, the same part makes about 24 mW fully on.[^4]\n\nHeat becomes temperature through the heat sink. Its [[thermal resistance]] says how far the junction, the silicon inside, rises above the air for each watt. Say 0.5 °C per watt, with the air at 40 °C. Fully on, 40 W lifts the junction 20 °C, to 60 °C. Half on, 3000 W would lift it 1500 °C. Say the transistor is rated to 150 °C. It fails long before that. An IGBT should not be run half on for this reason.[^6] Switching has its own cost. No switch changes over instantly. During each edge it sweeps through half on, so each edge makes a burst of heat. This [[switching loss]] is the energy per switching times the switching frequency.[^1] Say each edge takes 0.5 µs. Here it then costs about 1 mJ, or 2 mJ a cycle. At 10 kHz the edges make 20 W, and at 20 kHz they make 40 W. On half the time, the on-state heat averages 20 W. So switching makes about 40 W at 10 kHz, against 3000 W held half on. Both give the load about 300 V on average.\n\nThree kinds of power transistor share the job. A BJT, a bipolar transistor, turns on with a current into its base, held all the time it is on. A MOSFET turns on with a voltage on its gate, which draws almost no current except while it changes over. An IGBT puts a MOSFET's gate in charge of a bipolar output, and suits high voltages and currents. The heat rule is the same for all three. Coils add a danger of their own. A coil keeps its current going, as v = L di/dt says. Open its switch and the current has no other path. The switch tries to stop it in about 0.5 µs, the edge time. So di/dt is huge, and the coil can throw a spike far above the supply across the switch. A [[freewheeling diode]] across the coil, fitted the way that blocks the supply, gives the current a path round. The current flows on through the diode, so the coil sees only one diode drop, about 0.7 V.[^2] The switch then sees the supply plus that drop, about 601 V. This is a simplified picture, with one fixed on drop and straight edges. Real devices differ in both, and coil loads make each edge cost more. **A power transistor in a converter is kept fully on or fully off, because half on, its voltage times its current makes far more heat.**"
