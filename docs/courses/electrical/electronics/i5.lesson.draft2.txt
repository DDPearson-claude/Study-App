  "text": "A thyristor is a diode that waits for a pulse on its gate, its control lead. Once fired, it stays on until its current falls away. So an ordinary thyristor's gate chooses when it starts in each half cycle, never when it stops. Fire it later, and less of each half reaches the load. That gives less DC.\n\nLike a diode, a thyristor blocks reverse voltage. Unlike a diode, it also blocks forward voltage until its gate gets a pulse. The pulse fires it, and it latches on. The gate then has no more say. The thyristor lets go only when its current falls below a small [[holding current]].[^5] On AC that happens just before each zero, so it must be fired again every cycle.[^6] A second pulse while it conducts changes nothing. Removing the gate signal changes nothing either. The angle in each half cycle at which the gate fires is the [[firing angle]], α.\n\nThe load gets the slice of each half from α to 180°. Its average is the slice's area spread over the time. The area under a sine from α to 180° is 1 + cos α, the integral of the sine. A whole hump gives 2. So firing at 60° keeps 1.5 out of 2, three quarters of the area, from two thirds of the time. The start of a hump is low, so cutting it costs little. Swap a bridge rectifier's four diodes for thyristors, and it becomes a controlled rectifier. With a heater as its load it averages (Vmax ÷ π)(1 + cos α), where Vmax is the peak. On 230 V the peak is 325 V. So firing at 0° gives the diode bridge's 207 V, 60° gives 155 V and 90° gives 104 V. A [[triac]] behaves like two thyristors back to back,[^9] so it chops both halves and passes AC. Fired at 90°, it gives a heater half its full power, because each half keeps exactly half its squared area.\n\nA charger adds a choke, a large coil, to keep its current steady. The choke carries the current through each zero, so each pair of thyristors conducts on until the other pair fires. The average then becomes (2Vmax ÷ π) × cos α, about 207 V × cos α on 230 V. Past 90° it would turn negative, with power flowing from the DC side back to the supply.[^8] Firing later has a cost. The supply's current becomes a block that starts α after the voltage. Part of each block now flows while the voltage is low or reversed. So each amp delivers less power, and the power factor falls.[^2] For this bridge it is about 0.9 × cos α. So a charger cut to 104 V runs at a power factor of about 0.45. This is a simplified picture, with ideal switches, a perfectly steady choke current and a supply with no inductance of its own. **A thyristor is a diode that waits for a gate pulse, so firing it later each half cycle gives less DC.**"
