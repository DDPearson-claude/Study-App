   "now": "A diode lets current through one way only. Point a few of them the right ways, and both halves of the AC push current the same way through a load. That is a [[rectifier]]: AC in, DC out. The DC still pulses, because it follows the humps of the supply's wave.\n\nStart with one diode in series with a resistor load on 230 V. The supply peaks at 325 V. In the positive half the diode conducts, and the load gets the hump. In the negative half the diode blocks. The load gets nothing, and the diode holds the supply. So half of every cycle is wasted. Now use four diodes in a diamond, a [[bridge rectifier]]. The supply joins two opposite corners, and the load joins the other two. In the positive half, supply wire A is the high one. D1 carries current from wire A to the top of the load. D2 returns it from the bottom to wire B. In the negative half, wire B is the high one. Now D3 carries the current to the top of the load, and D4 returns it to wire A. Each pair conducts while the other pair blocks. So whatever the polarity of the input, the current through the load flows the same way.[^1] The negative half is flipped up, not thrown away. The blocked pair is not idle, though. The conducting pair joins each blocked diode straight across the supply. So a blocked diode holds the whole supply the wrong way round, up to its 325 V peak.\n\nThe DC level is the average, area ÷ length over a cycle. One hump of a sine has an area of twice the peak, in volts times radians. The half-wave has one hump in a cycle 2π long. So it averages the peak ÷ π, 0.318 of the peak. On 230 V that is about 104 V. The bridge fills the gap with a second hump. With no diode losses it averages 0.637 of the peak,[^2] about 207 V. Its output rises and falls twice a cycle, 100 times a second on a 50 Hz supply.[^3] That rise and fall is the [[ripple]]. The current always passes through two diodes in series, so a bridge takes about 1.4 V. That is under 1% of 207 V, but about 12% of a 12 V charger's output.\n\nA three-phase bridge uses six diodes. Each line has an upper diode to the positive rail and a lower diode from the negative rail. The positive rail can sit no lower than the highest line. So that line's upper diode conducts, and every other upper diode is pushed backwards and blocks. Likewise the lowest line holds the negative rail through its lower diode. So the load gets the largest line-to-line voltage of that moment. The largest changes every 60°, so each pair conducts for 60°, and there are six pulses a cycle. Each top is a slice of a cosine from 30° before its peak to 30° after it. So the output only dips to cos 30°, 0.866 of the peak. Area ÷ length over one top gives 3 ÷ π of the peak. The peak is √2 times the line voltage. So with a resistor load the average is 3√2 ÷ π, about 1.35 times the line voltage. That gives 540 V on 400 V and 594 V on 440 V. This is a simplified picture, with ideal diodes into a plain resistor. Real diodes take their drops, and a real supply hands over from one diode to the next a little less sharply. **Diodes in a bridge steer both halves of the AC the same way through the load, so the output is DC that pulses.**[^1]"
