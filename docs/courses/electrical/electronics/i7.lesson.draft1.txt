  "text": "An inverter builds AC from DC with switches that are only ever fully on or fully off. It varies how long each switch stays on, cycle by cycle, so that the average voltage traces a sine. The motor's inductance smooths the current, so the current follows that average.\n\nStart from lesson 6. A leg is two switches in series across the DC link, with the output between them. Over each switching cycle, its average is D × the link voltage. Make D rise and fall like a sine, and the average rises and falls like that sine. To set D, compare the sine you want, the reference, with a fast triangle, the carrier. The top switch is on whenever the reference is above the triangle. This is [[pulse width modulation]], PWM. Run a second leg as the mirror of the first, with the load between them, and you have an [[H-bridge]]. Its average swings either side of zero: AC. Three legs, with references 120° apart, give three-phase.\n\nSo the reference sets two things. Its frequency is the output frequency. Its depth sets the duty cycles, and so the output voltage.[^1] Take the 540 V link a 400 V supply gives. At full depth each leg's average swings 270 V either side of its middle. Between two legs 120° apart that becomes √3 × 270 = 468 V peak, or 331 V rms. For 200 V at 25 Hz, set the reference to 25 Hz and its depth to 200 ÷ 331, about 60%. The carrier frequency sets neither. It sets how fine the current's sawtooth is, and how much switching heat the IGBTs make. The motor filters the pulses into a near-sine current.[^2]\n\nOne limit sits in each leg. An IGBT turns off more slowly than it turns on. So the gate drive leaves a [[dead time]] with both switches off, or the pair would short the link.[^7] A long gap distorts the sine near its zeros, so the gap is kept as short as is safe.[^8] This is a simplified picture: ideal switches, a steady link, and a motor that makes no voltage of its own. **Make a leg's duty cycle follow a sine, and the pulses' average and the motor's current follow it. The reference's frequency sets the output frequency, and its depth sets the voltage.**"

  "text": "Isolate and prove dead before you work on a drive or a UPS. Its DC link holds charge after isolation. A UPS battery can keep the link live with the mains off.\n\n- Find the DC link: about 1.35 × the line voltage from a three-phase bridge.[^17]\n- Plain sine-triangle gives at most 0.612 × the link between lines, at full depth.\n- Depth needed = the voltage you want ÷ that maximum. The output frequency is the reference's.\n- Choose the carrier as a trade: faster gives a smoother current, slower gives cooler switches.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** the reference sets the output, and the carrier only sets how fine the pulses are. On a motor, voltage and frequency move together. Dead time must cover the switches' turn-off, and little more.\n\n**Worked example:** a 440 V, 60 Hz motor must run at 30 Hz from a drive on a 440 V supply. The link is about 1.35 × 440 = 594 V. Full depth gives 0.612 × 594 = 364 V between lines. At 30 Hz the motor needs 440 × 30 ÷ 60 = 220 V. So set the reference to 30 Hz, and the depth to 220 ÷ 364, about 60%.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Thinking the carrier sets the output frequency. It sets the sawtooth and the switching heat.\n- Reading a zero on the reference as the switches off. It is 50% on, the middle of the sine.\n- Thinking no dead time gives the cleanest wave. It shorts the link through the leg.\n- Expecting a sine on a scope across the motor lines. You see pulses at the full link voltage. Their average is the sine.\n- Thinking an online UPS changes over when the mains fails. Its inverter runs all the time.[^10]"
