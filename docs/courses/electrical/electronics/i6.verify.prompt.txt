Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- A buck converter takes its input from a DC source such as a battery, switching it through a transistor into a diode, inductor and capacitor. [R1]

- A boost converter steps a voltage up by storing energy in an inductor and releasing it to the load at a higher voltage. [R3]
- In a boost converter, when the switch is on the inductor voltage is about the input voltage and energy is stored in the inductor. [R4]
- A switching supply is more efficient than a linear one, and can step a voltage up, step it down or invert it. [R5]
- In a converter's steady state the average voltage across the inductor is zero. [R6]
- When a buck converter's switch opens, the inductor current keeps flowing through the diode and charges the output. [R7]
- A good first estimate for a buck converter's inductor ripple current is 20% to 40% of the output current. [R8]

Rearrange the same parts, and you get a steady higher DC instead.\n\nThe circuit is a switching transistor followed by a diode, an inductor and a capacitor.[^2] This is a [[buck converter]]. The share of each cycle the switch is on is the [[duty cycle]], D. While the switch is on, the inductor has the battery at one end and the output at the other. So it sees Vin − Vout, and its current climbs, as v = L di/dt says. When the switch opens, the inductor keeps its current going through the diode.[^3] The diode holds that end near 0 V. So the inductor now sees Vout the other way round, and its current falls.\n\nIn steady running the current must end each cycle where it began. So the voltage across the inductor must average zero.[^4] Drawn against time, the area above the line must equal the area below. That gives (Vin − Vout) × D = Vout × (1 − D).[^5] Multiply out, and Vout × D cancels from both sides, leaving Vout = D × Vin. So 48 V at 50% gives 24 V, and at 25% gives 12 V. If the output is too low, the area above is bigger, so the current gains a little each cycle. That extra current charges the capacitor until the areas balance. The switch is fully on or fully off, so it wastes little heat.[^1] The battery gives current only while the switch is on. At 50% that is 5 A on average for a 10 A load. So power in matches power out, less small losses. That is why switching supplies are more efficient than a transistor held part on.[^9] The current ripples by (Vin − Vout) × D ÷ (f × L) each cycle.
