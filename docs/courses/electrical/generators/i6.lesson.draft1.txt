  "text": "On a board with other sets running, a set's two controls change jobs. Alone, its fuel set its frequency and its field set its voltage. In parallel, every set shares one frequency and one voltage. So fuel decides how much of the kW a set carries, and field decides how much of the kVAr.[^26] Both are moved through the governor's and the AVR's set points.[^7]\n\nStart with why fuel cannot speed a paralleled set up. Its rotor is locked in step with the board, held there by the same pull that brought it into step as it closed. So more fuel pushes the rotor a few degrees further ahead instead. That widens the load angle δ between E and V. Now look again at the drawing of E as V plus the drop across Xs. The in-step part of the current makes the part of the drop that stands square to V. So E × sin δ = Xs × the in-step current. Multiply both sides by V and divide by Xs. V × the in-step current is the power. So, in per unit, power = E × V × sin δ ÷ Xs. With E at 2.25, V at 1.0 and Xs at 1.5, a 32.3° angle gives 2.25 × 0.534 ÷ 1.5 = 0.80 per unit. That is the set's full 504 kW. A wider angle sends out more. So a set's kW follows its engine's output.[^1]\n\nHow the kW divides comes from the droop lines. Take two 504 kW sets at 4% droop on 60 Hz. Each runs at 62.4 Hz with no load and 60 Hz at full load. So each takes 504 ÷ 2.4 = 210 kW for each hertz it falls. Both must run at the board's one frequency, and their kW must add up to the load. On a 600 kW load they settle at 300 kW each, at 62.4 − 300 ÷ 210 = 60.97 Hz. Raise A's setting by 0.3 Hz. A's extra fuel starts to push the board faster, and B's governor sees the rise and backs off. They settle with A on 331.5 kW and B on 268.5 kW, at 61.12 Hz. The frequency rose by half the nudge, because two sets shared it. In a big group it hardly moves.[^2] A board so big that no one set can move its frequency or voltage is an [[infinite bus]]. Droop is what makes the sharing steady. Two isochronous sets have flat lines, so they have no crossing to settle at. Set a little apart, the higher one takes the whole load, and the other has its fuel cut to nothing and is driven round as a motor. Textbooks add that isochronous sets share only through a link between their governors that measures each set's kW.\n\nThe field shares the kVAr the same way. Raise one set's field and its E grows, so it pushes out more lagging current. In a group the board's voltage hardly rises. Instead that set takes a bigger part of the kVAr, and its power factor falls.[^3] Its kW stays put, because only fuel moves kW. Each AVR can let its voltage fall a little as its kVAr rises. That [[voltage droop]] makes the AVRs share as droop governors do. Here each falls 1 V for every 30 kVAr, an example setting. Raise A's setting by 2 V and A carries 255 kVAr against B's 195, with the board up just 1 V. The extra 30 kVAr flows out of A and into B and does no work for the load. That is a [[cross current]], 30 000 ÷ (√3 × 440) = 39.4 A, warming both windings. Textbooks add a cure, cross-current compensation. It links the AVRs so each compares its kVAr with the other's and trims its field until they match. These figures come from a simplified picture with straight droop lines and example settings. **In parallel, more fuel moves kW onto a set and more field moves kVAr onto it, while the board's frequency and voltage barely change.**[^26]"

  "text": "**Sharing load on a running board**\n\nThe safe way first: where a power management system or load-sharing controller is fitted, let it share the load. Trim by hand only as your procedures allow.\n- Read each set's kW, kVAr and power factor, or its current.\n- Move kW with the governor controls only. Lower the heavy set's setting and raise the light one's by the same amount, so the frequency holds.\n- Bring the frequency back to rated by moving both settings together, the same way. That moves the frequency and leaves the shares alone.\n- Move kVAr with the voltage controls only, the same way, until the power factors match.\n- Check the currents, not only the kW. Two sets on equal kW but unequal currents are carrying a cross current.\n- To take a set off, lower its setting until its kW is small, then open its breaker. Stop before its kW reaches zero, or it is driven as a motor.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** a set takes its rating ÷ its droop in hertz for each hertz below its setting, 504 ÷ 2.4 = 210 kW on a 4% set. On two equal sets, a nudge to one setting moves the frequency by half the nudge. Raise one and lower the other, and the frequency stays put. Equal kW and equal power factor on equal sets means no cross current.\n\n**Worked example:** two 504 kW sets at 4% droop share 600 kW. A is set at 62.7 Hz and B at 62.1 Hz, so A carries 363 kW and B 237 kW, at 60.97 Hz. Each should carry 300 kW, so 63 kW must move. At 210 kW per hertz that is 63 ÷ 210 = 0.3 Hz. Lower A to 62.4 Hz and raise B to 62.4 Hz. The frequency stays at 60.97 Hz. Then lower both by 0.97 Hz together, to 61.43 Hz. The board comes back to 60 Hz with 300 kW on each. Then look at the kVAr. If A shows 0.76 power factor and B 0.84 on equal kW, A's field is high. Lower A's voltage setting and raise B's until they match.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Giving one paralleled set more fuel to raise the frequency. It takes load off the others instead.\n- Raising one set's field to lift the board voltage. It takes kVAr off the others and drives a cross current.\n- Running two isochronous governors with no link between them. One takes the load, and the other is driven as a motor.\n- Judging the share by kW alone. A set can carry its share of kW and still be overloaded by kVAr."
