Topic: Generators and paralleling
His level: KNOWS A LITTLE: he has met the basics but may hold common misconceptions.
This lesson: i1 "The alternator: a turning field makes three phases" — Direct current in the spinning rotor makes a field that sweeps past three fixed coils 120° apart, giving three phases, one cycle per pole pair. [mechanism]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i2 Excitation and the AVR: field current sets the voltage — The automatic voltage regulator, or AVR, measures the output and adjusts the current in the turning field to hold the voltage steady as load changes.
- i3 The loaded generator: synchronous reactance and the phasor — Synchronous reactance, the machine's inner reactance, drops voltage under load; drawn tip to tail, it shows the internal voltage the field must make.
- i4 The governor: fuel, speed and droop — The governor trims fuel to hold speed under load, and droop lets the speed fall a set few per cent from no load to full load.
- i5 Synchronising: closing onto a live board — Close onto a live board only when voltage, frequency, phase sequence and phase angle match, sending the close early for the breaker's closing time.
- i6 Load sharing: kW by fuel, kVAr by field — In parallel, more fuel moves kW onto a set and more field moves kVAr onto it, while the board's frequency and voltage barely change.
- i7 Protection: reverse power, a lost field and the short circuit — Each generator fault has its own signature, reverse power, a lost field or a short-circuit current falling with time, and protection acts on it.
- i8 The generator set in a power system — A big motor's start dips a set's voltage before the AVR can act, so big loads start softly, in sequence or with more sets on.

  "text": "A generator set's alternator is a magnet turning inside fixed coils. A north pole and then a south pole sweep past each coil. That makes one full cycle of voltage in it. Three coils spaced round the magnet make three waves 120° apart, the three phases.\n\nThe fixed outer part is the [[stator]], and it carries the coils. The turning part is the [[rotor]]. Direct current in the rotor's winding makes it an electromagnet, with poles that do not change. The engine turns the rotor, and its field turns with it.[^3] As a north pole comes up to a coil, more of its flux passes through the coil. As the pole moves on, that flux falls away. Then a south pole sends flux through the other way. A coil makes voltage only while the flux through it changes, just as in a transformer. So a pole parked in front of a coil makes nothing, however strong it is. A north and a south passing make one full cycle. On a 2-pole machine, coils B and C sit 120° round the stator from coil A. They see the same poles a third of a turn later. So their waves are the same shape, each 120° behind the last.\n\nA rotor can carry more than one north and one south. A north and a south together make a [[pole pair]], and each pole pair sweeping past a coil makes one cycle. So a 4-pole rotor makes two cycles in each coil every turn.
