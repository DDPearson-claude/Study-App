   "now": "A generator's protection works by recognising what each fault does to the set's meters. On a set in parallel, an engine that stops sends real power the wrong way. A lost field draws kVAr in and pushes the load angle towards 90°. A short circuit sends a huge current that falls with time. Each protection watches for one of these marks. Start with the engine that stops. In parallel, A's rotor is locked in step with the board. So when its fuel goes, it cannot simply slow down. The other sets hold the board's speed and drive A round as a motor. Real power now flows into A.[^1] That flow is [[reverse power]]. On two sets sharing 400 kW, A might draw 50.4 kW, an example figure, and B then carries 450.4 kW. Textbooks add that a reverse-power relay picks up once the inward flow passes its setting. Then it waits a few seconds before it opens the set's breaker. The wait sorts real faults from brief ones. A set just closed on can send a little power back for a second or two, until its governor picks up load. A stopped engine keeps drawing for as long as it stays on the board. So a nuisance trip like this is better cured with a longer delay than a higher pickup. A higher pickup could miss a stopped engine that draws only a little.\n\nA lost field shows a different mark. Remember from load sharing that power = E × V × sin δ ÷ Xs, in per unit. The engine still sends out its kW. So as E shrinks, sin δ must grow to carry it. Take A carrying 200 kW on its 630 kVA rating, 0.32 per unit, with Xs at 1.5. With a healthy field, say E = 1.44, δ is 19.3°. At E = 0.80, δ has grown to 36.5°. The kVAr follows the part of E that lies in line with V. Once that part falls below V, the set stops sending kVAr out and draws it in instead. At E = 0.80, A draws 150 kVAr from the board. At 90°, sin δ is 1, its largest. So once E falls below 0.32 × 1.5 = 0.48, no angle can carry the kW. The rotor slips a pole, and goes on slipping. The board then sees circulating currents and surges of torque and current.[^2] Textbooks add that loss-of-field protection watches for signs such as the inward kVAr, and trips the set, ideally before it slips.\n\nA short circuit has a third mark. Its current starts huge and falls with time. Textbooks explain the start this way. The rotor's windings resist any sudden change in their flux, as any coil resists a sudden change in its current. So for the first few cycles only a small reactance, the sub-transient reactance, holds the current back. That grip fades in two stages. The current falls through three periods, sub-transient, transient and sustained.[^3] Its plot against time is the set's [[decrement curve]]. On a 630 kVA, 440 V set, rated current is 630 000 ÷ (√3 × 440) = 826.7 A. With an example sub-transient reactance of 0.15 per unit, the first current is 826.7 ÷ 0.15 = 5511 A. Where it settles depends on the field. Shorted at its terminals, the set's own drawing leaves only Xs to hold the current back. So the steady current is E ÷ Xs. With the field held at its full-load E of 2.25, that is 2.25 ÷ 1.5 = 1.5 times rated, 1240 A. To hold 3 times rated, the AVR must force the field to twice that.\n\nThat forcing needs a supply the fault cannot pull down. A shunt-fed AVR takes its power from the set's own output. A short circuit pulls that output down, so the field fails, and the current dies away to nothing.[^4] A PMG on the shaft keeps the AVR fed whatever the output does. So the set can hold its output while the protection downstream clears the fault.[^5] It is easy to assume every set holds a large fault current for as long as needed. Only a set whose AVR keeps its supply can. Overload has the last answer. Sometimes one set is left carrying more than its rating. Textbooks add that [[preferential tripping]] then sheds non-essential loads in timed steps. The set and the essential loads stay on. These figures come from a simplified picture, with example settings, smooth decays and sets that hold their speed. **Each generator fault has its own signature, power flowing in, kVAr flowing in as δ climbs, or a short-circuit current falling with time, and each protection is set to see its own.**"
