  "text": "A generator set's voltage is set by the current in its turning field. A load drags that voltage down. So an [[automatic voltage regulator]], or AVR, measures the output and trims the field current to hold the voltage steady.\n\nRun a set at its steady speed with nothing connected. Its voltage then depends on the field current alone, because the field current sets the flux. Plot one against the other and you get the [[open-circuit curve]]. It climbs in step at first. Then it bends over at a knee, because the iron saturates, as on a B-H curve. Past the knee, each extra step of field buys less voltage. On the sketch here, doubling the field from 100% to 200% takes 440 V only to 555 V.\n\nConnect an ordinary load and the voltage falls. The load's current drops voltage inside the machine, much as a transformer's output falls on load. With the field held, the voltage stays down. So the field must rise as the load rises. The main field turns with the shaft, and a brushless set feeds it with no sliding contact. A smaller alternator on the same shaft, the [[exciter]], has its field fixed in the stator and its coils turning on the shaft. A diode bridge riding on the shaft turns their AC into DC for the main field. The AVR feeds only the exciter's fixed field. The engine turning the shaft supplies the power, so a small current there steers a much bigger one in the main field. It works as a loop. It measures the output, compares it with its set point and corrects the field. Say the drop is 50 V at half load. The AVR raises the field until the curve gives 490 V inside, at 125%. Then 490 − 50 = 440 V reaches the board. At full load the drop is 100 V, and 540 V needs 175%. The second half of the load costs twice the field of the first, because of the knee.\n\nThe AVR needs power too. A shunt supply takes it from the set's own output, so the set is self-excited. A permanent magnet generator, or PMG, on the same shaft gives power as long as the shaft turns. A self-excited set cannot keep up a short-circuit current. It dies away quickly unless the fault is cleared at once.[^2] These numbers come from a simplified picture, a sketched curve and a drop that grows in step with the load. A real drop depends on the kind of load, and a leading load can even raise the voltage. **On a set running alone, the AVR holds the output voltage by adjusting the strength of the exciter's field, which sets the main field current.[^1]**"

  "text": "**Safety first:** treat a turning set as live, even with its breaker open and its AVR off. Its output, its exciter and its rotating diodes all carry voltage while the shaft turns, and the iron's leftover magnetism makes some voltage with no field current.\n\n- Find out which supply feeds the AVR: shunt, PMG or a winding of its own. It tells you how the set behaves in a fault.\n- On load, read the field current, or the AVR's output, with the voltage. As the load rises, the field should rise and the voltage hold.\n- If the voltage sags on load while the field stays put, the AVR is not correcting. Check its supply, its sensing lead and the exciter's field.\n- If the field sits high on the curve at full load, the set is working past the knee. Each extra step of load then costs more field.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** once the set works past the knee, each extra 1% of load costs more field than the last.\n\n**Worked example:** take a set that makes 440 V with nothing connected at 100% field, with a drop of 1 V for each 1% of load and the curve drawn here. At 80% load the drop is 80 V. So the AVR must make 440 + 80 = 520 V inside. The curve gives 520 V at 150%, so the field settles at 150%. With the AVR off and the field left at 100%, the same load gives 440 − 80 = 360 V.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Expecting a set to hold its voltage by itself. With the field fixed, the voltage falls on load and stays down.\n- Thinking the AVR of a brushless set drives the main field. It feeds the exciter's fixed field, and the exciter and diodes feed the main field.\n- Expecting double the field to give double the voltage. Past the knee it gives much less.\n- Expecting a self-excited set to keep feeding a short circuit. Its AVR loses its power with the output."
