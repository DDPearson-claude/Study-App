  "text": "A loaded generator drops voltage inside itself, much as a transformer does, only far more. In each phase the field makes a voltage inside, E. The load current pushes through a reactance on its way to the terminals. So E must be the terminal voltage V plus that drop, added as arrows, tip to tail.\n\nTwo effects make that reactance. The stator's current makes a field of its own, which alters the rotor's. Some of the winding's flux also leaks past. Together they act like one reactance in series with each phase, the [[synchronous reactance]], Xs.[^1] The winding's resistance is there too, but it is small and left out here. Xs is given as a fraction of the set's own rating, in [[per unit]]. Per unit is per cent ÷ 100, so it is your percentage impedance in another form. Here Xs is 1.5 per unit, an example value. So rated current drops 1.5 times the terminal voltage inside. Textbooks add that a generator's synchronous reactance is far larger than a transformer's reactance.\n\nThe drop across a reactance runs 90° ahead of its current. At unity power factor the current lies along V, so the drop stands at right angles from V's tip. With V held at 1.0 and rated current flowing, E = √(1.0² + 1.5²) = 1.80.   "text": "**Working out the voltage inside**\n\n- Put V along the reference at 1.0 per unit.\n- Split the load current. The part in step with V is I × the power factor. The part at right angles is I × the sine of its angle.\n- Multiply each part by Xs. The in-step part gives the drop across V. The other gives the drop along V, added for a lagging load and taken off for a leading one.\n- Find E's part along V, 1.0 plus or minus that drop. Then E = √(along² + across²), and δ is the angle whose tangent is across ÷ along.\n- Multiply by the base, here 440 V, to read E in volts.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** at the same current, the more lagging the load, the more field it takes. A leading load takes less. So capacitors on a lightly loaded set can leave the AVR little room to cut the field.\n\n**Worked example:** a set with Xs of 1.5 per unit carries half its rated current, 0.5 per unit, at 0.6 lagging. The AVR holds V at 1.0. The sine of the current's angle is 0.8. Across V: 1.5 × 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.45. Along V: 1.0 + 1.5 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 1.6. E = √(1.6² + 0.45²) = 1.66 per unit, or 1.66 × 440 = 730 V on the 440 V base. δ = tan⁻¹(0.45 ÷ 1.6) = 16°. With the field in proportion to E, it sits at 166% of its no-load value.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Adding the drop straight onto V. That gives 1.0 + 1.5 = 2.5 at any power factor.
