   "now": "The governor and the AVR are the two basic controls on every set.[^4] The AVR trims the field to hold the voltage. The governor trims the fuel to hold the speed, and so the frequency. A sudden load slows the set first, and the governor then adds fuel to catch it. Where the speed settles after that is a setting.\n\nOn its own, a set makes whatever power its load draws. The alternator pulls back on the shaft with a torque, power ÷ speed. The engine's torque must match it, or the gap speeds up or slows the spinning mass. Add 126 kW to a set turning at 1800 rev/min, which is 188.5 rad/s. The load's torque rises by 126 000 ÷ 188.5 = 668 N m at once. The fuel cannot follow at once, so for a moment the engine falls short. With 20 kg m² on the shaft, an example value, the set slows at 668 ÷ 20 = 33.4 rad/s². Say the governor takes 0.1 s to answer. By then the set has lost 3.34 rad/s, 1.8% of its speed. The frequency follows the speed, so it falls 1.8% too, from 60 to 58.9 Hz. This fall is a [[frequency dip]].[^1] The governor then adds fuel, the gap closes and the speed climbs back.\n\nWhere it climbs back to depends on the governor. Textbooks call a governor that brings the speed exactly back to its set point, at any load, [[isochronous]]. The other kind lowers its own target speed as it opens the fuel. More load needs more fuel, so the speed settles lower. That fall is its [[droop]]: the change in speed, as a percentage, that takes the governor through its full travel, from no load to full load.[^2] With 5% droop, full load runs at 100% of rated speed and no load at 105%.[^3] Take a 60 Hz set with 4% droop, set to give 60 Hz on full load. 4% of 60 Hz is 2.4 Hz. So no load runs at 62.4 Hz and full load at 60 Hz, with a straight line between. At half load, 252 kW of 504 kW, the set runs at 62.4 − 2.4 × 0.5 = 61.2 Hz. The set point slides the whole line up or down without tilting it.\n\nDroop is set on purpose. When sets run side by side, speed control is what shares the kW between them.[^5] On a set alone, its cost is that the frequency moves with the load. If all the load trips, the set settles at the top of its line, usually above its rated frequency. These figures come from a simplified picture, with a fixed response time and example values for the inertia. **The governor trims the fuel to hold the speed: isochronous brings it back exactly, and droop lets it settle a set few per cent lower from no load to full load.**"

   "now": "**Working out a droop line**\n\n- Find the droop in hertz: the droop % × the rated frequency ÷ 100.\n- If the set runs at its rated frequency on full load, its no-load frequency is the rated frequency plus the droop in hertz.\n- At any load, the frequency is the no-load frequency − the droop in hertz × the load ÷ the rated load.\n- To run at a chosen frequency at a chosen load, set the no-load set point to that frequency + the droop in hertz × the load ÷ the rated load.\n- For a sudden load, find the torque gap: the kW added × 1000 ÷ the speed in rad/s. The set slows at that gap ÷ the inertia until the fuel catches up.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** a sudden load dips the frequency before the governor can catch it. A set alone on droop runs faster on light load than on heavy load. It sits at its rated frequency only at the load its set point was chosen for.\n\n**Worked example:** a 50 Hz set rated 400 kW has 3% droop and runs at 50 Hz on full load. Its droop in hertz is 3 × 50 ÷ 100 = 1.5 Hz, so no load sits at 51.5 Hz. At 240 kW, 0.6 of its rating, it runs at 51.5 − 1.5 × 0.6 = 51.5 − 0.9 = 50.6 Hz. To run it at 50 Hz on 240 kW, set its no-load point to 50 + 0.9 = 50.9 Hz.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Treating droop as a fault. A set on droop that settles below its no-load frequency is doing what it was set to do.\n- Taking the droop % as hertz. 4% of 60 Hz is 2.4 Hz, not 4 Hz.\n- Expecting the governor to stop the dip. A governor that measures speed adds fuel only once the speed has started to fall, and the engine takes time to answer.\n- Forgetting that a load trip sends a set on droop to the top of its line, usually above its rated frequency."
