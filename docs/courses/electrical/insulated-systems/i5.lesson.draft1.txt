    "text": "An [[insulation monitor]] watches an insulated system's insulation while it runs. It pushes a small DC voltage, or a very slow AC one, between the system and the hull.[^1] The current that comes back gives the resistance to the hull of everything connected, all at once. Where to set its alarm is a question on which published guidance disagrees.\n\nThe older way to watch is three [[earth lamps]], each joining one phase to the hull. Each lamp shows its phase's voltage to earth. On a healthy 440 V supply, all three see 254 V and glow the same. A solid fault on L1 moves the hull to L1, as in the shifting triangle. L1's lamp goes out, and L2's and L3's see 440 V and glow brighter. But lamps only compare the phases. Suppose damp lowers the insulation equally on all three. Three equal leaks balance, as a balanced star load does, so the hull stays at the star point. Each lamp still sees 254 V, and all three stay equal. Lamps cannot see a fall that is the same on every phase.[^8]\n\nThe monitor measures resistance instead, like your insulation tester left connected. It uses DC because the cables' capacitance passes AC. At 60 Hz, 1 µF a phase looks like 884 Ω to the hull, all three together. That would swamp a 30 kΩ fault. At DC the capacitance charges and then passes nothing, so only the leakage resistance is left. Every leak, on every feeder and phase, is a path in parallel, so the monitor reads their total. Say six feeders read 3 MΩ each. Together that is 3,000 ÷ 6 = 500 kΩ. A 30 kΩ fault on one feeder brings the total to 500 × 30 ÷ 530 = 28.3 kΩ. The lowest path sets the reading. The lamps barely move for that fault, as the capacitance holds the hull near the star point. The capacitance still has to charge first, so the more of it, the slower the reading settles.\n\nAlarm levels are set in [[ohms per volt]], multiplied by the operating voltage. A ship classification rule alarms below 50 Ω/V.[^7] A maker's guide advises about 100 Ω/V.[^3] A maker's white paper calls 500 Ω/V typical.[^4] On 440 V those are 22 kΩ, 44 kΩ and 220 kΩ. So the 28.3 kΩ reading alarms at 100 and 500 Ω/V, but not at 50 Ω/V. A higher level warns sooner, but a large or damp system can sit below it with no single fault. This is a simplified model: it treats the lamps as voltmeters and gives every phase the same leak. **An insulation monitor reads the whole system's resistance to the hull with DC, so it sees leaks the lamps miss.**"

Which one is taught with generators and paralleling.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** The total is always below the lowest single path. So one bad feeder drags the whole reading down.\n\n**Worked example:** A 440 V board has four feeders reading 2 MΩ, 2 MΩ, 1 MΩ and 40 kΩ to the hull.
