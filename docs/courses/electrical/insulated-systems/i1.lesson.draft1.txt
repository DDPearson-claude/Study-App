    "text": "A three-phase supply's windings meet at its star point, and what that point is tied to decides what an earth fault does. Tie it to earth and a fault to a frame has a way home, so a large current flows and the supply trips. Leave it floating, an [[insulated system]], and the fault has no deliberate way home. Only a small current flows, nothing trips and the supply runs on. Two letters name the choice. The first says what the star point does: T for earth, from the French terre, or I for isolated.[^1] The second says how the frames reach earth: N through a conductor back to the supply's star point, or T through an electrode of their own.\n\nCurrent only flows round a complete loop, out of its source and back in. Picture a 440 V supply, say, so each winding has 440 ÷ 1.732 = 254 V across it. Line L1 touches a motor frame, and the frame is bonded to the hull. To flow, the current must get from the hull back to the star point, the far end of L1's winding. On TN a protective conductor runs straight there, all metal. Say that loop is 0.5 Ω. Then 254 ÷ 0.5 = 508 A flows, and the breaker trips at once.[^3] Earthing does not drain the fault away. It closes the loop so the current is big enough to trip. On TT the loop runs through the ground between two electrodes, say 10 Ω and 100 Ω. Now 254 ÷ 110 = 2.3 A flows, too little for a 32 A breaker, so an RCD does the tripping.\n\nOn IT the star point is isolated from earth, while the frames are still connected to earth.[^2] The fault reaches the hull and finds no conductor back to the star point. Its only way home is back through the insulation of the other lines. Say the whole system's insulation measures 100 kΩ to earth. Then 254 ÷ 100,000 gives about 2.5 mA. A [[first fault]] on an IT supply should cause no disconnection, and its current is measured in milliamps.[^4] The motor runs on, and an alarm tells someone the fault is there. A star point tied to earth through a high impedance, say 1,500 Ω, ends the same way: 254 ÷ 1,500 gives about 0.17 A, far too little to trip. Textbooks add that this counts as a form of IT. At sea that is the whole point.     "text": "- Safety first: the letters never tell you a conductor is dead. Isolate and prove dead as on any job.\n- Find the star point, on the drawing or at the generator or transformer. Is it tied to earth, tied through a high impedance or left floating?\n- Read the first letter: T means tied to earth, I means isolated.[^1]\n- Read the second: N means the frames go back to the star point through a protective conductor. T means they have an electrode of their own.\n- Trace the loop a fault to a frame would take, and what is in it: metal, the ground between two electrodes, or only insulation.\n- Divide the voltage across one winding by that loop's resistance. The answer tells you whether a breaker, an RCD or nothing will act.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** Hundreds of amps means a metal loop, TN, and the breaker trips. A few amps means the ground is in the loop, TT, and the RCD trips. A current too small to trip anything, with the supply running on, means an insulated supply.\n\n**Worked example:** A 440 V supply has 254 V across each winding. On TN with a 0.5 Ω loop, a fault to a frame drives 254 ÷ 0.5 = 508 A, and the breaker trips at once. Float the star point, with the whole system's insulation at 100 kΩ to earth. The same fault now drives 254 ÷ 100,000, about 2.5 mA. Nothing trips, and the motor runs on.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Reading the first letter as the frames. It is the star point.\n- Thinking the earth conductor drains a fault away. It closes the loop back to the star point.\n- Expecting a breaker to clear a TT fault. A few amps needs an RCD.\n- Taking an insulated supply's quiet first fault as harmless. It stays on the system until someone finds it and clears it."
