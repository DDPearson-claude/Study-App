Where to set its alarm is a question on which published guidance disagrees.\n\nThe older way to watch is three [[earth lamps]], each joining one phase to the hull. Each lamp shows its phase's voltage to earth. On a healthy 440 V supply, all three see 254 V and glow the same. A solid fault on L1 moves the hull to L1, as in the shifting triangle. L1's lamp goes out, and L2's and L3's see 440 V and glow brighter. But lamps only compare the phases. Suppose damp lowers the insulation equally on all three. Three equal leaks balance, as a balanced star load does, so the hull stays at the star point. Each lamp still sees 254 V, and all three stay equal. Lamps cannot see a fall that is the same on every phase.[^2]\n\nThe monitor measures resistance instead, like your insulation tester left connected. It uses DC because the cables' capacitance passes AC. At 60 Hz, three phases of 1 µF look like 884 Ω to the hull. That would swamp a 30 kΩ fault. At DC the capacitance charges and then passes nothing, so only the leakage resistance is left. Every leak, on every feeder and phase, is a path in parallel, so the monitor reads their total. Say six feeders read 3 MΩ each. Together that is 3,000 ÷ 6 = 500 kΩ. A 30 kΩ fault on one feeder brings the total to 500 × 30 ÷ 530 = 28.3 kΩ. The lowest path sets the reading. The lamps barely move for that fault. Beside the capacitance's 884 Ω, a 30 kΩ fault is weak, so the hull stays near the star point. The capacitance still has to charge first, so the more of it, the slower the reading settles.\n\nAlarm levels are set in [[ohms per volt]]. Multiply by the operating voltage to get ohms.
