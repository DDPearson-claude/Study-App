Treat each pole as live to the hull.[^10]\n- Measure each pole to the hull with a high-resistance meter. Then measure pole to pole.\n- Add the two pole readings. They should come to the bus voltage. If they fall well short, your meter is most likely loading the poles.\n- Equal halves mean balanced. That rules out a one-sided leak, but not an equal leak on both poles. If the board has an injecting monitor, read it too.\n- One pole low means that pole is leaking to the hull. The lower it reads, the lower its resistance.\n- Zero on one pole and the full bus on the other means a solid fault on the pole reading zero.[^11] A slight difference points to a partial one.[^12]\n- With one pole at zero, the other sits at the full bus voltage to the hull. A second fault on it would short the bus through the hull.[^13] On a bus above 120 V DC, that pole is past the safe touch voltage.[^3]\n\n**Rule of thumb:** The low pole is the leaky pole. The two readings add to the bus voltage.\n\n**Worked example:** A 24 V control board has 10 kΩ detector arms, an example value. The positive reads 4 V to the hull and the negative reads 20 V. They add to 24 V, so the meter is not loading them much. The positive reads low, so it is leaking. Say the negative has no leak, so its side is just its 10 kΩ arm. Each side's resistance is in proportion to its voltage, so the positive's side is 10 × 4 ÷ 20 = 2 kΩ. That is the 10 kΩ arm in parallel with the leak. So the leak is 1 ÷ (1/2 − 1/10) = 2.5 kΩ.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Blaming the pole that reads high.\n- Taking equal halves as healthy insulation.\n- Forgetting that your meter is a path to the hull.\n- Treating a floating bus as dead to the hull.\n- Reading two joined panels' detectors as one. Their arms add in parallel, so the same leak looks smaller."
