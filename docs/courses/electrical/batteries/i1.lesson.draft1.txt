  "explain": {
    "text": "A lead-acid cell is two plates standing in sulphuric acid. As it discharges, both plates turn into the same white substance, lead sulphate, and the acid is used up to make it. Charging runs all of that backwards. The positive plate is lead dioxide and the negative plate is plain lead. The acid, mixed with water, is the cell's [[electrolyte]].\n\nConnect a load and each plate reacts with the acid where they touch. At the lead plate, lead and acid form lead sulphate and set electrons free.[^1] At the lead dioxide plate, lead dioxide and acid form lead sulphate too, and take electrons in.[^2] So both plates end up as lead sulphate, and the acid is used up, leaving water behind.[^3] The acid is not a bath the plates sit in. It is one of the things being used, so its strength falls as the cell discharges.[^4] The freed electrons cannot cross the acid. They run round the outside wire, through your load, from the lead plate to the lead dioxide plate. Conventional current, as you draw it, runs the other way, out of the positive terminal. Inside the liquid the current is carried by [[ions]], the acid's charged pieces, drifting between the plates. An ion does not pick up electrons at one plate and carry them to the other.\n\nA charger pushes current in the other way, at a voltage above the cell's own. Textbooks add that this turns the lead sulphate back into lead and lead dioxide, giving the acid back to the liquid. So the acid's strength rises on charge.[^4]\n\nWhy about 2 V? Each plate has its own electrical push against the acid, fixed by what it is made of. Textbooks list them against the same reference: +1.685 V for lead dioxide and −0.356 V for lead. The cell's voltage is the gap, 1.685 + 0.356 = 2.04 V. A charged cell's acid is stronger than the tables assume, so it rests a little higher. Its [[nominal voltage]] is 2 V, and charged and rested it reads about 2.1 V.[^5] Plate size never enters that sum. A bigger plate holds more material to turn into lead sulphate, so it gives more ampere-hours at the same volts. For more volts you put cells in series. Six make 12 V, twelve make 24 V and twenty-four make 48 V. This is a simplified picture, without the separators, the grids inside the plates or the gas a cell gives off near full charge. So a battery's volts come from what its plates are made of, and its ampere-hours from how much of them can still react. **Discharging turns both plates into lead sulphate and uses up the acid, and charging runs it all backwards.**"

    "text": "**Safety first:** the electrolyte is strong acid, so wear eye protection and gloves near open cells. A battery has no off switch: its terminals are live whenever it holds charge.\n\n- Read the label for the nominal voltage and the ampere-hour rating.\n- Count the cells: nominal voltage ÷ 2 V.\n- Expect about 2.1 V a cell when charged and rested, so multiply the cell count by 2.1.\n- Take the ampere-hours from the rating, not from the voltage.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** the volts come from the plate materials, about 2 V a cell. The ampere-hours come from how much plate there is.\n\n**Worked example:** Say a ship's emergency battery is marked 24 V. 24 ÷ 2 = 12 cells in series. Charged and rested it should read about 12 × 2.1 = 25.2 V. A 48 V bank has 24 cells and reads about 50.4 V. A bigger 24 V battery has bigger cells, not more volts per cell.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Picturing electrons crossing the acid. They go round the wire, and ions carry the current inside.\n- Thinking ions pick up electrons at one plate and carry them to the other.\n- Treating the acid as a bath. It is used up on discharge and given back on charge.\n- Expecting a bigger cell to give more volts. It gives more ampere-hours.\n- Thinking only one plate changes. Both turn to lead sulphate."
