This lesson: i1 "The lead-acid cell: two plates and an acid" — Discharging a lead-acid cell turns both its plates to lead sulphate and takes acid out of the liquid, and charging runs that backwards. [mechanism]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i2 State of charge: two gauges, and when to trust them — The acid's strength, read as specific gravity, and the rested voltage both track the charge, once each reading is rested, corrected for temperature, level-checked and mixed.
- i3 Capacity and rate: what an ampere-hour is worth — A battery gives fewer ampere-hours the faster or colder you draw them, and Peukert's rough fit, found from two ratings, estimates how many fewer.
- i4 Charging: the charger holds volts per cell — A charger holds a set voltage per cell through bulk, topping and float, because a little too low sulphates the plates and too high corrodes them.
- i5 Gassing, hydrogen and the air that clears it — Near full charge, current the plates no longer take splits water into hydrogen and oxygen, so a battery room's vents are sized from the charger's largest current.
- i6 Strings: the weakest cell sets the pace — Cells in series share one current, so the weakest cell empties first on discharge and fills first on charge, and is overdriven either way.
- i7 Ageing, faults and the trend that finds them — A failing cell shows as readings drifting from its own baseline, so trend them and confirm with a capacity test; sources differ on how much drift matters.
- i8 Short circuits, safety and other chemistries — A large battery's few milliohms of resistance let it drive thousands of amps into a short, even part discharged, and its cells are always live.
