Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Lead acid uses constant-current constant-voltage charging: a regulated current raises the terminal voltage to the upper limit, then the current drops as the cell saturates. [R1]
- The charge voltage limit is critical and ranges from 2.30 V to 2.45 V per cell. [R2]
- Too low a limit risks sulphation on the negative plate; over-saturation by not switching to float causes grid corrosion on the positive plate. [R3]
- The recommended float voltage of most flooded lead acid batteries is 2.25 V to 2.27 V per cell, and large stationary batteries typically float at 2.25 V per cell at 25 degrees C. [R4]
- Charge voltage is reduced by 3 mV per cell for every degree above 25 degrees C and raised by 3 mV per cell for every degree below. [R5]
- An equalising charge raises the voltage to 2.50 V per cell, about 10 per cent above the recommended charge voltage, to bring cells to similar levels. [R6]
- Prolonged overcharge contributes to grid corrosion. [R7]
- With CCCV, lead acid is charged in three stages: constant-current (bulk), topping (a falling current that saturates the cell) and float (making up self-discharge). [R8][R9]
- Full charge is reached when the topping current falls to 3 to 5 per cent of the Ah rating. [R10]
