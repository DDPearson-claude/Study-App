Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i4 Charging: the charger holds volts per cell — A charger holds a set voltage per cell through bulk, topping and float, because a little too low sulphates the plates and too high corrodes them.
- i5 Gassing, hydrogen and the air that clears it — Near full charge, current the plates no longer take splits water into hydrogen and oxygen, so a battery room's vents are sized from the charger's largest current.

Peukert's rule, fitted through two of the maker's ratings, estimates how many fewer.\n\nPicture the battery as a fixed voltage behind a small resistance, both inside the case. The fixed voltage is the [[emf]], E. It is what the plates and acid push. A charged, rested cell reads about 2.1 V,[^1] so a battery of six cells has an emf of about 12.6 V. The small resistance is the [[internal resistance]], r. It is the plates, the acid and the joints between them. Say r is 7 mΩ, which is 0.007 Ω. Current through r drops volts there, just as current drops volts along a cable. So the terminals read E − Ir. At 100 A the lost volts are 100 × 0.007 = 0.70 V, and the terminals read 11.90 V. Read the emf there instead, a common slip, and you over-read by those 0.70 V. With nothing connected, no current flows and nothing is lost across r. A voltmeter draws almost nothing, so it reads the emf, 12.6 V. The battery does not set the current either. The load does. The current is E ÷ (R + r), where R is the load's resistance. Halve R and the current nearly doubles. It is not quite double, because r stays in the sum. The terminal voltage dips a little.\n\nMakers rate a lead-acid battery's ampere-hours at a slow, 20-hour discharge.[^3] Say a battery is rated 100 Ah. It gives 5 A for 20 hours. Drain the same battery in 2 hours and it may give only 56 Ah.[^2] That is a current of 28 A. Two things cause the shortfall. A discharge test ends when the terminal voltage falls to a set end voltage. More current means more lost volts, so it gets there sooner, with charge left in the plates. The acid must also soak into the plates to react. One way to picture it: at a high current the acid deep in the plates is used faster than fresh acid can reach it. Cold makes both worse. It raises the internal resistance and lowers the capacity. A battery giving 100 per cent at 27 °C typically gives only about 50 per cent at −18 °C.[^5]\n\nPeukert's rule puts a number on the rate effect. It says the current raised to a power n, times the run time, stays the same. That n is the battery's [[Peukert exponent]], and two ratings give it. Here 5ⁿ × 20 = 28ⁿ × 2, so 5.6ⁿ = 10, and n = log 10 ÷ log 5.6 = 1.34. A perfect battery, with the same capacity at any current, would have n = 1.00.[^4] Capacity is current times run time, so it goes as current to the power 1 − n. A power law is a straight line on log-log axes, and its power is the gradient. So the rule draws a straight line through both ratings, of gradient 1 − n, here −0.34. Now try 25 A.
