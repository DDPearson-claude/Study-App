  "text": "Use this whenever you work on, near or downstream of a battery.\n\n**Safe way first:**\n- Treat every battery as live. Its exposed metal always carries a voltage.[^6]\n- Switch off all circuits before connecting or disconnecting a battery.[^13] That stops their currents, and the cells stay live.\n- Keep metal off the battery. Don't rest tools on top of it.[^14]\n- Work on one connection at a time, and cover the terminals you are not working on.\n- Protect your eyes near the electrolyte. Battery electrolyte is sulphuric acid or potassium hydroxide.[^15] Both can permanently damage the eyes and burn the skin.[^16]\n- If it splashes, wash with plenty of clean water and get medical help at once.[^12]\n\n**To estimate a battery's fault current:**\n- Find the open-circuit voltage: the cells × the volts a cell.\n- Add every resistance in the loop: the cells, the links between them, and the cable out and back.\n- Divide the voltage by that total. That is the battery's share of the fault.\n- Add what the charger and any running motors can give.[^10]\n- Check each protective device against the fault just past it, at full charge. Its breaking capacity must be above that current.[^9]\n\n**Rule of thumb:** a shorted battery can give 100 to 1000 times its normal discharge current.[^1] A low voltage does not mean a low fault current.\n\n**Worked example:** A 24 V emergency battery has 12 lead-acid cells at 2.1 V, so it reads 25.2 V open circuit. Say its cells and links total 14 mΩ. Across its terminals, 25.2 ÷ 0.014 = 1800 A. A fault 2 m down a cable with 0.5 mΩ a metre in each core adds 2 mΩ. Then 25.2 ÷ 0.016 = 1575 A. So a device at the battery must break more than 1800 A, plus the charger's share. These are example values, and a real battery's figures come from its maker.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Thinking 12 V or 24 V is too low to matter. At about 60% charge it still gives over 90% of its full fault current.\n- Switching off the loads and treating the cells as dead.\n- Leaving the links, the cable or the battery's own resistance out of the sum.\n- Reading a nickel-cadmium cell's charge with a hydrometer."
