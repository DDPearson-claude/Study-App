  "explain": {
    "text": "Two gauges tell you how charged a lead-acid cell is. A hydrometer weighs the acid. A voltmeter reads the rested voltage. Both follow the acid's strength, so both follow the charge. Each can be fooled, though. Trust a reading only when four things hold. The cell is rested, the reading is corrected for temperature, the level is right and the acid is mixed.\n\nDischarge takes acid out of the liquid and locks it in the plates. So the liquid gets lighter and more like water as the charge falls.[^1] Its [[specific gravity]] measures that. It is the liquid's density divided by water's, the relative density you met with floating. Water reads 1.000. A hydrometer draws some acid into a glass barrel with a float inside. The float sinks until the acid it pushes aside weighs as much as it does. In denser acid it pushes aside less, so it rides higher. It is why a ship rides higher in seawater. The scale reads in points, and one point is 0.001. Say this stationary cell's maker marks full charge at 1.225. The level matters too. Water evaporates but the acid stays, so a low cell reads high. An overfilled cell reads low. When the level falls by evaporation, top up with water, not acid. Only water has gone.\n\nTemperature moves the float as well. Warm acid expands, so it is less dense and reads low. Cold acid reads high. Makers give a correction to 25 °C. A common one adds one point for every 1.67 °C above 25 °C.[^7] Below 25 °C, take points off the same way. Say the acid is at 35 °C and the float reads 1.219. That is 10 °C above, and 10 ÷ 1.67 is about 6 points. So the corrected reading is 1.219 + 0.006 = 1.225, full. Take the points off instead and you get 1.213. That calls a full cell twelve points short.\n\nThe voltmeter reads the acid's strength another way. Stronger acid makes a cell rest a little higher, so the rested voltage rises with the charge. A cell resting at 2.10 V at room temperature is about 90 per cent charged.[^4] Straight after a charge, though, it reads high. That is [[surface charge]]. One way to picture it: charging makes fresh acid right at the plates, and it takes hours to spread. So rest the cell open circuit for at least four hours. Makers recommend 24.[^3] A brief discharge also clears it. Acid can also settle into layers, strong at the bottom. That is [[stratification]]. The lower parts of the plates then stand in strong acid, and stronger acid rests higher. So the cell looks more charged than it is, and its rested voltage is pushed up.[^9] A layered cell can read well and still run out early. The picture is a simplified model with straight-line gauges. Real cells curve, and differ by type and maker. **Each gauge tracks the charge, but trust it only when the cell is rested, corrected, level and mixed.**"
