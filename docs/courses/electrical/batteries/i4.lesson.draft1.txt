    "text": "A charger puts a lead-acid battery back by holding a set voltage on every cell. It runs in three stages: bulk, topping and float. The setting per cell sits in a narrow band. A little too low, and sulphate builds on the negative plate. Too high, and the positive grid corrodes.\n\nCharging runs lesson 1's reaction backwards. Sulphate on both plates turns back into lead and lead dioxide, and acid returns to the liquid. In the bulk stage the charger holds its current, and the volts per cell climb as the cell fills. When they reach the charge setting, the charger holds the volts instead. That is the topping stage. The current now falls by itself, because the cell is saturating: with less sulphate left to turn back, the same volts push less current in. The cell is full when the current has fallen to 3 to 5 per cent of its Ah rating,[^10] so 3 to 5 A on a 100 Ah battery. Then the charger drops to the [[float charge]]. A large stationary battery floats at about 2.25 V a cell at 25 °C.[^4] That is above its rested 2.1 V by enough to push a small current, which makes up the cell's self-discharge. Holding the volts is what lets the current fall away. A fixed current would keep flowing into a full battery, and current a full cell cannot store turns into heat and gas.\n\nCells in series add their volts, and every cell takes the same current. So the setting is chosen for one cell and multiplied up: the charger's volts are the float volts per cell times the number of cells.[^11] One maker floats its vented cells at 2.23 V a cell at 20 °C. So 24 cells need 2.23 × 24 = 53.52 V. The band is narrow because both sides do harm. Held too low, the cells never quite get back to full, and sulphate left on the negative plate builds up. Held too high, the current a full cell cannot use corrodes the positive grid.[^3] Past 2.40 V a cell the cells also gas freely. So higher is not better. It trades one kind of damage for another.\n\nTwo settings sit above float on purpose, for a while. A [[boost charge]] at 2.33 to 2.40 V a cell recharges faster after a discharge, and it must go back to float once the battery is full.[^12] An [[equalising charge]] at 2.50 V a cell is a deliberate overcharge. It clears built-up sulphate and brings the cells level.[^6] Temperature moves the pin too. A warm cell needs a lower setting: one common rule takes 3 mV a cell off for each degree above 25 °C, and adds it back below.[^5] So a 2.25 V float becomes 2.22 V at 35 °C. One maker corrects only outside 10 to 30 °C, so follow the battery's own maker. These figures are for flooded cells, a simplified picture. Sealed cells tolerate overcharge less and have their own. **A charger holds a set voltage per cell, multiplied by the number of cells, and that setting lives in a narrow band: too low sulphates the negative plate, and too high corrodes the positive grid.**[^3]"
