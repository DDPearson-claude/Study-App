    "text": "A battery is not a fixed tank of ampere-hours. It gives fewer the faster you draw them, and fewer again in the cold. Peukert's rule, fitted through two of the maker's ratings, estimates how many fewer.\n\nPicture the battery as a fixed voltage behind a small resistance, both inside the case. The fixed voltage is the [[emf]], E. It is what the plates and acid push. A charged, rested cell reads about 2.1 V,[^14] so a battery of six cells has an emf of about 12.6 V. The small resistance is the [[internal resistance]], r. It is the plates, the acid and the joints between them. Say r is 7 mΩ, which is 0.007 Ω. Current through r drops volts there, just as current drops volts along a cable. So the terminals read E − Ir. At 100 A the lost volts are 100 × 0.007 = 0.70 V, and the terminals read 11.90 V. Read the emf there instead, a common slip, and you over-read by those 0.70 V. With nothing connected, no current flows and nothing is lost across r. A voltmeter draws almost nothing, so it reads the emf, 12.6 V. The battery does not set the current either. The load does. The current is E ÷ (R + r), where R is the load's resistance. Halve R and the current nearly doubles. It is not quite double, because r stays in the sum. The terminal voltage dips a little.\n\nMakers rate a lead-acid battery's ampere-hours at a slow, 20-hour discharge.[^3] Say a battery is rated 100 Ah. It gives 5 A for 20 hours. Drain the same battery in 2 hours and it may give only 56 Ah.[^4] That is a current of 28 A. Two things cause the shortfall. A discharge test ends when the terminal voltage falls to a set end voltage. More current means more lost volts, so it gets there sooner, with charge left in the plates. The acid must also soak into the plates to react. One way to picture it: at a high current the acid deep in the plates is used faster than fresh acid can reach it. Cold makes both worse. It raises the internal resistance and lowers the capacity. A battery giving 100 per cent at 27 °C typically gives only about 50 per cent at −18 °C.[^9]\n\nPeukert's rule puts a number on the rate effect. It says the current raised to a power n, times the run time, stays the same. That n is the battery's [[Peukert exponent]], and two ratings give it. Here 5ⁿ × 20 = 28ⁿ × 2, so 5.6ⁿ = 10, and n = log 10 ÷ log 5.6 = 1.34. A perfect battery, with the same capacity at any current, would have n = 1.00.[^5] Capacity is current times run time, so it goes as current to the power 1 − n. A power law is a straight line on log-log axes, and its power is the gradient. So the rule draws a straight line through both ratings, of gradient 1 − n, here −0.34. Now try 25 A. Dividing 100 Ah by 25 A says 4 hours. The rule says 20 × (5 ÷ 25)^1.34 = 2.3 hours, about 2 hours 20 minutes, so 58 Ah. A battery monitor's default n of 1.25 would say 67 Ah, so use the battery's own ratings where you have them. The rule is a simplified model, a fit and not a law. At very high currents a real battery gives even less than a fixed n predicts.[^8] **A battery gives fewer ampere-hours the faster or colder you draw them, and Peukert's fit through two ratings estimates how many fewer.**"

    "text": "Use this to work out how long a battery will carry a load.\n\n- Find the maker's rating: its ampere-hours and the hours behind them, say 100 Ah at 20 hours. Its current is 100 ÷ 20 = 5 A.\n- Find a second rating at a faster rate, say 56 Ah at 2 hours. Its current is 56 ÷ 2 = 28 A.\n- Work n: log(20 ÷ 2) ÷ log(28 ÷ 5) = 1.34.\n- Work the run time at your current I: t = 20 × (5 ÷ I)ⁿ hours. The ampere-hours are I × t.\n- Check that your current sits between the two ratings. Past the highest, expect less than the answer.\n- Take the capacity down for cold, using the maker's figures for the battery's temperature.\n- Under load, work the terminal voltage as E − Ir, not the emf.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** the label's ampere-hours are for the label's rate and temperature. Faster or colder, expect fewer. Makers often write the 20-hour rate as 0.05C: a current of 0.05 times the ampere-hours.\n\n**Worked example:** A 100 Ah battery, rated at 20 hours, also gives 56 Ah in 2 hours. So n = 1.34. At 25 A, t = 20 × (5 ÷ 25)^1.34 = 2.3 hours, about 2 hours 20 minutes. That is 25 × 2.3 = 58 Ah. Dividing 100 by 25 would have promised 4 hours. Sizing a battery to a whole load profile is a separate method, but it rests on this arithmetic.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Dividing the label's ampere-hours by a fast current.\n- Reading the emf as the voltage under load.\n- Using a monitor's default n when the battery's own ratings give another.\n- Trusting the fit far past the highest rating.\n- Expecting the warm figure from a battery in a cold space."
