  "text": "An earth fault opens the breaker in time only if enough current flows. That current runs round a loop. It goes out on the line conductor, back on the cpc, and home through the supply. The loop's impedance is Zs = Ze + (R1+R2). Every metre of cable adds to R1+R2. So a longer run means a bigger Zs and a smaller fault current.\n\nWork R1+R2 as milliohms per metre × multiplier × length ÷ 1000. The milliohm figures in Table I1 are for copper at 20 °C. A fault happens on a circuit that has been working, and warm copper has more resistance. The multiplier brings the figure up to working temperature. The fault current is then Uo × Cmin ÷ Zs. The supply can sag below 230 V, as low as 218.5 V. Cmin, 0.95, allows for that.[^7] You design on the lowest voltage because it gives the least current. If the breaker opens on that, it opens on anything higher.\n\nA breaker opens within the time only once the current reaches a set figure, its Ia. For a 6 A type B, Ia is 30 A. Its maximum Zs comes straight from that: 230 × 0.95 ÷ 30 = 7.28 Ω. So checking Zs against the maximum is the same as checking the fault current against Ia. On the showroom motor's 2.5 mm² run, Zs reaches its 0.87 Ω limit near 37.9 m. That takes Zs at DBP2 as a practice 0.15 Ω.
