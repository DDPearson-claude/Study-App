  "text": "Voltage drop is the voltage the cables use up on the way to the load: the load gets what is left. Your slides count it as mV/A/m × Ib × L ÷ 1000, where mV/A/m is the millivolts dropped for every ampere of design current for every metre of conductor,[^4][^5] and they allow up to 3% of the supply for lighting and 5% for other circuits,[^6][^7] taken of the supply voltage you were given where one is given.[^2]\n\nThe limit runs from the origin of the installation to the most distant point of the circuit,[^8] because the load gets what the origin's voltage leaves after every cable it is fed through. So the submain's drop is added to the final circuit's,[^15] and a final circuit that passes on its own can still fail: on your slides' 395 V supply, 10 V in the submain and 9.8 V in the circuit make 19.8 V, over the 19.75 V allowed.[^1] A three-phase submain's drop is a line-to-line figure. Where it feeds a single-phase circuit, which runs line to neutral, a √3 smaller, the formula sheet divides it by √3 before adding;[^13] where the load is three-phase too, the drops stay three-phase and are judged against 3% or 5% of the three-phase voltage.[^16]\n\nTwo refinements can bring a narrow fail inside. The tables' mV/A/m are worked for the conductor at its maximum operating temperature, which it reaches only at full load.[^23] Current heats a conductor and a hotter conductor has more resistance,[^24] so a cable carrying well under its rating runs cooler and drops less,[^25] and a factor, [[Ct]], corrects the drop for it. Your slides' heater cable, chased up to 6.0 mm², still carries just 13.04 A of the 41 A it is rated for,[^21][^22] so it drops less than its 8.09 V, and with Ct the smaller 4.0 mm² may pass.[^57][^33] From 25 mm² up the tables give r, x and z.[^37] With a known power factor, the r drop is in step with the current and the x drop a quarter of a cycle ahead of it, so only their parts along the load's voltage take away from it: (mV/A/m)r × cos φ + (mV/A/m)x × sin φ.[^43] Using z alone may give a drop higher than the actual one.[^44] This is a simplified picture: taking the drops' shadows along the voltage is very close to the exact figure while the drop is small beside the supply voltage.\n\n**Add every drop from the intake to the far end and judge the total against 3% or 5% of the voltage you were given; where it fails narrowly, a lightly loaded cable's Ct, or with a known power factor its r and x, may show that it passes.**[^3][^2][^33][^40]"
