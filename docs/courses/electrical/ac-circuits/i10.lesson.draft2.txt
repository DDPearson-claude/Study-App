    "text": "In its simplest form, a three-phase generator is three of lesson 1's coils on one shaft, set a third of a turn apart,[^1] so they make three equal voltage waves 120° apart. How you join the three windings, and three equal loads, decides which reading picks up a factor of √3. Join one end of each at a common point and the voltage between two lines is √3 times the voltage across one winding. Join them nose to tail in a ring and the current in each line is √3 times the current in one winding. Either way, the power is √3 × line voltage × line current × power factor.[^4]\n\nStart with the [[star]] connection: one end of every winding meets at the star point, and a line conductor runs from each free end, L1, L2 and L3. Now it matters where you measure. Across one winding, line to star point, a voltmeter reads the phase voltage, 230 V on UK mains.[^22] Between two lines it reads the [[line voltage]]. Going from L2 to L1 you pass back down L2's winding and up L1's, so the line voltage is L1's arrow minus L2's, found by adding L2's arrow turned half round,[^2] as in Trigonometry's Adding waves with phasors. Two equal arrows 120° apart differ by an arrow √3 times as long, so the line voltage is 230 × 1.732 = 398.4 V, which the trade rounds to the 400 V nominal of a UK three-phase board. Each line runs straight into one winding with nothing branching off, so the line current is the phase current.[^3] Say each load is 23 Ω at a power factor of 0.8: each phase carries 230 ÷ 23 = 10 A. The three currents meet at the star point and return along the neutral. Laid tip to tail, three equal arrows 120° apart close a triangle, so a balanced load leaves the neutral carrying nothing,[^5] and a load that is always balanced can be fed by the three lines alone. Drop L3 to 5 A and the equal part, 5 A in each phase, still cancels; what is left is L1's and L2's extra 5 A, two equal arrows 120° apart whose sum is one 5 A arrow. So the neutral carries 5 A, not 25 A. That cancelling is for sine-wave currents: currents with harmonics at three times the supply frequency add in the neutral instead.[^6]\n\nIn [[delta]], each load is joined between two lines, in a ring. So each has the line voltage across it: the phase voltage is the line voltage, 398.4 V, and each 23 Ω load draws 398.4 ÷ 23 = 17.32 A. At the corner where L1 joins, L1 brings the current drawn by the load that starts there, less the current the other load delivers back into that corner. So L1 carries the difference of two phase currents 120° apart. It is the same subtraction, now on currents: 17.32 × 1.732 = 30.0 A. The √3 has moved from the voltage to the current, and with no star point there is no neutral. A motor's three windings are joined in these same two ways, which the Machines and drives course takes up.\n\nEach load takes V_ph × I_ph × cos φ, lesson 8's true power,[^18] where φ is the angle inside that load between its own voltage and current. Three loads take 3 V_ph I_ph cos φ. In star, V_ph = V_L ÷ √3 and I_ph = I_L; in delta, V_ph = V_L and I_ph = I_L ÷ √3. Either way one √3 divides into the 3 and leaves √3 V_L I_L cos φ,[^4] so for a balanced load line readings give the power without your knowing the connection. Star: 3 × 230 × 10 × 0.8 = 5520 W. The same loads in delta take 3 × 398.4 × 17.32 × 0.8 = 16,560 W, three times as much, because each has √3 times the voltage and so √3 times the current. This is a simplified model, with balanced loads, pure sine waves and a supply that holds its voltage. For the course's puzzle, each phase has its own power triangle, so three-phase cables carry √3 V_L I_L volt-amps, of which only the cos φ share arrives as watts. **In star the line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage and the line current equals the phase current;[^3] in delta the √3 moves to the current; either way three equal loads take √3 V_L I_L cos φ.[^4]**"
