Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- A three-phase generator has three coils of wire placed at 120 degrees to each other and rotated in a magnetic field. [R1]
- The line voltage is found by taking the phasor sum of the voltages in two phases, which gives the factor root 3. [R2]
- In balanced star circuits line voltage is root 3 times phase voltage and line current equals phase current; in balanced delta the roles reverse. [R3]
- Three-phase power equals the product of line-to-line voltage, line current, root 3 and the power factor. [R4]
- The neutral conductor carries no current in a balanced three-phase network. [R5]
- Triplen harmonic currents add within the neutral of a 4-wire Y-Y system, so 'balanced means no neutral current' holds for sine-wave loads. [R6]
- A single coil turned at constant speed in a uniform magnetic field produces an emf that varies sinusoidally in time. [R7]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The induced emf depends on how quickly the flux through the coil is changing, so a faster-turning coil gives a larger voltage. [R8][R9]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For i1: a real a.c. generator holds its terminal voltage by adjusting its d.c. field current (screen 12's Why? and the explain). [R10]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The phasors for a resistor and a reactance are 90 degrees apart, so the series impedance comes from Pythagoras' law. [R11]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Voltages 90 degrees apart do not add arithmetically: a 6 V source and an 8 V source at 90 degrees give 10 V. [R12]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Inductive reactance is X_L = 2πfL, so a measured reactance at a known frequency gives the inductance. [R13]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Capacitors and inductors absorb energy during one half-cycle and return it in the other, so the power wave goes below zero. [R14]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- True power is measured in watts, reactive power in volt-amps reactive (VAR), and their combination is apparent power in volt-amps. [R15][R16]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Power factor is true power divided by apparent power, which equals the cosine of the phase angle; average power is V I cos φ with rms values. [R17][R18]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Any power factor less than 1 means the wiring must carry more current to deliver the same true power. [R19]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For i8: a generator is rated in kVA at a stated power factor, with a kW figure as well, so it is checked against both: 100 kVA supplies 80 kW at 0.8, and a 60 Hz diesel set's data sheet reads 630 kVA and 504 kW at 0.8. [R10][R20][R21]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- UK domestic supply is nominally 230 V, 50 Hz, kept within 216 V to 253 V by the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations. [R22]  (whole topic)
- Ships mostly run 60 Hz, because higher frequency lets motors and generators run faster and so be smaller for a given power. [R23]  (whole topic)

  "text": "In its simplest form, a three-phase generator is three of lesson 1's coils on one shaft, set a third of a turn apart,[^1] so they make three equal voltage waves 120° apart. How you join the three windings, and three equal loads, decides which reading picks up a factor of √3. Join one end of each at a common point and the voltage between two lines is √3 times the voltage across one winding. Join them nose to tail in a ring and the current in each line is √3 times the current in one winding. Either way, the power is √3 × line voltage × line current × power factor.[^3]\n\nStart with the [[star]] connection: one end of every winding meets at the star point, and a line conductor runs from each free end, L1, L2 and L3. Now it matters where you measure. Across one winding, line to star point, a voltmeter reads the phase voltage, 230 V on UK mains.[^2] Between two lines it reads the [[line voltage]].
