At the top, a 6 Ω resistor and an ideal coil of about 25.5 mH sit in series on an AC supply of 20 V at 50 Hz, example values picked so the coil's reactance is 8 Ω and the numbers come out whole. An ammeter in the loop reads the current, 2 A, and three voltmeters read across the resistor, across the coil and across the supply; like ordinary AC meters on a sine wave, they read rms. Under the circuit, each part's voltage is drawn as an arrow whose length is its meter reading. One current flows through every part of a series loop, so the current is the reference and its arrow lies flat. The resistor's arrow, V_R, lies flat with it, because a resistor's voltage is in step with its current (lesson 3). The coil's arrow, V_L, starts at V_R's tip and points straight up, because an ideal coil's voltage peaks a quarter cycle before its current (lesson 4). The arrow from V_R's tail to V_L's tip closes a right-angled triangle; by Pythagoras its length is √(V_R² + V_L²), and it lands on the supply meter's reading. The angle between it and the flat side is the phase angle φ, by which the supply's voltage leads the current, with tan φ = V_L ÷ V_R. Beside the triangle, a bar shows V_R + V_L added as plain numbers, struck through, next to the supply's own bar. Readouts give the three meter readings. Screen 1 opens on the meters: 12 V across the resistor, 16 V across the coil and 20 V across the supply, with the 28 V plain-sum bar struck through. Screen 2 asks why 12 V and 16 V make 20 V, not 28 V: the two voltages peak a quarter cycle apart, so they are never both at full size together; the coil wastes 8 V as heat (an ideal coil takes no watts, lesson 4); or a smaller current flows in the coil (one series loop carries one current). A right Check plays both voltage waves on one time axis with their instant-by-instant sum, the supply's wave: the parts peak at 17.0 V and 22.6 V, but at different instants, and the sum peaks at 28.3 V, which is 20 V rms.
