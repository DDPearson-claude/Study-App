  "text": "A coil fights any change in the current through it. A changing current changes the flux through the coil's own turns, and a changing flux induces an emf that pushes back, so the coil's push follows how fast its current is changing, not how big the current is. On AC that puts the current of an ideal coil a quarter cycle behind the voltage, and it makes the coil's opposition rise in step with the frequency.\n\nRemember the turning coil: an emf appears whenever the flux through a coil changes, and the faster it changes, the bigger the emf.[^3] Here the flux comes from the coil's own current, since current through the turns makes a field through them, as in any electromagnet: more current, more flux. So when the current changes, the flux through the coil changes, and the coil induces an emf in itself. The induced emf is the negative of the change in flux per unit time;[^4] that minus sign means it pushes against the change that made it. This self-made push is the [[back-emf]]. How much back-emf a coil makes for each amp per second of change is its [[inductance]], L, in henries: textbooks add that the coil's voltage is L times the rate of change of its current. A steady current makes a steady flux and meets no back-emf at all, only the wire's resistance. The coil pushes back against change, not against current.\n\nOn the back-emf coil, a tangent line rides the current wave, and the coil's voltage is L times its steepness. In an ideal coil, one with no resistance, there is nothing else in the coil for the supply's voltage to appear across, so the back-emf matches the supply at every instant. The current therefore changes fastest exactly when the supply pushes hardest. A sine current is steepest as it crosses zero and flat for an instant at its top. So the voltage is at its peak as the current crosses zero, and zero as the current tops out: the voltage peaks a quarter cycle, 5 ms at 50 Hz, before the current. The voltage leads and the current lags.[^5] This is the cause behind the +jX_L of Complex numbers lesson 7; this course measures the phase angle as the angle by which the voltage leads the current, so a coil's is +90°. Multiply the two waves instant by instant, as on lesson 3's scrubber, and the power dot spends half its time below the line. While the current grows, the coil takes energy into its field; while it dies away, the coil hands it back. For an ideal coil the humps below match those above, so it averages 0 W, where the heater element with the same 100 V and 10 A peaks averaged 500 W. A resistor never returns power to the source, as reactive loads like this coil do.[^6]\n\nNow raise the frequency and keep the supply's 100 V peak. The back-emf must still match the supply, so the current's steepest slope is fixed by the supply's peak. To climb to the same peak in half the time, the current would have to change twice as fast, making twice the back-emf, more than the supply gives. So at twice the frequency the current reaches that same steepest slope with half the peak. The coil's opposition to AC, the voltage ÷ the current, is its [[inductive reactance]], X_L, in ohms. You met X_L = 2πfL in Complex numbers lesson 7;[^7] here is where it comes from, and why it is directly proportional to the frequency.[^8] The 2π comes from Trigonometry's turning arm: as the wave crosses zero, the arm's tip is moving straight up, so the wave rises at the tip's speed, and the tip travels 2π radii in each turn and makes f turns a second. So a sine's steepest slope is 2π × f × its peak, and the back-emf's peak is L times that. The example coil of about 31.8 mH has 10 Ω at 50 Hz, 12 Ω at the 60 Hz most common on board ship[^1] and 20 Ω at 100 Hz, so on the 100 V peak its current peaks at 10 A, 8.33 A and 5 A. At 0 Hz, steady DC, X_L is 0 Ω and only the wire's resistance is left.
