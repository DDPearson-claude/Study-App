    "text": "The 230 V on a heater's label is not the top of the mains wave. It is the steady DC voltage that would heat that heater just as much, and for a sine wave that steady level is about 0.707 of the peak, so 230 V mains climbs to about 325 V at the top of each cycle.\n\nTake two identical heater elements of 10 Ω, one on AC with a 100 V peak and one on a DC level you can set. Heat in a resistor goes with the voltage squared, P = V² ÷ R, and that holds at each instant on AC too. At the top of the wave the AC element takes 100² ÷ 10 = 1000 W, as the wave crosses zero it takes nothing, and on the backward half it heats just the same, because a minus squared is a plus. So its power is a row of humps between 0 and 1000 W, and remember Calculus fundamentals' fold: a squared sine's humps fit exactly into its dips, so they average half their top, 500 W. The DC element takes its level squared ÷ 10, steadily, which is 500 W when the level squared is 5000, at 70.7 V. That steady DC, the one giving the same heat in an equal resistance, is how the size of an AC voltage or current is defined.[^1] It is the [[rms value]], root mean square, after Calculus fundamentals' recipe: square the wave, take the mean, take the root. For a sine it is about 0.707 of the peak.[^2] That is 1 ÷ √2, as the 70.7 V from a 100 V peak shows.\n\nThe plain average will not do. Over a whole cycle the voltage's average is zero, and even over half a cycle it is only 63.7 V, and 63.7 V of DC heats the element by about 406 W, not 500 W. The high parts of the wave count for more than the low parts, because heat goes with the square. That also makes the answer depend on the wave's shape. A square wave sits at its full height, one way or the other, almost the whole time, so its rms value is its peak, a ratio of 1.0. A triangle spends more of its time low down than a sine, and the mean of a ramp's square is a third of its top's square, so its rms value is the peak ÷ √3, 0.577 of it. So 0.707 belongs to the sine, and only to it of these three.\n\nUK mains is 230 V nominal, kept between 216 V and 253 V.[^4] The 230 V is an rms figure, so the wave peaks at about 230 × √2 = 325 V,[^3] and everything across the supply meets 325 V at the top of each cycle and −325 V at the bottom, a swing of 650 V. A [[true-rms meter]] works out the heating value whatever the shape.
