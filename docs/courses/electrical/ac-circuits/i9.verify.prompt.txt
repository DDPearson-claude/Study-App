Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- A poor power factor can be corrected by adding a load drawing equal and opposite reactive power, which means a capacitor for an inductive load. [R1]
- Correction does not change the true power consumed, but cuts apparent power and the total current drawn from the source. [R2]
- Too much capacitance gives a low power factor just as too much inductance does, so a correction must not over-correct. [R3]
- A capacitor opposes changes in voltage by drawing or supplying current as it charges or discharges to the new voltage level. [R4]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- In a capacitor the current leads the voltage, so the voltage lags behind the current. [R5]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Capacitive reactance is X_C = 1/(2πfC) and decreases as frequency rises, the opposite of inductive reactance. [R6]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For i5: the memory aid ELI the ICE man: the emf (E) leads the current (I) in an inductor (L), and the current leads the emf in a capacitor (C) (screen 9's rows and the practice). [R7]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Capacitors and inductors absorb energy during one half-cycle and return it in the other, so the power wave goes below zero. [R8]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- True power is measured in watts, reactive power in volt-amps reactive (VAR), and their combination is apparent power in volt-amps. [R9][R10]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Power factor is true power divided by apparent power, which equals the cosine of the phase angle; average power is V I cos φ with rms values. [R11][R12]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Any power factor less than 1 means the wiring must carry more current to deliver the same true power. [R13]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For i8: a generator is rated in kVA at a stated power factor, with a kW figure as well, so it is checked against both: 100 kVA supplies 80 kW at 0.8, and a 60 Hz diesel set's data sheet reads 630 kVA and 504 kW at 0.8. [R14][R15][R16]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- UK domestic supply is nominally 230 V, 50 Hz, kept within 216 V to 253 V by the Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations. [R17]  (whole topic)
- Ships mostly run 60 Hz, because higher frequency lets motors and generators run faster and so be smaller for a given power. [R18]  (whole topic)
