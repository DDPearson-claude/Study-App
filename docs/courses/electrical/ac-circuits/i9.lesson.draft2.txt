    "text": "A motor's current has two parts: an in-step part that does the work, and a quarter-cycle part that carries vars out to its windings and back every cycle. Fit a capacitor across the motor and it draws a current a quarter cycle the other way, so it hands the motor those vars from close by. The supply then sends only what is left, so its current falls while the true power stays put. This is [[power factor correction]]: adding a load that draws an equal and opposite amount of reactive power.[^1]\n\nStart from lessons 4 and 5. The motor's quarter-cycle part lags the voltage by a quarter cycle, as lesson 4's coil did. A capacitor across the same voltage draws a current that leads it by a quarter cycle,[^5] as in lesson 5. So the two currents are half a cycle apart: whenever the motor's quarter-cycle current flows in, the capacitor's flows out, and the other way round. That is why, on the power triangle, the motor's vars, Q_L, point up and the capacitor's, Q_C, point down, and the supply sees only Q_L − Q_C. The motor barely notices: the capacitor sits beside it, not in its path, so it still has the supply's 230 V across it, still draws 10 A and still takes 1600 W. The correction does not change the true power.[^2] Take lesson 8's motor: 1600 W, 1652 var and 2300 VA. A capacitor of 1652 var cancels the upright completely, so the supply's apparent power drops to 1600 VA and its current to 1600 ÷ 230 = 6.96 A, from 10 A.[^2] The capacitor draws 7.18 A and the motor still draws 10 A, yet the supply carries 6.96 A, not 2.82 A or 17.18 A. Currents out of step add as arrows: the capacitor's 7.18 A cancels exactly the motor's quarter-cycle part, 10 × sin 45.9° = 7.18 A, and leaves its in-step part, 10 × cos 45.9° = 6.96 A. That 7.18 A now runs round the short loop between the capacitor and the motor instead of along the supply cable. Any power factor below 1 makes the wiring carry more current than it would with no reactance,[^13] and here the cable's heat, which goes with the current squared, falls to (6.96 ÷ 10)² = 0.48 of what it was.\n\nTo size the capacitor, read it off the triangle in three steps. First, the vars it must supply: Q_C = Q before − Q after. Second, its reactance: it has the full supply voltage across it, so its current is V ÷ X_C and its vars are V × I_C = V² ÷ X_C, which turns round to X_C = V² ÷ Q_C. Third, its capacitance, from lesson 5's X_C = 1/(2πfC)[^6] turned round: C = 1 ÷ (2πf X_C). For a power factor of 1 on this motor, X_C = 230² ÷ 1652 = 32.0 Ω, and at 50 Hz, C = 1 ÷ (2π × 50 × 32.0), about 99 µF. You need not go all the way. For an example target of 0.95, the angle whose cosine is 0.95 is 18.2°, so the vars left are Q after = P × tan 18.2° = 1600 × 0.329 = 526 var, and Q_C = 1652 − 526 = 1126 var. That needs 47.0 Ω, about 68 µF, and leaves 1600 ÷ (230 × 0.95) = 7.32 A in the supply.\n\nToo much is as bad as too little. Past 1652 var the capacitor's current outgrows the motor's quarter-cycle part, so the difference, a quarter cycle ahead of the voltage, has to come along the supply cable. The triangle swings below the floor: the power factor is now leading. Twice the capacitor, 3304 var, leaves 1652 var leading, so the supply is back to 2300 VA and 10 A, the same poor 0.70 the other way. Too much capacitance gives a low power factor just as too much inductance does,[^3] and adding more than the load's own vars is [[over-correction]]. This is a simplified model, with pure sine waves, an ideal capacitor and a steady 230 V; a real motor's vars also change with how hard it is working. **A capacitor across an inductive load supplies the load's vars from close by, so, sized to Q before − Q after, it cuts the supply's current while the true power stays the same; too big a capacitor makes the power factor poor again, leading.[^2][^3]**"
