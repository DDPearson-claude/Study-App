    "text": "Turn a coil of wire steadily between two magnet poles and the field passing through it grows, shrinks to nothing and builds the other way, over and over. The coil makes a voltage only while that field is changing, and the faster it changes the bigger the voltage, so the voltage is biggest when the coil is edge-on to the field, with none passing through it, and nothing when the coil faces the field.\n\nPicture the field as straight lines running across from the north pole to the south. How many of them pass through the coil depends on which way it faces: face-on it catches the most, edge-on they all slip past, and turned on past edge-on they pass through from the other side. That amount of field through the coil is its [[flux]]. A changing flux through a coil makes a push round it, and the push is set by how fast the flux changes, not by how much there is.[^2] A push made this way is called an [[emf]], measured in volts like any voltage. Face-on, the flux is at its most, and for that instant it is neither growing nor shrinking, like a ball at the top of its throw, so the emf is zero. Edge-on, the flux is zero but changing fastest, so the emf is at its peak. A faster turn changes the flux faster, so it gives a bigger emf.[^3]\n\nThe emf traces a sine wave, and From circle to wave shows why. Measure the coil's angle θ from face-on. The flux follows the across shadow of an arm at that angle, cos θ, the share of the coil square to the field. The arm's tip moves at a steady speed at right angles to the arm, so the across part of its speed is that speed × sin θ: nothing when the arm lies along the floor, all of it when the arm points straight up. So the flux changes at a rate that follows sin θ, and the emf is its peak × sin θ: a coil turned steadily in an even field makes an emf that varies as a sine in time.[^1] Past half a turn the flux builds through the coil from the other side, so the emf pushes the other way round the coil, and the current it drives through a lamp reverses too. That is what alternates: the direction of the push and of the current. The charge in the wires moves one way and then back, making no journey round the circuit, and current driven like this is [[alternating current]]. One turn draws one whole wave, so the turns a second are the frequency: 50 for UK mains, and 60 for most ships' supplies, the most common frequency on board ship because it lets motors and generators run faster and so be smaller for a given power.[^5] Textbooks add that the peak is N × B × A × ω, the turns, the field strength, the coil's area and the angular speed. More turns each add their own push, and a stronger field or a bigger coil gives more flux to change. Of the four, only the speed changes the period too, so in a simple generator turning faster raises the voltage and the frequency together.\n\nThe course's motor puzzle runs on this wave: the supply's voltage and the motor's current both reverse every half cycle, and the rest of the course is about what happens when they do not reverse together. One coil in an even field is a simplified model; real generators have many coils, and their waves are close to a sine rather than exactly one. **A coil turned steadily in a magnetic field makes a sine-wave emf,[^1] because its emf follows how fast the flux through it is changing,[^2] so the emf peaks edge-on, where the flux is zero, and reverses every half turn.**"

Turns, field strength and coil area scale the peak alone.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** The emf follows the change in the flux, not the amount.\n\n**Worked example:** A test coil gives 12 V peak at 25 turns a second. At 50 turns a second the flux changes twice as fast, so the peak is 24 V and the frequency 50 Hz, a period of 1 ÷ 50 = 20 ms.
