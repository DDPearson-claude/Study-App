    "text": "Put a resistor and a coil in series on AC and meter each one, and the two readings add up to more than the supply meter shows: 12 V across the resistor, 16 V across the coil, yet 20 V across the supply. Nothing is lost. The two voltages peak a quarter cycle apart, so they add as arrows at right angles, and the supply's voltage is the long side of the right-angled triangle they make. Divide every side by the current and the same triangle gives the circuit's resistance, its reactance and its whole opposition to AC, which is how an ohmmeter, a voltmeter and an ammeter find an unknown coil's inductance.\n\nIn a series loop one current flows through every part, so the current is what the parts share. The resistor's voltage is in step with that current (lesson 3). The ideal coil's voltage peaks a quarter cycle earlier, because its back-emf follows how fast the current is changing (lesson 4). At every instant the supply's voltage is the resistor's plus the coil's at that instant, just as on DC. But when the resistor's voltage is at its peak the coil's is passing through zero, and the other way round, so the two are never both at full size together, and their sum peaks lower than their two peaks added. Trigonometry lesson 7 showed how to add two such waves: draw each as an arrow, lay them tip to tail, and the closing arrow is the sum. With the current's arrow lying flat, the resistor's arrow lies flat too and the coil's points straight up, so the closing arrow is the long side of a right-angled triangle, and Pythagoras gives its length. A meter reads rms, which is the peak ÷ √2 for every one of these waves, so the readings make the same triangle: √(12² + 16²) = 20 V. A textbook adds a 6 V source to an 8 V source a quarter cycle apart the same way, and gets 10 V at a phase angle of 53.13°.[^2] Here too the closing arrow sits 53.13° above the flat side: the supply's voltage leads the current by that angle, φ.\n\nEach side is the current times something. The resistor's voltage is I × R, the coil's is I × X_L, and the supply's is I × the circuit's whole opposition to AC, its [[impedance]], Z, in ohms. Divide every side by the same 2 A and the triangle shrinks without changing shape: 6 Ω of resistance lying flat, 8 Ω of inductive reactance standing up, and Z = √(R² + X_L²) = 10 Ω along the long side,[^1] still at 53.13°. This is the [[impedance triangle]], the one Complex numbers lesson 7 wrote as 6 + j8 = 10∠53.13°, now read off meters. It turns an unknown coil into numbers. A real coil's wire resistance and inductance sit in one body, so no meter can be put across one without the other. But an ohmmeter's steady current makes no back-emf, so it reads the wire's resistance alone (lesson 4); a voltmeter and an ammeter on AC give Z = V ÷ I; Pythagoras gives X_L = √(Z² − R²); and X_L = 2πfL[^3] gives L. Only the reactance side depends on the frequency, so a higher frequency stands the triangle taller and a lower one lays it flatter, until on steady DC only R is left.\n\nSwap the coil for a capacitor of the same reactance and the meters read exactly the same, but its voltage peaks a quarter cycle after the current (lesson 5), so its arrow points straight down and the triangle flips below the line: φ is −53.13°, and the supply's voltage lags the current. Square, add, square root.\n\n**Worked example:** An example coil reads 30 Ω on an ohmmeter and draws 4.6 A from 230 V, 50 Hz mains.[^14] Z = 230 ÷ 4.6 = 50 Ω. X_L = √(50² − 30²) = 40 Ω.
