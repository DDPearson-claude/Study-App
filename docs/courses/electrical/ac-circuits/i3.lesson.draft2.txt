    "text": "Feed a resistor AC and its power is not steady. Multiply the voltage by the current at each instant and you get a power wave that pulses twice for every cycle of the supply and never drops below zero, and for a resistance its average is the rms voltage times the rms current, just as volts times amps on DC.\n\nIn a resistor the current is the voltage ÷ the resistance at every instant, so the two waves rise and fall together: they reach their tops and bottoms at the same times.[^1] Waves that keep step like this are [[in phase]]. The power at any one instant is that instant's voltage times that instant's current, the P = V × I you use on DC, worked moment by moment: the [[instantaneous power]]. Take the heater test's 10 Ω element on a 100 V peak, so the current peaks at 10 A. At the top of the wave it takes 100 × 10 = 1000 W. As the wave crosses zero both are zero, so it takes nothing. On the backward half both are negative, −100 V and −10 A at the bottom, and a minus times a minus is a plus, so it takes 1000 W again: the element heats whichever way the current flows. So there is one hump of power on the forward half and another on the backward half. That is the heater test's row of humps again, now built from two waves: the current is the voltage ÷ 10 Ω at each instant, so voltage × current is voltage² ÷ 10. Multiplying the two waves is the form that lasts, because in later lessons the current will not keep step with the voltage. For a resistor the power wave never goes below zero,[^4] touching zero only as the waves cross it, and its frequency is double that of the voltage and current.[^3] On 50 Hz mains that is 100 humps a second.\n\nThe humps run from 0 to 1000 W. The power is 1000 W × the sine squared, and remember Calculus fundamentals' fold: a squared sine's humps fit exactly into its dips, so they average half their top, 500 W. That is the [[average power]], the heat the element actually gives. The rms values give it straight away. For a sine each is about 0.707 of its peak,[^10] so 70.7 V and 7.07 A, and 70.7 × 7.07 is about 500 W, because 0.707 is 1 ÷ √2 and 1 ÷ √2 squared is a half. For a resistance, the product of the rms voltage and the rms current equals the average power.[^2] The peaks multiplied give the top of each hump, twice the average, not the heat. Averaging first fails too: over a whole cycle the voltage and the current each average zero, yet the element still warms, because it is the product that has to be averaged, and here the product never goes below zero.\n\nUK mains is 230 V nominal,[^12] peaking near 325 V.[^11] A 1 kW heater on it is 230² ÷ 1000 = 52.9 Ω and draws 230 ÷ 52.9 = 4.35 A, so its power averages 230 × 4.35 = 1000 W, in humps that reach twice that, 2000 W, 100 times a second. On the 60 Hz most common on board ship,[^17] the humps come 120 times a second. This is a simplified model, an ideal resistor on a pure sine; a real element's resistance changes as it warms, and real mains is close to a sine rather than exactly one. **In a resistor the voltage and current keep step, so the power wave never goes below zero,[^4] beats at twice the supply's frequency[^3] and averages the rms voltage times the rms current.[^2]**"

Appliances and devices are commonly quoted in rms values, rather than peak values.[^15]\n- For a heater, an element or any plain resistance, multiply them: average power = V_rms × I_rms.[^2]\n- The power humps top out at twice that, for a sine, and come at twice the supply's frequency.[^3]\n- For a motor, or anything with a coil or a capacitor, V × I is not yet the watts; lessons 4, 5 and 8 show why.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** On a resistance, watts = rms volts × rms amps, and the hump tops are twice that, 100 a second on 50 Hz.\n\n**Worked example:** A clamp meter reads 10 A on a heater at 230 V. Its power is 230 × 10 = 2300 W, and 230² ÷ 2300 = 23 Ω checks it as a resistance. Its voltage peaks at 325 V and its current at 14.1 A, so the humps top out at twice 2300 W, 4600 W, for an instant each, about 325 × 14.1.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Multiplying the peaks, about 325 × 14.1 = 4600 W. That is the top of each hump, twice the heat.\n- Averaging first. In a resistor both are negative together, so the power stays positive.[^4]\n- Expecting the power to pulse at the supply's frequency. It pulses at twice it.[^3]\n- Using V × I as the watts of a motor. It works on a resistance; a coil changes it."
