  "text": "A reading on a screen comes at the end of a chain. A sensor feels the pressure in the pipe. A transducer turns what it feels into an electrical quantity. A transmitter sends that on as a current, often set to run from 4 to 20 mA.[^2] At the far end a display turns the current back into bar. The transmitter here is set for 0 to 10 bar, and its output follows a straight line. Its range is 0 to 10 bar, the lowest and highest values it is set to read. Its [[span]] is the difference between the two ends,[^1] 10 bar on the way in and 16 mA on the way out. So each bar adds 16 ÷ 10 = 1.6 mA to the 4 mA at the bottom. 5 bar sends 4 + 8 = 12 mA. A range of −1 to 9 bar has the same 10 bar span, because 9 − (−1) = 10.\n\nChecking that line against a standard, and setting it right, is [[calibration]]. Known pressures go in at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% of the range. Each output is compared with the ideal line, and its error is worked out in per cent of span, as the error in mA ÷ 16 mA × 100. Two screws set the line. The zero screw slides the whole line up or down, and the span screw changes its slope.[^3] Here the span screw tilts the line about its bottom end. So a zero error is the same size at every point. A span error is nothing at 0 bar and grows in step with the pressure, to its largest at 10 bar. Say a transmitter reads 4.32 mA at 0 bar and 20.32 mA at 10 bar. Both ends are 0.32 mA high, which is 0.32 ÷ 16 × 100 = 2% of span. The same error at both ends is a zero error, and the zero screw slides it out. Another reads 4.00 mA at 0 bar but 19.52 mA at 10 bar. It is right at the bottom and 0.48 mA low at the top, which is −3% of span. That error grows up the range, so it is a span error.
