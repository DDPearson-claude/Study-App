   "now": "A reading on a screen comes at the end of a chain, where each link turns one quantity into the next. To trust the number, you check a transmitter's straight line against a standard. One adjustment slides the line and the other tilts it. A sensor feels the pressure in the pipe. A transducer turns what it feels into an electrical quantity. A transmitter sends that on as a current. This one is calibrated so its range gives 4 to 20 mA.[^3] At the far end a display turns the current back into bar. The transmitter here is set for 0 to 10 bar, and its output follows a straight line. Its range is 0 to 10 bar, the lowest and highest values it is set to read. Its [[span]] is the difference between the two ends,[^1] 10 bar on the way in and 16 mA on the way out. So each bar adds 16 ÷ 10 = 1.6 mA to the 4 mA at the bottom. 5 bar sends 4 + 8 = 12 mA. A range of −1 to 9 bar has the same 10 bar span, because 9 − (−1) = 10.\n\nChecking that line against a standard, and setting it right, is [[calibration]]. Known pressures go in, often at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% of the range. Each output is compared with the ideal line, and its error is worked out in per cent of span, as the error in mA ÷ 16 mA × 100. Two adjustments, often screws, set the line. The zero screw slides the whole line up or down, and the span screw changes its slope.[^2] A slide moves every point by the same amount, so a zero error is the same size at every point. Here the span screw tilts the line about its bottom end. That leaves 0 bar where it is and moves each point in proportion to its pressure. So a span error is nothing at 0 bar and grows in step with the pressure, to its largest at 10 bar. Say a transmitter reads 4.32 mA at 0 bar and 20.32 mA at 10 bar. Both ends are 0.32 mA high, which is 0.32 ÷ 16 × 100 = 2% of span. The same error at both ends is a zero error, and the zero screw slides it out. Another reads 4.00 mA at 0 bar but 19.52 mA at 10 bar. It is right at the bottom and 0.48 mA low at the top, which is −3% of span. That error grows up the range, so it is a span error. Zero and span errors are corrected by a calibration.[^4] Set the zero at the bottom first, then the span at the top. Then check the zero again, because on many transmitters each screw moves the other end a little.\n\nAccuracy is often stated in per cent of full scale, which on a 0 to 10 bar range is the same as its span. Then the error stays the same size across the range, so it is a bigger share of a low reading.[^5] ±0.5% of 10 bar is ±0.05 bar anywhere on the line. At 10 bar that is 0.5% of the reading, but at 1 bar it is 5%.\n\nNot every poor reading is the line in the wrong place. Repeat readings at one pressure can scatter round the line. A digital display rounds each reading onto its steps. One that steps in 0.1 bar and shows 6.3 bar says only that the value is nearer 6.3 than 6.2 or 6.4. So log 6.3 bar, not 6.27. Some instruments read a little differently on the way up than on the way down. That is [[hysteresis]], where the output depends on which way the input last moved. The screws move the whole line, so they cannot cure scatter, steps or hysteresis. Accuracy is how close a reading comes to the true value.[^6] Precision is how closely it can be pinned down, without reference to the true value.[^7] So tight clusters of readings 2% high at every point are precise but not accurate, and the zero screw puts them right. An oil pressure trip often acts on a number that comes down a chain like this, so the trip can be trusted only as far as that number can. **Calibration checks an instrument's input-to-output line against a standard. The zero screw slides the line and the span screw changes its slope,[^2] so a zero error is the same at every point and a span error grows from the bottom of the range to the top.**"

   "now": "**Safety first:** a transmitter in service feeds a display, a controller or a trip. Its output will move while you test it, and whatever it feeds may act on that. Agree with whoever relies on the reading, and put the loop or trip it feeds in a safe state under your permit, before you isolate it from the process. Then vent the pressure trapped behind the isolating valve safely before you connect the reference.\n\n- Note the range, then work out the span on the way in and on the way out.\n- Write down the ideal output at 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% of the range.\n- Feed each pressure in from a reference, going up and then coming down. Record each output.\n- Work out each error as (reading − ideal) ÷ output span × 100, in per cent of span.\n- The same error at every point is a zero error. An error that grows from nothing at the bottom is a span error. Set the zero at the bottom, then the span at the top, then check the zero again.\n- Up and down runs that differ, or repeat readings that spread, are not the line. The screws will not cure them. Record them and compare them with the instrument's stated accuracy.\n- Log a reading to the display's step, never finer.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** on a 4 to 20 mA signal, 1% of span is 0.16 mA.\n\n**Worked example:** a 0 to 16 bar transmitter sends 4 to 20 mA, so its line rises 1 mA a bar. It reads 4.08 mA at 0 bar, which is 0.08 mA high, or +0.5% of span. It reads 19.92 mA at 16 bar, which is 0.08 mA low, or −0.5%. At 8 bar it reads 12.00 mA, exactly right. So a check at the middle alone would pass it. Slide the zero down 0.08 mA. Now 0 bar reads 4.00 mA, and 16 bar reads 19.84 mA, 0.16 mA low, or −1% of span. That is a span error, so tilt the top up 0.16 mA to 20.00 mA. Then check 0 bar again.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Dividing the error by the reading, or by 20 mA, instead of by the 16 mA span.\n- Forgetting the 4 mA at the bottom. 75% of the range is 16 mA, not 12 mA.\n- Turning the span screw to cure an error that is the same at every point.\n- Checking one point only. A zero error and a span error can cancel in the middle.\n- Reading ±0.5% of span as ±0.5% of the reading. At a tenth of the range it is 5% of the reading.\n- Logging a reading finer than the display steps.\n- Expecting a calibration to cure scatter or hysteresis."
