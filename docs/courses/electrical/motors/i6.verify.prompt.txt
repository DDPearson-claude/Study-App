Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- In a series motor the same current flows through field and armature. [R1]
- A turning armature makes a back emf opposing the supply; it is zero at standstill, so a motor not yet turning draws maximum current, and a load that slows it drops the back emf and raises the current. [R2][R3][R4][R5]
- The armature current is limited at start by armature resistance alone, so an external starting resistance is placed in series with the armature. [R5][R6][R7]
- The motor's back emf is the supply voltage less the voltage lost in the resistance. [R8]
- In its linear (unsaturated) region a series motor's torque goes with the square of the armature current, so it has very high starting torque. [R9][R10][R11][R12]
- Remove the load and the current falls, the field weakens, and the speed rises towards destruction: series motors must have overspeed protection and be coupled directly, never belt driven. [R13][R14][R15][R16][R17]
- A shunt or compound motor can be run safely with no load but a series motor cannot. [R18][R19]
- A series motor suits crane hoists, elevators and large excavators, where a high starting torque is crucial. [R20]
- A universal motor can operate on either AC or DC power and uses an electromagnet as its stator. [R21]
- Starting current is supply voltage over armature resistance. Worked case: a 10 hp motor, 0.4 ohm armature, 260 V would draw 650 A, about twelve times full load; a 0.5 ohm, 220 V, 7.5 kW motor draws 440 A against 34 A. Guides for the limit differ (125 to 200 percent, or two to three times full-load … [R22][R23][R24][R25][R26]
- Armature resistance is low (0.05 to 0.5 ohm for most motor armatures), and counter emf does not exist until the armature turns, so an external starting resistance goes in series with the armature to keep the first current safe. [R27][R28]
- Series motor laws: below saturation flux follows current, so speed goes with 1 over current (very small current gives dangerous speed). - Reversing: reverse the armature or the field connections, not both. Reversing the supply reverses both, so direction is unchanged. [R34][R35]
- On AC, a plain DC series motor with a laminated field has its field current cut by the field coil reactance, so it runs poorly; small universal motors are built for either supply. [R36]
- The series-motor starter is a two-point starter with a no-load release (hold-on) coil in series with the armature: if the load is lost the current falls, the coil lets go and the arm springs back to OFF, preventing overspeed. It also drops out on voltage loss. [R37][R38][R39]
