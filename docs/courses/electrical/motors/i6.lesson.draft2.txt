 "explain": {
  "text": "A DC series motor has its field winding in series with the armature, so one current flows through both.[^1] Torque comes from the field acting on the armature current. Here both are that one current. So below saturation, torque goes with the current squared.[^10] Double the current and the torque is four times. That is why it starts so hard.\n\nA turning armature is a generator too, so it makes a [[back-emf]] that opposes the supply. Like any generator, it makes more with a stronger field or a faster spin. At standstill there is none, so only the windings' resistance limits the current.[^5] As the motor speeds up, the back-emf rises and the current falls. It settles where the back-emf is just under the supply voltage.\n\nNow take the load off. The motor speeds up, the back-emf rises and the current falls. But less current means a weaker field. A weaker field makes less back-emf at the same speed, so the motor speeds up again. Ignoring resistance, the speed goes with one over the current.[^29] At half the current it doubles. With no load it keeps climbing until the motor can damage itself.[^14] That is [[runaway]]. The picture is this ideal case, with no saturation or resistance. Real curves bend, but the speed still climbs as the load falls.\n\n**One current makes both the field and the torque, so torque climbs with the current squared, and with the load off the speed climbs dangerously.**[^13]"

  "text": "- Isolate, lock off and prove dead before you touch the motor or its starter.\n- Check the drive. Never bypass the starting resistance.\n- Megger the windings to frame.\n- Measure the armature and field resistance. An open circuit means a broken winding or connection.\n- Look at the brushes and commutator for wear, pitting and sparking.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** with no starter, the first current is the supply voltage over the resistance. That is many times full load.\n\n**Worked example:** take a 260 V motor with a 0.4 Ω armature. At standstill there is no back-emf. So I = 260 ÷ 0.4 = 650 A, about twelve times its full-load current.[^22] A starting resistance in series with the armature brings that down. Starters keep it to about 125 to 200% of full load.[^23] Some aim for two or three times.[^24]\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Running it on test with the load uncoupled. A series motor cannot run safely with no load.[^18]\n- Swapping the supply leads to reverse it.
