  "text": "A single winding on one phase cannot start a motor, because its field only pulses. A split-phase motor adds a second winding whose current is out of step with the first. Together they make a field that turns, and the rotor follows it.\n\nRemember lesson 1. Three currents peaked one after another, in coils set round the stator, so the field turned. One winding has only one current. Its field grows, collapses and reverses along one line. At rest, that gives the rotor no push either way. Once the rotor is turning, though, the same winding keeps it going.\n\nSo the motor gets a [[start winding]], set 90 electrical degrees round from the main winding and fed in parallel with it. It is thin wire with fewer turns, so it has more resistance and less inductance. In the impedance triangle, that gives a smaller angle. So its current lags the supply less than the main winding's. Now two coils at right angles carry currents that peak at different moments, and the field turns. The gap is small, about 30° in a typical motor.[^8] Starting torque goes with both currents and the sine of the angle between them.[^10] Sin 30° is 0.5, against 1 at 90°, so the start is weak.\n\nAt about 75% of full speed a [[centrifugal switch]] opens and drops the start winding out.[^11] Its thin wire is not rated to stay in. The main winding runs the motor from there, a little below synchronous speed.\n\n**A second winding, its current out of step with the main winding's, turns a pulsing field into a turning one.**[^3]"
