Topic: Electric motors: what you need on each one
His level: KNOWS A LITTLE: he has met the basics but may hold common misconceptions.
This lesson: i2 "The DOL starter" — Start pulls in the contactor, a contact across Start keeps its coil fed; Stop, overload or power cut drops it out until restarted. [mechanism]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i3 The variable speed drive — A variable speed drive turns the supply to DC, then back to AC at a chosen frequency, with voltage in proportion up to rated speed.
- i4 The split-phase motor — One single-phase winding alone makes a field that only pulses, so a thin-wire start winding adds a current out of step and the field turns.
- i5 The capacitor-start motor — A capacitor in series with the start winding widens the angle between the two currents towards 90°, giving much more starting torque than split-phase.
- i6 The DC series motor — One current feeds both field and turning armature, so torque climbs steeply with current, and with the load taken off the speed climbs dangerously.
- i7 The DC shunt motor — With its field across the supply holding the flux steady, the speed dips only a little under load, though sources differ on how little.
- i8 The DC compound motor — A series and a shunt field together give high starting torque yet a limited no-load speed, as long as the series field aids the shunt.

  "text": "A DOL starter switches a motor straight onto the full supply through a contactor. Start pulls the contactor in. A contact across Start then keeps the coil fed, so the motor runs on after you let go. Anything that opens the coil's circuit drops it all out, and it stays out until someone presses Start again.\n\nFollow the coil circuit in the drawing. Start (3), Stop (8) and the overload's release contact (10) are all in series with the coil (4). Here it is fed from one phase and the neutral, so it sees 230 V. Start is normally open and sprung. Press it and the coil is fed. The coil's magnet pulls the contactor's moving part in. That closes the three main contacts (5), and the motor starts on full voltage. The same movement closes a small extra contact, the [[latching contact]] (7). It is wired across Start. So when Start springs back, the coil still has a path through 7. The coil does the pulling and the holding. The latching contact only keeps the coil fed.\n\nStop is normally closed. Press it and the coil circuit opens. The coil lets go, so the main contacts and 7 open together. Let go of Stop and nothing happens, because Start and 7 are both open now. The overload works the same way. Too much current heats its bimetal strips (9) until they bend and open 10. The overload has no main contacts of its own. It stops the motor only by opening the coil's circuit. A supply cut long enough to drop the coil out does it too. When the supply comes back, Start and 7 are still open, so the motor stays stopped. That is [[no-volt protection]]: a machine does not start up again by itself after a power cut.\n\n**A contactor with a latching contact across Start gives no-volt protection.
