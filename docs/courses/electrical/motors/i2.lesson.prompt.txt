  i4  The split-phase motor — One single-phase winding alone makes a field that only pulses, so a thin-wire start winding adds a current out of step and the field turns. [mechanism] (needs i1)
  i5  The capacitor-start motor — A capacitor in series with the start winding widens the angle between the two currents towards 90°, giving much more starting torque than split-phase. [quantity] (needs i4)
  i6  The DC series motor — One current feeds both field and turning armature, so torque climbs steeply with current, and with the load taken off the speed climbs dangerously. [quantity]
  i7  The DC shunt motor — With its field across the supply holding the flux steady, the speed dips only a little under load, though sources differ on how little. [mechanism] (needs i6)
  i8  The DC compound motor — A series and a shunt field together give high starting torque yet a limited no-load speed, as long as the series field aids the shunt. [concept] (needs i6, i7)

Research notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- The isolator closes the supply; Start (normally open, sprung) makes the coil circuit; the coil pulls in the main contacts, and a latching contact across Start keeps the coil fed once Start is released. [1][2][3][4][5]
- Stop (normally closed) breaks the coil circuit, and so does the overload's release contact after its bimetal strips bend; the coil drops out and the contacts release. [6][7][8][9]
- The coil does the pulling and holding of the main contacts; the latching contact's job is to keep the coil fed. (His sheet's item 7 words it as the latching contacts pulling and holding the main contacts.) [3][5][10]
- After a power cut the coil drops out and the motor stops; it does not restart by itself, because the holding contact has returned to open. A contactor with a holding contact gives low-voltage protection; maintained contacts give low-voltage release. [11][12][13][14][15]
- The overload's contacts sit in series with the coil and are operated by heat, not by the contactor's movement. [16]
- Overload relays protect against overload only: fuses or a breaker give the short-circuit protection, and a maker advises fuses with the thermal overload relay. [17][18][19]
- His coil is 230 V, fed from one phase and neutral; the coil rating must be right for how it is connected and for the supply voltage. [20][21][22]
- Direct-on-line starting is uncontrolled: full current and torque from the start. Start current is usually 6 to 8 times rated and can exceed 10 times. [23][24][25]
- His sheet gives DOL up to 5 kW; the web summary gives often under 4 kW; ABB says the limit depends on the area of use, and some utilities restrict DOL starting for motors of 5.5 kW or higher. [26][27][28]  [CONTESTED: Rules of thumb for the largest DOL motor differ (5 kW, under 4 kW, 5.5 kW); the maker says it depends on the area of use and gives 5.5 kW as one utility limit.]
- DOL starters are used for compressors, pumps and fans; repeated stopping and starting can make the thermal overload operate. [29][30]
- Star-delta cuts starting current to about 30% (a third) and starting torque to about 25% of direct-on-line; the motor must be delta connected when running, and the starter uses three contactors, an overload and a timer. [31][32][33][34][35]
- Star-delta works only when the load is light at the start, and there are current peaks at the switch from star to delta. [36][37]
- His group's star-delta worked answer multiplies the star power by 3 to get the delta power. [38]
- The web summary's wording that DOL gives 100% of rated torque is loose: ABB gives full starting torque as 1.5 to 2.5 times rated for a small motor. [39][40]
- His group handout says that start buttons are wired in parallel. [41]
- Isolate and prove dead before work on a starter: the maker's own instruction is to disconnect power before any work on the equipment. [42]
- A contactor is rated for the motor it switches: for squirrel-cage motors (duty AC-3) it makes about 6 to 8 times the rated current when closing, or 5 to 7 on another maker's figure, and breaks only the rated current when opening; most contact wear comes from the making. [43][44]
- Restarting a motor before it has stopped turning can give a current peak up to twice that of a start from standstill, so a quick Stop-then-Start is hard on the contacts. [45]
- A DOL starter connects the motor straight to the supply at full voltage; the start current can cause voltage dips on the supply and heavy forces in the cables and the motor windings. [46][47]
- On a generator supply, DOL gives the highest inrush of any starting method, and the motor can develop several times full-load torque; on a ship the generator, not the starter, is the limit, and large motors starting one after another during manoeuvring can cause severe voltage dips. [48][49][50]
- Star-delta puts each winding on 1/sqrt(3) of the line voltage while starting; the changeover can briefly open the supply and give a current spike, which on one generator maker's figures can be as high as 5 x run kVA if it is badly timed, but ABB says is typically lower than a DOL start. [51][52][53]  [CONTESTED: Sources differ on the size of the star-to-delta changeover spike: ABB says typically lower than a DOL start; a generator maker warns it can reach 5 x run kVA if the changeover is not set well.]
- The overload relay is set to the motor's rated current, not its trip current: no trip at 1.05 times the setting, trip at 1.2 times; it also protects against phase failure, and it has no power contacts so it can only work through the contactor. [54][55][56]
- After an overload trip the relay will not reset until it has cooled, which lets the motor cool before it is restarted. [57]
- The contactor coil must have the right voltage: the usual limits are 0.85 to 1.1 times its rating; too low a voltage can fail to close it, make it chatter and melt the coil, and too high a voltage can burn it out. [58][59][60][61]
- A buzzing or chattering contactor points to low or unstable coil voltage, a poor control connection, a wrong coil, dirty or unseated magnet faces, or a damaged shading ring; check the voltage across the coil terminals while it is closing. [62]

interactive

- controls: one. Add a second only if the idea cannot be seen without it. Each has an id (camelCase) and a label (at most 6 words), and is either
    numeric: min < max, step dividing the range, value (the opening setting), unit (at most 10 characters, "" if none); an on/off switch is min 0, max 1, step 1 and may start off; or

  Open on a realistic case (zero when zero is the real case). Ranges make the effect unmistakable but stay on the side of it the idea is about (past a turning point only when that is the lesson).

THE NUMBER RULE

- computed: worked out from the rule in whatAmILookingAt; a peak or threshold between slider steps is "near 85%", never "at 85%";
