Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i2 The DOL starter — Start pulls in the contactor, a contact across Start keeps its coil fed; Stop, overload or power cut drops it out until restarted.
- i3 The variable speed drive — A variable speed drive turns the supply to DC, then back to AC at a chosen frequency, with voltage in proportion up to rated speed.
- i4 The split-phase motor — One single-phase winding alone makes a field that only pulses, so a thin-wire start winding adds a current out of step and the field turns.
- i5 The capacitor-start motor — A capacitor in series with the start winding widens the angle between the two currents towards 90°, giving much more starting torque than split-phase.
- i6 The DC series motor — One current feeds both field and turning armature, so torque climbs steeply with current, and with the load taken off the speed climbs dangerously.
- i7 The DC shunt motor — With its field across the supply holding the flux steady, the speed dips only a little under load, though sources differ on how little.
- i8 The DC compound motor — A series and a shunt field together give high starting torque yet a limited no-load speed, as long as the series field aids the shunt.

Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- Three windings 120 electrical degrees apart, each fed from one phase, make one combined magnetic field that turns at a constant speed, the synchronous speed. [R1]
- The turning field cuts the short-circuited rotor conductors and induces a current in them; that current makes its own field, which interacts with the stator field to give torque. [R2][R3]
- The rotor has to turn slower than the field: with no speed difference there is no cutting, so no current is induced and no torque is produced. An induction motor can approach synchronous speed but never reach it. [R4][R5][R6][R7][R8]
- Slip is the difference between synchronous and actual speed, normally written as a percentage. Load makes the rotor slow and slip grow; taking load off lets it speed up and slip shrink. [R9][R10][R11][R12]
- Synchronous speed is 120 f / p rpm for f hertz and p poles. [R13]
- Full-load slip of a cage motor is a few percent: about 2 to 3% (one maker) and under 5% (another). [R10][R14]
- Starting from the supply direct, a motor draws usually 6 to 8 times its rated current and can draw more than 10 times. [R15][R16]
- For a small motor (up to about 30 kW) ABB's 2010 handbook gives a direct-on-line starting torque of 1.5 to 2.5 times rated; its 2004 edition gave 2.5 to 3. The figure is a range. [R17][R18]
- Swapping the supply connections on any two of the three coils reverses the field and so the direction of rotation. [R19][R20]
- A cage rotor is simple, rugged and low-maintenance, with no brushes or slip rings; a wound rotor gives higher starting torque and speed control by external resistance, at more upkeep. Light load gives a poor power factor. [R21][R22][R23]
- Cage induction motors suit constant-speed work such as fans, pumps, conveyors and compressors; their speed is hard to control without a drive and their starting current is high. [R24][R25][R26]
- Induction motors are used on ships for pumps, fans and compressors up to 700 kW or more, totally enclosed; the UK chose an induction motor to drive its Type 45 destroyer. [R27][R28][R29]
- A three-phase motor run from a single phase will not self-start but can be hand started; a motor already running when one phase is lost heats very quickly and must be taken off by its protection. [R30][R31][R32]
- In one long-run record of motor failures, overloads were 30%, single-phasing 14% and bearing failure 13%; worn starter contacts are given as the most common cause of single-phasing. [R33][R34]
- An insulation resistance test shows a wet or contaminated motor or a dead short to earth but cannot prove it good; a winding resistance test compares phases, and under 1% difference is typical of a good winding. [R35][R36][R37]
- His sheet says a brushless motor is safe for hazardous areas; a maker builds hazardous-area motors to prevent ignition and certifies them to ATEX and IECEx. - Slip is proportional to the load: a motor at half load has slip halfway between its full-load slip and zero. [R48][R49]
- When load goes up the rotor slows, slip grows, and rotor current and torque grow with it until the motor's torque balances the load's. At small slip the torque is directly proportional to slip. [R50][R51][R52][R53]
