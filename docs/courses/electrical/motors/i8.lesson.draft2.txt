  "text": "A DC compound motor has two field windings on the same poles. One is a shunt field across the supply, as in lesson 7. The other is a series field in line with the armature, as in lesson 6. When their fields aid each other, it is [[cumulative compound]]. It starts hard like a series motor. Yet running light, it settles at a definite speed instead of running away.\n\nTorque goes with flux times armature current. Speed goes with back-emf over flux. In this motor the flux is a steady shunt part plus a series part that grows with the current. Put a heavy load on and the current rises. The series part adds flux, so the torque climbs faster than the current. Take the load off and the current falls to a trickle. The series part fades, but the shunt part stays. In the series motor the whole field faded with the current. Here the shunt part keeps the flux up. So the speed rises to a definite no-load speed and holds there.[^7]\n\nThe price is speed regulation. The flux falls as the load comes off, so the speed rises further than a shunt motor's. On the picture's example motor, with 15% of its field from the series winding, it rises by 22.4%. A compound motor is steadier than a series motor, but not as steady as a shunt motor.[^9] More series share gives more starting torque, and a higher no-load speed.\n\nReverse the series field and it subtracts from the shunt field. That is [[differential compound]]. Now the flux falls as the load rises. With a very light series winding the speed stays almost constant, or rises slightly, as the load goes on.[^12] With more, the speed rises with load, and it can become unstable.[^36] It is rarely used. **A cumulative compound motor's series field takes the heavy load, while its shunt field stops it racing when the load comes off.**[^52]"
