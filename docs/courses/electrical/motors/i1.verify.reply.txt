   "now": "A three-phase induction motor makes a magnetic field that turns, and the field drags the rotor round a little behind it. That shortfall is what makes the torque.\n\nThe three windings sit 120 electrical degrees apart, one on each phase. The currents peak in turn, so the strongest point of the field moves from winding to winding. The field turns at a steady speed, the [[synchronous speed]], ns = 120 × f ÷ p.[^1] Here f is the supply frequency and p the number of poles. On 50 Hz a two-pole field turns at 3000 rpm and a four-pole one at 1500 rpm.\n\nIn the usual cage motor, the rotor is a cage of bars, shorted together by a ring at each end. As the field sweeps past the bars it induces a voltage in them. The bars are shorted, so a current flows. A current in a magnetic field feels a force, so the bars are pushed round after the field. Now picture the rotor at the field's own speed. The field would not move past the bars, so nothing would be induced. No current means no torque. So under its own power the rotor always runs a little slower than the field. That shortfall, as a fraction of ns, is the [[slip]]: (ns − n) ÷ ns. Put load on and the rotor slows a little. The field then sweeps past the bars faster and induces more voltage in them. More current flows, and the torque rises until it matches the load. Take load off and the rotor speeds up until the slip is small again. The picture keeps slip in step with load, a simplified model that holds up to full load but not far past it.\n\nA cage motor will normally start by itself as soon as all three lines are live. So whether a pump comes back after a power cut is down to its starter, not the motor. **Driving a load, the rotor always runs a little below synchronous speed, because only that slip lets the field sweep past the bars. More load means more slip, more rotor current and more torque.**[^21]"
