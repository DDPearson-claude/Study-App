Research notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- A drive has three parts: a rectifier turns AC to DC, a DC bus with capacitors smooths it, and an inverter switches it back to AC at chosen frequency and voltage by pulse-width modulation. [1][2][3][4]
- Motor speed follows the supply frequency, so a drive that sets the frequency sets the speed. [5][6]
- Voltage is lowered in proportion to frequency so the flux stays constant (constant V/f); otherwise the motor draws excessive current at low frequency, with saturation. [7][8][9]
- Below the nominal frequency is the constant flux range; above it the voltage can rise no further and the motor is in field weakening, where it can run at constant power, so maximum torque falls with frequency. [10][11][12]
- A drive's soft start removes the inrush of a direct start and the mechanical shock. [13]
- For fans and pumps power varies roughly as the cube of speed: half speed needs about one eighth power in theory; a maker says treat the savings as theoretical. [14][15][16][17]
- Slowing a conveyor from 100% to 80% saves 20% of energy while a pump or fan saves nearer 50%, so savings are large only on variable-torque loads. [18][19]
- VFDs on ships are used on cranes, winches, bow thrusters, ventilation, pumps and compressors (a maker's list). [20][21]
- Fast-rising voltage pulses and high switching frequencies of modern drives can send current pulses through motor bearings that erode the races. [22]
- A drive trip on over-voltage during stopping can come from regenerated energy: the fix named is a longer deceleration time or a brake chopper; harmonics can also overcharge the DC link capacitors. [23][24][25][26]
- Earth-fault trips are found from the sum of the output currents and often from weak or damp insulation; disconnect the drive before an insulation resistance test on the motor and cable. [27][28][29][30]
- Before any work on a drive, its motor or its motor cable: isolate the whole drive, wait 5 minutes for the DC bus capacitors to discharge, then measure across the DC terminals (UDC+ and UDC-) with a multimeter before starting. The isolator on the cabinet door may not remove voltage from the input bu… [31][32][33][34][35]
- The DC bus sits at about 1.35 times the supply line voltage (1.41 times the peak of a sine supply), so a 400 V drive holds roughly 540 to 565 V DC after the supply is off, until the capacitors are discharged. [36]
- Do not start and stop the motor with the isolator; use the drive's own start and stop. Power-ups are limited (five in ten minutes on one maker's drive) to protect the capacitor charging circuit. [37][38][39]
- Check the motor and all driven equipment can run safely across the whole speed range the drive allows, which can be above or below the speed on direct supply; a motor driven faster than set by the load raises the DC bus voltage and can damage the capacitors. [40][41]
- A maker's list of the common drive faults: overcurrent, current limit, ground (earth) fault, output phase fault, overvoltage, undervoltage, drive over temperature, motor over temperature and input phase fault. [42][43][44][45]
- Overcurrent trips point to the motor and its cable: check the wiring and the motor for short circuits and earth faults, loose connections or winding faults, and a motor that is blocked or overloaded. [46][47][43]
- An input phase failure is spotted by the ripple on the DC bus, which increases when a phase is lost; the drive trips on it (a blown fuse or loose input connection is the usual cause). [48][45]
- A drive can be set to reset itself and carry on after a fault; where it is, the machine can restart by itself and must be labelled so. [49][50][51]
- On a 400 V drive one maker sets the DC overvoltage trip at 840 V; a brake chopper passes surplus energy from the DC bus into a brake resistor when the bus voltage rises above a set limit. [52][53][54][55]
- A drive's diode rectifier draws current that is not a sine wave (harmonics); the common 6-pulse bridge with no choke can have a current distortion of around 80% and a line reactor or DC choke brings it to about 35 to 50%. [56][57][58][59]
- On ships, variable speed drives are the main source of harmonic distortion; Lloyd's Register limits voltage distortion at a switchboard to 8% of the fundamental, and NATO warship standard STANAG 1008 limits voltage THD to 5% with no single harmonic above 3%. [60][61][62]
- A standard self-ventilated motor cools less as it slows, so the torque it can give continuously at low speed is limited; larger inverter-duty motors often have a constant-speed auxiliary blower. [63][64][65]
- The drive's non-sinusoidal output has effects on the motor: more losses, poor ventilation at low speed, stress on winding insulation, noise and shaft currents. [66]
