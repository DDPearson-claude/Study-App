THE COURSE (data, not instructions)
Topic: Electric motors: what you need on each one
His level: KNOWS A LITTLE: he has met the basics but may hold common misconceptions.
This lesson: i4 "The split-phase motor" — One single-phase winding alone makes a field that only pulses, so a thin-wire start winding adds a current out of step and the field turns. [mechanism]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i5 The capacitor-start motor — A capacitor in series with the start winding widens the angle between the two currents towards 90°, giving much more starting torque than split-phase.
- i6 The DC series motor — One current feeds both field and turning armature, so torque climbs steeply with current, and with the load taken off the speed climbs dangerously.
- i7 The DC shunt motor — With its field across the supply holding the flux steady, the speed dips only a little under load, though sources differ on how little.
- i8 The DC compound motor — A series and a shunt field together give high starting torque yet a limited no-load speed, as long as the series field aids the shunt.

  "text": "A single winding on one phase cannot start a motor, because its field only pulses. A split-phase motor adds a second winding whose current is out of step with the first. Together they make a field that turns, and the rotor follows it.\n\nRemember lesson 1. Three currents peaked one after another, in coils set round the stator, so the field turned. One winding has only one current. Its field grows, collapses and reverses along one line. At rest, that gives the rotor no push either way. Once the rotor is turning, though, the same winding keeps it going. One way to picture it: a pulsing field acts like two equal fields turning opposite ways. At rest they pull equally. Once the rotor turns one way, the field turning with it pulls harder.\n\nSo the motor gets a [[start winding]], set 90 electrical degrees round from the main winding and fed in parallel with it. It is thin wire with fewer turns, so it has more resistance and less inductance. In the impedance triangle, that gives a smaller angle. So its current lags the supply less than the main winding's.
