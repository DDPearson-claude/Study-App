  "text": "A variable speed drive sets a motor's speed by setting its supply frequency. It turns the supply into DC, then builds new AC at the frequency it wants. It lowers the voltage in step with the frequency, so the motor's flux stays where it should be.\n\nStart from the last two lessons. The field turns at 120 × f ÷ p, and the rotor follows a little behind. So change f and you change the speed. The drive does that in three stages. A rectifier turns the incoming AC into DC. Capacitors on the [[DC bus]] smooth it. On a 400 V supply the bus sits at about 400 × 1.35 = 540 V DC.[^36] An inverter then switches that DC on and off very fast. By varying the width of its pulses, it builds AC at the frequency and voltage it is asked for.\n\nWhy change the voltage too? Each half cycle, the voltage drives the flux up in the iron. Halve the frequency and each half cycle lasts twice as long. So at full voltage the flux would try to double. The iron saturates, and the motor draws far too much current. So the drive keeps volts per hertz constant. Our example motor is rated 400 V at 50 Hz, which is 8 V per hertz. At 25 Hz it gets 8 × 25 = 200 V. The flux stays at rated, so the motor can still give its rated torque at low speed. Its own fan slows too, though, so a self-cooled motor cannot keep that up continuously. That holds up to 50 Hz, the motor's [[base speed]]. Above it, the drive has no more volts to give. The frequency rises, the voltage stays at 400 V, and the flux falls. At 60 Hz it is 50 ÷ 60 = 83% of rated. That is [[field weakening]]. The motor runs at about constant power, so the torque it can give falls.\n\n**A drive sets the speed by the frequency, and keeps the volts per hertz constant up to base speed, so the flux stays at rated.**[^7]"
