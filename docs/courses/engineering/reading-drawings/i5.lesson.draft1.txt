  "text": "- A centred valve does not prove a load is safe. A closed centre still leaks, so a raised load can creep down. Lower it or prop it mechanically, then isolate and vent by the plant's procedure before you work on it.\n- Count the boxes. That is the number of positions.\n- Count the lines on the box in use. That is the number of ports. Ports then positions make the label, such as 4/3.\n- Name the ports: P from the pump, T to the tank, A and B to the cylinder. Air valves are often numbered instead: 1 the supply, 2 and 4 the working lines, 3 and 5 the exhausts.\n- Read each end. A solenoid tag such as -Y1, a lever, a pilot line, a spring or a detent says what moves the valve and what brings it back.\n- Read the box against the ports. That is the drawn state, with nothing operating the valve.\n- For each actuator, slide its box against the ports and follow each arrow from port to port.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** the box beside a solenoid or lever is the box it brings in. Springs bring back the box at rest, which on a three-box valve is the middle one.\n\n**Worked example:** a valve has three boxes, with -Y1 and a spring at the left end and -Y2 and a spring at the right. Four ports meet its middle box, which joins P to T and blocks A and B. Four ports and three boxes make it a 4/3 valve. At rest the springs centre it, so the pump unloads to the tank and the cylinder holds, though it may creep. Energise -Y1 and the left box comes in. P joins A and B joins T, so oil fills the piston side and the rod comes out. Energise -Y2 and the right box's crossed arrows join P to B and A to T, so the rod goes back.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Reading the ports as positions, so a 4/2 becomes a 2/4.\n- Reading the box already against the ports after a solenoid is energised, instead of sliding the box beside it in.\n- Taking a detent for a spring, and expecting the valve to come back by itself.\n- Trusting a closed centre to hold a load for ever.\n- Forgetting the relief valve.
