  "explain": {
    "text": "A force can turn things as well as push them. Its turning effect is called its [[moment]].[^1] The moment is the force times its distance from the pivot, measured at right angles to the force's line. A spanner on a nut is the one you know from Vectors: 150 N on a 0.3 m spanner gives 45 N m. Push at half the distance and you get half the turn. Moments are measured in newton metres.[^2] Only the right-angle distance counts, because a force can slide along its own line without changing its moment.[^3] So a slanted push on the end of a bar has a shorter arm than the bar.\n\nSomething that stays still does not start to turn. So its turning effects balance, just as its pushes did in Forces in balance. The clockwise moments add up to the same as the anticlockwise moments.[^4] That is the [[principle of moments]]. It holds about any point you choose.[^6] That gives you a trick. Choose the point where an unknown force acts. That force then has no distance from the pivot, so its moment is zero and it drops out.[^7]\n\nTake a lifting beam resting on two trestles 4 m apart, with an example 6 kN chain block 1 m from the left trestle. Take moments about the left trestle. The block turns the beam clockwise with 6 × 1 = 6 kN m. The right trestle pushes up 4 m away, turning it anticlockwise. So its push times 4 must make 6 kN m, and it carries 1.5 kN. The two up pushes must add to the load. So the left trestle carries 6 − 1.5 = 4.5 kN. The nearer trestle always carries more. The far one pushes on a longer arm, so a smaller push balances the same moment. Hang the block at the middle and they share it equally.\n\nA uniform beam's own weight acts at its middle, since it is the same either side. Textbooks add that any evenly spread load acts the same way, as its total at its middle. Say this beam weighs 2 kN. About the left trestle that adds 2 × 2 = 4 kN m, so the right trestle carries 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 kN and the left 5.5 kN. One more case only turns. Two equal pulls in opposite directions, on different lines, push nowhere. Textbooks call the pair a [[couple]], and its moment is the same about any point. A shaft's torque is a couple, which comes back with rotation. **To find what each support carries, take moments about one support so its own push drops out, then make the up pushes add to the load.**"

  "practice": {
    "text": "- Before the lift, work out each support's share. An off-centre load puts most of its weight on the nearer support, so check that one against its rating, not half the load.\n- Sketch the beam. Mark each support, and every load with its distance from the left support.\n- Put the beam's own weight at its middle. Put an evenly spread load's total at the middle of where it lies.\n- Take moments about the left support. Add up each load times its distance.\n- Divide by the span. That is the right support's reaction.\n- Take that from the total load. That is the left support's reaction.\n- Check by taking moments about the right support. The two sides should match.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** each support carries the load times its distance to the other support, divided by the span. A block a quarter of the way along puts three quarters of its load on the nearer support.\n\n**Worked example:** a 5 m beam on two trestles weighs an example 1.5 kN, with an 8 kN chain block 2 m from the left. About the left trestle, the block gives 8 × 2 = 16 kN m. The beam's weight sits at 2.5 m and gives 1.5 × 2.5 = 3.75 kN m. That is 19.75 kN m in all, so the right trestle carries 19.75 ÷ 5 = 3.95 kN. The total load is 9.5 kN, so the left carries 9.5 − 3.95 = 5.55 kN. Check about the right trestle: 5.55 × 5 = 27.75 kN m, and 8 × 3 + 1.5 × 2.5 = 27.75 kN m.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Using the length of a slanted bar instead of the distance at right angles to the force's line.\n- Leaving out the beam's own weight, or putting a uniform beam's weight anywhere but its middle.\n- Giving the bigger share to the far support because more beam sits on its side.\n- Forgetting the force check at the end: the reactions add up to the total load."
