    "text": "A moving thing needs no net force to keep it moving. A net force changes how it moves. With no net force, a moving trolley rolls on at a steady speed in a straight line, and a still one stays still.[^1] A net force speeds it up or slows it down. Velocity has a direction too, so a net force across the motion turns it. Remember the speed-time graph from Calculus fundamentals? Its slope is the acceleration, how fast the speed changes, in m/s². The area under it is the distance covered. A crane trolley that speeds up from rest to 0.5 m/s in 2 s has a slope of 0.5 ÷ 2 = 0.25 m/s². The area under that part is a triangle, ½ × 2 × 0.5 = 0.5 m. For a steady acceleration, the slope and the area give four handy equations. Call the start speed u, the end speed v, the acceleration a, the time t and the distance s. The slope gives v = u + at. The area is the average speed times the time, so s = ½(u + v)t. Split it into a rectangle and a triangle, and s = ut + ½at². Take t out of the first two, v = u + at and s = ½(u + v)t, and v² = u² + 2as.\n\nPush a loaded trolley and let go, and it slows down and stops. That is why it feels as if motion needs a force. But a force did act: friction, holding it back. On a level rail with no friction and no air drag, nothing would slow it, and it would roll on at the same speed. The [[net force]] is what is left when you add every force on a body, as on a free-body diagram. A body moving at a steady velocity has no net force on it.[^2] The hook in Forces in balance stayed still with its forces adding to nothing. A trolley rolling at a steady 0.5 m/s along a straight rail is in balance in just the same way. Its drive pushes it forward exactly as hard as friction holds it back.\n\nA net force changes the velocity, and a change in velocity is an acceleration.[^4] The acceleration is in proportion to the net force and in its direction.[^3] The same force gives a heavier body less acceleration, in inverse proportion to its mass. So net force = mass × acceleration, F = ma, with F in newtons, m in kilograms and a in m/s². The 2000 kg trolley gaining 0.5 m/s in 2 s needs 2000 × 0.25 = 500 N net. If friction holds it back with 100 N, its drive must push 600 N. Running steady, the drive pushes just 100 N, and the net force is zero. Braking to rest in 2 s takes 500 N net the other way. So a graph of net force against time is not a copy of the speed graph. It follows the speed graph's slope. On a straight rail it is zero wherever the speed is steady, whatever that speed.\n\nFor a steady net force acting for a time t, the acceleration is the change in speed ÷ t. So F × t = m × the change in speed. Mass × velocity is a body's [[momentum]], in kg m/s. Force × time is the [[impulse]], the area under the force graph. A newton is a kg m/s², so a newton second is the same unit as a kg m/s. The 500 N net push for 2 s is 1000 N s, and it gives the trolley 2000 × 0.5 = 1000 kg m/s. Momentum is not a moment, the turning effect from Moments and beams, in newton metres. Textbooks add Newton's third law: forces come in pairs. When one body pushes on another, the other pushes back just as hard, the opposite way. So when a moving trolley couples to a still one, each pushes the other equally, for the same time. One loses as much momentum as the other gains, so the total stays the same while nothing else pushes along the rail. A 2000 kg trolley at 0.5 m/s carries 1000 kg m/s. Coupled to a still 2000 kg trolley, that is shared by 4000 kg, so the pair roll on at 0.25 m/s. This lesson treats each trolley as one solid body on a level rail, a simplified case. Real trolleys also lose a little to their wheels' spin, to any slope and to air drag. **A moving thing needs no net force to keep moving: a net force changes its velocity, and net force = mass × acceleration.**"
