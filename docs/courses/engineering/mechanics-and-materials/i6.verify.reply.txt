How fast the speed climbs is set by the shaft's moment of inertia: how much mass it carries, and how far out that mass sits. Torque is a moment on a shaft, the turning effect of a force.[^1] A motor's own torque acts like the couple in Moments and beams. It turns the shaft without pushing it sideways. How fast the shaft turns is its [[angular speed]], ω, in radians per second. One turn is 2π radians, so 1450 rev/min is 1450 × 2π ÷ 60 = 151.8 rad/s.\n\nPicture one mass m on an arm r out from the shaft. A torque T on the shaft pushes it along its circle with a force T ÷ r. By Newton's second law that force speeds the mass along its circle by T ÷ (m × r) each second. Its speed along the circle is ω × r, since it moves r metres for each radian the shaft turns. So ω rises by that gain ÷ r, which is T ÷ (m × r²) each second. That rise per second is the [[angular acceleration]], α, in rad/s². The radius counts twice. A longer arm turns the same torque into a smaller push. The mass must also go faster round its bigger circle for the same ω. Add up m × r² for every mass on the shaft and you have its [[moment of inertia]], I, in kg m². So net torque = I × α, Newton's second law for turning. The spin-up rig has two 25 kg masses 0.2 m out, so I = 2 × 25 × 0.2² = 2 kg m². A steady net torque of 50 N m gives α = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 rad/s². The speed rises 25 rad/s every second and reaches 151.8 rad/s in 151.8 ÷ 25 = 6.1 s. Slide the masses out to 0.4 m and I becomes 8 kg m², four times as much, so the same run-up takes 24.3 s. Remember the trolley in Motion and Newton's laws. A shaft turning at a steady speed has no net torque on it. The motor's torque just matches the load's and the friction's.\n\nPower follows the way it did for the winch in Work, energy and power. Each mass moves at ω × r, so its energy of motion is ½ × m × (ω × r)², which is ½ × (m × r²) × ω². Add the masses and a spinning shaft stores ½ × I × ω². Twice the speed stores four times the energy. More mass, or mass further out, stores more at the same speed.[^3]\n\nA real motor's torque changes with its speed. A load such as a pump or a fan often needs more torque the faster it turns. The net torque is the gap between the two.
