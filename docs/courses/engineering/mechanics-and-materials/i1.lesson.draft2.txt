  "explain": {
    "text": "Anything that stays still has forces on it that add to nothing. Any leftover force would start it moving.[^1] So you can find a force you cannot see. Pick one part, draw every force on it, and make them balance. That drawing is a [[free-body diagram]]. Each force on it has a giver: something touching the part, or something pulling from a distance, like the Earth on its weight. Take the crane hook. The crane wire pulls it up, and each sling leg pulls it down along its own line. The hook stays still, so those three pulls laid nose to tail close a triangle.\n\nNow the sling. Each leg pulls along its own line, but only its vertical part holds the load up.[^3] Two legs share the load, so each vertical part is half the load's weight. The two legs lean the same amount in opposite directions. So their sideways parts are equal and opposite, and cancel. They lift nothing. As the legs spread, each leg leans further over. The same pull then has a smaller vertical part, so the pull must grow to keep its half of the load up. In numbers, each leg's tension is half the weight divided by sin θ, where θ is the leg's angle from the horizontal. Take an example 1000 kg load, which weighs 9.81 kN. With 60° between the legs, θ is 60° and sin 60° is 0.866. Half the load is 4.905 kN, and 4.905 ÷ 0.866 = 5.66 kN a leg. With 120° between the legs, θ is 30° and sin 30° is 0.5. Each leg now pulls 9.81 kN, the whole load, and 8.50 kN of that pull is sideways. It is the ring you balanced in Vectors: two equal pulls 120° apart balance one pull the same size.\n\nThe number 1 ÷ sin θ is the [[load angle factor]]. It is 1.155 at 60°, 1.414 at 45° and 2.000 at 30° from the horizontal.[^8] Each leg carries half the load times that factor. The leg is no weaker at a wide angle.[^7] Its strength stays the same while its load grows, so a sling can be overloaded by its angle alone. Say one leg can safely take 8 kN, as an example. At 60° between the legs it carries 5.66 kN and is fine. At 120° the same leg carries 9.81 kN and is overloaded. Friction is a force with a giver too. Push a crate along a deck and the deck grips its base, holding it back along the deck. While the crate stays still, that grip matches your push exactly, up to the most the surfaces can give.\n\nTo size a pump's slow shaft, you start the same way: free it, then find what loads it. **Each leg holds up its share with its vertical part only, so the load in the legs of a sling increases as the angle between the legs increases.**[^4]"

  "practice": {
    "text": "- Before the lift, check the angle between the legs. Keep each leg well above 30° from the horizontal.[^5]\n- Free one part. Draw it on its own.\n- Draw every force on it, and name the giver of each one.\n- Make them balance: the across parts add to nothing, and so do the up parts.\n- For a two-leg sling, find each leg's angle from the horizontal, θ. Check which angle a chart uses before you read it.\n- Each leg's tension is half the load times the load angle factor, 1 ÷ sin θ.\n- Compare that tension with what one leg can safely take.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** 60° from the horizontal adds about 15% to each leg's share. 45° adds about 40%. At 30°, each leg carries the whole load.\n\n**Worked example:** a 500 kg pump, an example, on a two-leg sling with 90° between the legs. Its weight is 500 × 9.81 = 4905 N, so each leg's share is 2452.5 N. Each leg is at 45° from the horizontal, so the factor is 1.414. Each leg pulls 2452.5 × 1.414 = 3468 N, about 3.47 kN.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Halving the load and stopping there. Half is right only for legs hanging straight down.\n- Thinking a wide angle weakens the leg. Its strength is the same. Its load is bigger.\n- Reading an angle from the vertical as one from the horizontal. At 60° from the vertical, each leg carries the whole load, not 15% more than half.\n- Adding both leg tensions to get the hook's load. The sideways parts cancel, so the crane wire carries just the load.\n- Leaving friction off the diagram, or giving it its biggest value. On a still crate it only matches the push."
