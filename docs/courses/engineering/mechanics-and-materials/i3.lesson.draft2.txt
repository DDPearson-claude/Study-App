  "explain": {
    "text": "Any pull stretches a metal bar a little. Pull harder and it stays stretched, and harder still it breaks. A tensile test pulls a bar steadily to the break and records the force and the stretch all the way. Plotted as one curve, they show three different things: how stiff the metal is, where it starts to stretch for good, and how strong it is. Remember each sling leg's tension in Forces in balance? This is what such a pull does to the metal.\n\nTo make the curve belong to the metal, not to one bar, take out the bar's size. A thicker bar spreads the same pull over more metal. So divide the force by the area of the bar's section. That is the [[stress]], in newtons per square millimetre. A 10 kN pull on a 20 mm bar, with 314 mm² of section, gives 31.8 N/mm². Stress is not a force. The same 10 kN on a 10 mm bar, with a quarter of the area, gives four times the stress, 127 N/mm². A longer bar stretches further under the same stress, because every millimetre of it stretches the same. So divide the stretch by the length. That is the [[strain]]. It is a length over a length, so it has no units. A 1 m bar stretched 0.5 mm has a strain of 0.0005, or 0.05%. A squeeze works the same way, with the stress and strain pushing instead of pulling.\n\nAt low stress, strain rises in step with stress, like a spring's load against its stretch.[^2] Take the load off and the bar springs back to its first length. The slope of that straight start, stress ÷ strain, is the elastic modulus.[^3] For a pull it is called [[Young's modulus]]. It is the metal's stiffness: how much stress it takes to stretch it a given amount. Steel's is about 200 000 N/mm². An aluminium alloy's is about a third of that, so the same stress stretches it about three times as far. Past a certain stress the line bends over, and the bar starts to stretch for good. That stress is the yield strength.[^6] Let go now and only part of the stretch springs back. That part is still the elastic stretch, with the same stiffness. So the bar gives back the stress ÷ Young's modulus, along a line parallel to the straight start, and keeps the rest. Many metals bend over without a clear corner. Their yield strength is taken as the stress that leaves 0.2% of permanent strain.[^7] Pull on and the stress climbs to a peak, the tensile strength.[^8] Past it, the bar thins at one spot into a neck. The neck loses area faster than the metal gets stronger, so the force it carries falls until it breaks. Stiffness is the slope of the straight start. Strength is the height of the peak. A metal can be stiff and weak, or springy and strong.\n\nSo a part is designed to run well below its yield strength. The factor of safety is the failing stress ÷ the working stress. Even well below yield, a load that comes and goes can crack a part. At a tiny flaw or a sharp corner, the stress is higher than in the rest of the part. A crack starts there and grows a little each cycle, until the part breaks. That is fatigue. A turning shaft bent a little by its load sees it every turn. Each spot on its surface is stretched on one side and squeezed on the other. Textbooks add that fatigue is the most common way shafts fail. **Stiffness, yield and strength are three different things on one curve: the slope of its straight start, where it bends over, and the height of its peak.**"

  "practice": {
    "text": "- Safe way first: never load a part past its safe working load. That figure already has the factor of safety in it, so it is a limit, not a target.\n- Find the area of the section in mm². For a round bar it is π × radius².\n- Work out the stress: the force in newtons ÷ the area. Use newtons, not kN.\n- Compare it with the metal's yield strength. For a part that must not bend for good, the factor of safety is the yield strength ÷ your stress.\n- For the stretch, divide the stress by Young's modulus to get the strain. Then multiply by the length in mm.\n- On anything that turns or shakes, ask whether the load comes and goes. If it does, fatigue can set a safe stress well below yield.\n\n**Rule of thumb:** half the diameter means a quarter of the area, so four times the stress. Steel stretches about 0.5 mm per metre at 100 N/mm².\n\n**Worked example:** a 4 m steel tie rod, 16 mm across, carries 12 kN. Its area is π × 8² = 201 mm². The stress is 12 000 ÷ 201 = 59.7 N/mm². The strain is 59.7 ÷ 200 000 = 0.000298, so it stretches 0.000298 × 4000 = 1.19 mm. If its steel yields at an example 250 N/mm², the factor of safety is 250 ÷ 59.7 = 4.2.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Treating a stress as a force. A stress is the force on each square millimetre.\n- Dividing the area by the force instead of the force by the area.\n- Using a strain in per cent as if it were a plain fraction, which puts the answer out by 100.\n- Mixing up stiffness and strength. A stiff part stretches little. A strong part takes a big stress before it fails.\n- Ignoring sharp corners such as a keyway. Fatigue cracks often start at them."
