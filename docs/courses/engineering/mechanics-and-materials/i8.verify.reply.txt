A balance weight with the same mass × radius, opposite the heavy spot, cancels that pull at every speed.\n\nStart with the pull. A mass going round a circle needs a pull towards the centre to stay on it. That pull is m r ω²: the mass × its radius × the angular speed squared, in rad/s. A rotor with more mass on one side of its axis than the other has an [[unbalance]]. Its shaft must pull that heavy spot inwards. By Newton's third law, the spot pulls the shaft outwards just as hard. That outward pull points at the spot wherever it is, so it turns with the shaft. The speed counts twice. At double the speed, the spot moves twice as fast and swings its direction round twice as quickly. So the pull is four times as big.[^2] Take a 20 g spot 0.15 m from the axis at 1450 rev/min, which is 151.8 rad/s. Its pull is 0.02 × 0.15 × 151.8² = 69 N. At 2900 rev/min it is 277 N.\n\nNow the mounts. Remember the driven swing: a mass M on a spring of stiffness k swings on its own at its natural frequency, √(k ÷ M) in rad/s. Here the mass is a 50 kg rotor, on mounts 500 kN/m stiff. They swing on their own at √(500,000 ÷ 50) = 100 rad/s, which is 15.9 times a second. Seen from the side, the turning pull is a push that comes and goes once a turn. At 15.9 turns a second, 955 rev/min, each push arrives in step with the swing and adds to it. That speed is the rotor's [[critical speed]].[^3] Well below it, the pull is small and the stiff mounts give only a little. Well above it, each push reverses before the rotor's 50 kg can get moving, so the rotor cannot follow. The shake falls back from its peak and settles near a steady 0.06 mm, as the rotor turns about its own centre of mass. So the shaking does not just grow with speed. It peaks, then falls. Damping takes energy out of every swing, so more damping gives a lower, wider peak.[^4] At the critical speed the shake is 0.06 mm ÷ (2 × the damping factor). At a damping factor of 0.05 that is 0.6 mm, and doubling the damping halves it. So a running speed near a critical speed is one to avoid.\n\nDamping softens the peak, but the pull is still there. Balancing removes it. Fit a balance weight with the same mass × radius on the opposite side. Its pull is just as big and points the other way, at every speed. The two pulls cancel. In the model nothing is left to drive the mounts, while a real balance leaves a small remainder. A long rotor needs more care. A heavy spot near each end, on opposite sides, cancels in total but still rocks the shaft end over end, as a couple. A rigid rotor is balanced with weights at its two ends, and a flexible one needs weights in more places.[^5] This is a simplified model: a real rotor moves every way on its mounts and its shaft bends, so it has more than one critical speed. A gearbox's slow shaft is usually made thick to carry its torque. Good balance, and a critical speed clear of its running speed, let it carry that torque smoothly. **An unbalanced rotor shakes its mounts hardest near its critical speed, where its turning rate matches the mounts' natural frequency.**[^3]"
