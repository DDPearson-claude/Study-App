  "text": "A heavy spot on a spinning rotor pulls its shaft outwards, and the pull turns with it. So it pushes the mounts one way, then the other, once every turn. The shaking is worst at one speed, where the turning rate matches the rate the mounts swing at on their own. A balance weight with the same mass × radius, opposite the heavy spot, cancels that pull at every speed.\n\nStart with the pull. A mass going round a circle needs a pull towards the centre to stay on it. That pull is m r ω²: the mass × its radius × the angular speed squared, in rad/s. A rotor with more mass on one side of its axis than the other has an [[unbalance]]. Its shaft must pull that heavy spot inwards. By Newton's third law, the spot pulls the shaft outwards just as hard. That outward pull points at the spot wherever it is, so it turns with the shaft. The speed counts twice. At double the speed, the spot moves twice as fast and swings its direction round twice as quickly. So the pull is four times as big.[^8] Take a 20 g spot 0.15 m from the axis at 1450 rev/min, which is 151.8 rad/s. Its pull is 0.02 × 0.15 × 151.8² = 69 N. At 2900 rev/min it is 277 N.\n\nNow the mounts. Remember the driven swing: a mass M on a spring of stiffness k swings on its own at its natural frequency, √(k ÷ M) in rad/s. Here the mass is a 50 kg rotor, on mounts 500 kN/m stiff. They swing on their own at √(500,000 ÷ 50) = 100 rad/s, which is 15.9 times a second. Seen from the side, the turning pull is a push that comes and goes once a turn. At 15.9 turns a second, 955 rev/min, each push arrives in step with the swing and adds to it. That speed is the rotor's [[critical speed]].[^6] Well below it, the pull is small and the stiff mounts give only a little. Well above it, each push reverses before the rotor's 50 kg can get moving, so the rotor cannot follow. The shake falls back from its peak and settles near a steady 0.06 mm, as the rotor turns about its own centre of mass. So the shaking does not just grow with speed. It peaks, then falls. Damping takes energy out of every swing, so more damping gives a lower, wider peak.[^2] At the critical speed the shake is 0.06 mm ÷ (2 × the damping factor). At a damping factor of 0.05 that is 0.6 mm, and doubling the damping halves it. So a running speed near a critical speed is one to avoid.\n\nDamping softens the peak, but the pull is still there. Balancing removes it. Fit a balance weight with the same mass × radius on the opposite side. Its pull is just as big and points the other way, at every speed. The two pulls cancel. In the model nothing is left to drive the mounts, while a real balance leaves a small remainder. A long rotor needs more care. A heavy spot near each end, on opposite sides, cancels in total but still rocks the shaft end over end, as a couple. A rigid rotor is balanced with weights at its two ends, and a flexible one needs weights in more places.[^11] This is a simplified model: a real rotor moves every way on its mounts and its shaft bends, so it has more than one critical speed. The slow shaft is made thick to carry its torque. Good balance, and a critical speed clear of its running speed, let it carry that torque smoothly. **An unbalanced rotor shakes its mounts hardest at its critical speed, where its turning rate matches the mounts' natural frequency.**[^6]"

  "text": "- Safe way first: isolate and lock off the machine before you touch its rotor or fit a balance weight. A balance weight's fixing must hold its pull of m r ω², which is large at speed, so fix it firmly before a run.\n- Note the speed where the shaking peaks on a run-up or run-down. That is near a critical speed.\n- Compare it with the running speed. Machines are designed to run at speeds away from their critical speeds.[^7] For a shaft's twisting vibration, one guideline keeps the running speed at least 10% clear of a critical speed where it can.[^10]\n- Critical speed in rev/min = the natural frequency in Hz × 60.\n- Work out an unbalance pull as m r ω², with ω in rad/s: rev/min × 2π ÷ 60.\n- To balance a single disc, fit a weight opposite the heavy spot with the same mass × radius. Balance mass = the spot's mass × its radius ÷ the weight's radius.\n- Balance a long rotor at both ends.\n- If shaking remains after balancing, check the coupling's alignment too.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** double the speed and an unbalance pulls four times as hard. Damping softens a resonance. Balancing removes its cause.\n\n**Worked example:** a fan has a 12 g heavy spot 0.25 m from its axis and runs at 1480 rev/min. That is 1480 × 2π ÷ 60 = 155.0 rad/s. The pull is 0.012 × 0.25 × 155.0² = 72.1 N, turning with the fan. A balance weight goes 0.2 m out, opposite the spot. Its mass is 12 × 0.25 ÷ 0.2 = 15 g.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Leaving the speed in rev/min in m r ω².\n- Expecting the shake to rise with speed for ever. It peaks near the critical speed and falls back above it.\n- Adding damping to cure an unbalance. It softens the peak, but the pull remains.\n- Matching the balance weight's mass, not its mass × radius.\n- Fitting the balance weight on the same side as the heavy spot."
