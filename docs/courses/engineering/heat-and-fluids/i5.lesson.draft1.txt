  "explain": {
    "text": "Still liquid presses on everything in it, and it presses harder the deeper you go. The reason is weight. The pressure at any depth comes from the weight of the liquid above it.[^1] Floating follows straight from that. The liquid under a thing pushes up harder than the liquid over it pushes down.\n\nPicture a column of liquid standing on a patch of 1 m², reaching up to the surface. Its height is the depth, h. Its volume is h × 1 m². Its mass is that volume times the liquid's [[density]], its mass per cubic metre.[^4] Its weight is that mass times g, 9.81 newtons for each kilogram. All that weight rests on the 1 m² patch. So the pressure there is density × g × depth, written p = ρgh, where ρ, the Greek letter rho, is the density. Textbooks add that a liquid barely squeezes, so its density is near enough the same all the way down. Seawater's density is about 1025 kg/m³.[^3] So 10 m of it adds 1025 × 9.81 × 10, about 100.6 kPa. That is about as much again as the whole atmosphere.[^11] A gauge reads that ρgh. Add the atmosphere's 100 kPa for the absolute pressure, as with gases.\n\nLook at what ρgh leaves out. The tank's width is not in it, and nor is its shape. Join a narrow tank, a wide one and a flaring one at the bottom, and fill them. The liquid settles at one level in all three. At any one depth, the probe reads the same in each. At a point, the pressure pushes every way at once. Still water presses because it has weight.\n\nNow hold a cube 1 m on a side under seawater, its top 2 m down. The pushes on its sides match in pairs, so they cancel. Its top is pushed down by about 20.1 kPa. Its bottom is 3 m down, so it is pushed up by about 30.2 kPa. On each 1 m² face, the bottom wins by about 10.1 kN. That net push up is the [[upthrust]]. It is ρg × the cube's volume, the weight of the seawater it has pushed aside. So the upthrust on anything in a liquid equals the weight of the liquid it displaces.[^6] Push the cube deeper and both pushes grow by the same amount, so the upthrust stays the same.\n\nWhether it floats is a contest between its weight and that upthrust. A cube less dense than the liquid rises until the upthrust equals its weight. The depth of its bottom below the surface is its [[draught]]. A 1 m cube of density 600 kg/m³ weighs about 5.9 kN and floats at about 0.59 m in seawater. Fresh water is less dense, about 1000 kg/m³, so the same cube sits deeper, at 0.60 m. A cube as dense as the liquid hovers, and a denser one sinks. So size or weight alone does not decide it.\n\n**In a still liquid, pressure grows with depth as ρgh, and the extra push under an object is an upthrust equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces.[^6]**"
