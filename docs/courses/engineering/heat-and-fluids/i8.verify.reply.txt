Close a valve, and the crossing slides to less flow and more head.\n\nStart with the pump alone, at a steady speed. Its impeller flings the water outward and gives it head. With the outlet shut, no water flows and the head is at its highest.[^3] As the flow grows, the head it gives falls away. Textbooks add that the blades' shape and the losses inside the pump take more of the head as more water passes. Take a pump that gives 30 m at no flow, with its head falling as 30 − 0.04q², where q is the flow in L/s. Now lay a system curve over it: a lift of 10 m plus friction of 0.06q². Where the two meet, the pump gives exactly what the pipe needs, and that is the [[operating point]].[^4] So 30 − 0.04q² = 10 + 0.06q², which gives 0.1q² = 20 and q² = 200. The pump runs at about 14.1 L/s and 22 m. It is the crossing from Linear systems, with curves in place of lines. Why does it stay there? At 10 L/s the pump would give 26 m, while the pipe needs only 16 m. The spare 10 m is an unbalanced push, so the water in the pipe speeds up. Past the crossing the pipe needs more than the pump gives, and the water slows. Either way, it comes back.\n\nThe crossing has limits. Textbooks add that cool water boils if its pressure falls low enough, the other end of the boiling in Temperature and heat. The pressure is lowest where the water enters the impeller. If it boils there, vapour bubbles form, then collapse as the pressure rises further in. That is [[cavitation]], and textbooks add that it rattles like gravel and can pit the impeller. A pump's [[net positive suction head]] required, NPSH for short, is the least absolute pressure it needs at its suction to avoid it.[^5] What the suction offers is the atmosphere's push on the tank, plus the tank's height above the pump, less the suction pipe's friction and the water's boiling pressure. It must stay above what the pump needs. Nearly all its shaft power goes into the trapped water. Say 3.0 kW goes into 5 kg of seawater in the casing. That is 3,000 ÷ (5 × 3850), about 0.16 °C a second, near 9 °C a minute, before any heat escapes.\n\n**Finding where a pump will run**\n- Get the pump's curve of head against flow at its running speed.\n- Work out the system curve: the lift, plus friction growing with the flow squared.\n- Find where they cross. That flow and head are where it will run.\n- Check the efficiency there. Textbooks add that a pump is chosen to run near the top of its efficiency hump.\n- Check that NPSH available beats NPSH required at that flow, with a margin to spare.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** Close the discharge valve, and the flow falls while the outlet gauge rises. On most centrifugal pumps the motor's current falls too. Throttle on the discharge side, not the suction. A suction valve's friction comes straight off the NPSH available. Slowing the pump cuts the flow without burning head in a valve. Textbooks add that the flow goes with the speed and the head with the speed squared. The power goes with the speed cubed, the pump law from Powers and roots. With a lift, the flow falls faster than the speed. Below the speed where its most head matches the lift, it gives no flow at all.\n\n**Worked example:** A pump gives 30 − 0.04q² metres, with q in L/s. The pipe needs 10 + 0.06q². Set them equal: 0.1q² = 20, so q is about 14.1 L/s and the head is 22 m. The water takes 1025 × 9.81 × 0.0141 × 22, about 3.13 kW. Now close the valve until the friction doubles, to 0.12q². The pump settles at about 11.2 L/s and 25 m, and the water takes about 2.81 kW.\n\n**Reading the gauges:** Outlet gauge up, with flow and current down, usually means something on the discharge side is choking the flow. Look for a valve left partly shut or a fouled cooler. Outlet gauge down, with flow and current up, usually means the discharge side has got easier. Look for a valve opened or a leak.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Expecting a centrifugal pump to give its rated flow whatever the pipe. It gives the flow at the crossing.\n- Thinking a closing valve loads a centrifugal pump's motor harder. On most, the current falls.\n- Expecting a closing valve to cut a positive displacement pump's flow. Its flow barely changes, and its pressure climbs.\n- Treating the flow as going with the speed when there is a lift.\n- Fighting cavitation with more speed or a throttled suction.
