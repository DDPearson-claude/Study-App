   "now": "Water going through a narrower part of a pipe speeds up, and its pressure drops while it is there. Both follow from two things you already trust: what goes in must come out, and energy is not made from nothing. Measure that drop, and the narrowing becomes a flow meter.\n\nStart with what goes in. Water barely squeezes, so in a full pipe every litre that enters pushes a litre out further along. The flow, in litres a second, is the same at every section.[^2] The flow through a section is its area × the water's average speed. Each second, a slug of water as long as that speed slides through that area. A pipe of 20 cm², which is 0.002 m², carrying water at 2 m/s passes 0.002 × 2 = 0.004 m³ each second. That is 4 L/s. Narrow it to a throat of 5 cm², a quarter of the area. The same 4 L/s must pass, so the water there moves four times as fast, at 8 m/s. That is [[continuity]]. Area × speed stays the same along the pipe, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂.\n\nSpeeding up needs a push. Along a level pipe, what pushes the water forward is the pressure of the water around it. So, going into the throat, the pressure behind each bit of water must be higher than the pressure in front of it. That means the pressure falls as the speed rises. When water flows into a narrower part, its speed and its kinetic energy go up,[^3] and that energy comes out of the pressure. Pressure is itself energy per cubic metre. Pushing 1 m³ of water in against 1 Pa takes 1 J. Moving water also carries ½ρv² of kinetic energy in each cubic metre, where ρ is its density and v its speed. With no friction, along a level pipe, the two add to the same total. So p + ½ρv² stays constant. That is [[Bernoulli's principle]]. Textbooks add a third share for a pipe that climbs, the ρgh from Pressure in liquids. So the throat's pressure is lower by ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²). In seawater, at 1025 kg/m³,[^1] that is ½ × 1025 × (8² − 2²) = ½ × 1025 × 60, about 30.8 kPa. After the throat the pipe widens, the water slows back to 2 m/s, and the pressure comes back. This is a teaching model with no friction. Real water has some, so a real venturi gets most of its drop back after the throat, not all of it. Many people expect the opposite, that faster water means higher pressure. A hose's jet hits hard because it is fast. Out in the air, the pressure inside the jet is only the air's.\n\nTurn that round and you have a flow meter. The drop grows with the speed squared, so twice the flow gives four times the drop. Measure the drop between the pipe and the throat, and you can work back to the speed, then the flow. A pipe narrowed smoothly and tapped for this is a [[venturi]].[^4] Through our pipe, 4 L/s gives a drop of about 30.8 kPa, and 2 L/s gives a quarter of that, about 7.7 kPa. The flow itself can glide in smooth layers or churn in eddies. Which you get depends on the speed, the bore and how thick the liquid is, which engineers roll into one figure, the Reynolds number. Most sources put smooth flow below about 2000 and eddies above about 4000. They do not agree on one switch point between, and in that band a flow can go either way. Textbooks add that water in a plant's pipes at everyday speeds is usually well into the eddying range.\n\n**Through a narrowing the same flow must go faster, and the energy for that speed comes out of the pressure, so a venturi's pressure drop measures the flow.[^4]**"

   "now": "**Safe first:** A flow meter's pressure tappings and their small pipes are part of the pressure system. Isolate and vent them, as for any pipe under pressure, before you break into them.\n\n**Reading flow from a venturi**\n- Find the pipe's area and the throat's area, in m². 1 cm² is 0.0001 m².\n- Read the drop in pressure from pipe to throat, in Pa. A differential pressure gauge reads it directly.\n- Work out the area ratio, pipe ÷ throat, and square it.\n- Speed in the pipe: v₁ = √(2 × drop ÷ (ρ × (ratio² − 1))).\n- Flow = the pipe's area × v₁, in m³/s. Multiply by 1000 for L/s.\n- Textbooks add that a real meter comes with its maker's correction factor, a little under 1 for a venturi. Multiply the flow by it.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** The flow goes with the square root of the drop. Twice the flow gives four times the drop. So a meter reads poorly at low flow, where the drop is tiny. In seawater, each 10 kPa of drop is about 1 m of column. Textbooks add that an orifice plate, a flat plate with a hole in it, works the same way more cheaply, but loses more of the drop for good.\n\n**Worked example:** Seawater at 1025 kg/m³ runs through a pipe of 20 cm² with a throat of 5 cm². The gauge reads a drop of 7,700 Pa. The area ratio is 4, squared 16, less 1 is 15. v₁ = √(2 × 7,700 ÷ (1025 × 15)), about 1.0 m/s. Flow = 0.002 × 1.0 = 0.002 m³/s, which is 2 L/s.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Expecting the pressure to rise where the pipe narrows. It falls, because the water is speeding up.\n- Leaving out the pipe's own speed. The drop is ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²), not ½ρv₂².\n- Subtracting the speeds before squaring them.\n- Treating the drop as in step with the flow. It goes with the flow squared.\n- Mixing cm² and m².\n- Expecting all of the drop to come back after the throat. A real meter loses a little to friction."
