    "text": "An engine turns heat into work, but only ever part of it. Energy is not made or destroyed on the way. So every kilowatt of fuel leaves as work or as heat carried away to somewhere colder.\n\nStart with a gas in a cylinder under a piston. Heat it, and the gas can push the piston out, doing work. Or it can keep the energy, stored in the gas itself. That stored energy is its [[internal energy]]. The [[first law of thermodynamics]] says the rise in internal energy is the heat put in minus the work the gas does.[^1] Count heat into the gas as plus, and work done by the gas as plus. The work is the area under the gas's line on a pressure–volume chart, as in Calculus fundamentals. At a steady 200 kPa, pushing the piston out by 1 L, 0.001 m³, does 200 J. Put 500 J of heat in while that happens, and the internal energy rises by 300 J. Lock the piston and no work is done, so all 500 J stays in the gas. Push the piston in, and work is done on the gas. That work counts as minus, so it adds to the internal energy.\n\nAn engine runs its gas round a loop, back to where it started. Its internal energy comes back too. So over each loop, the work out equals the heat taken in minus the heat given out. No engine running in a loop can turn all its heat into work.[^2] Some must leave to somewhere colder. People often call it heat loss, but nothing is lost. It is carried away by the cooling water and the exhaust. In a typical engine, those two carry 60 to 70% of the fuel's energy.[^3] Say the fuel brings 1,000 kW and the shaft gives 350 kW. Then 650 kW is rejected. If the cooling water carries 300 kW, the exhaust carries the other 350 kW.\n\nTextbooks add that no engine taking in heat at a hot temperature and rejecting it at a cold one can beat the [[Carnot limit]], 1 − T cold ÷ T hot, both in kelvin. With 800 K and 300 K, that is 62.5%. Only the two temperatures move it. Done in Celsius, the same sum gives about 95%, which is nonsense. How the limit applies to a real diesel is debated. A diesel burns its fuel inside the cylinder, so it is not quite that engine. Large marine two-stroke diesels reject about half of their fuel energy as heat.[^4] On a generator set, the work is the electrical output, and the cooler must carry the cooling flow.\n\n**Every kilowatt of fuel leaves an engine as work, cooling or exhaust, and an engine running in a loop cannot turn all of it into work.[^2]**"

    "text": "**An energy balance on an engine or a generator set**\n- Start from the fuel energy, in kW.\n- Take off the work. On a generator set, that is the electrical output.\n- Split the rest between the cooling and the exhaust.\n- Check the flows add back to the fuel.\n- Check the work's share sits under the Carnot limit, with both temperatures in kelvin.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** In a typical reciprocating engine, cooling and exhaust carry 60 to 70% of the fuel energy.[^3] A cooler is sized on what the engine rejects, not on the set's rating.\n\n**Worked example:** A generator set gives 400 kW of electrical output from 40% of its fuel. So the fuel brings 400 ÷ 0.4 = 1,000 kW, and 600 kW is rejected. Say the cooling takes 30% of the fuel, 300 kW. Seawater warming 10 °C carries 3,850 × 10 = 38,500 J per kg. So the cooler needs 300,000 ÷ 38,500, about 7.8 kg/s of seawater.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Getting the sign of work wrong. Work done by the gas is taken off the heat. Work done on it is added.\n- Treating heat rejected as gone. It all has to go somewhere, and the cooler has to carry its share.\n- Putting Celsius into the Carnot limit. Use kelvin.\n- Multiplying by the efficiency to find the fuel. Divide the output by it."
