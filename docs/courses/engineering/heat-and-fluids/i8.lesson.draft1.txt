    "text": "A centrifugal pump doesn't choose its own flow. It settles at the one flow where the head it gives equals the head the pipe needs. On a graph of head against flow, that is where the pump's curve crosses the system curve. Close a valve, and the crossing slides to less flow and more head.\n\nStart with the pump alone, at a steady speed. Its impeller flings the water outward and gives it head. With the outlet shut, no water flows and the head is at its highest.[^3] As the flow grows, the head it gives falls away. Take a pump that gives 30 m at no flow, with its head falling as 30 − 0.04q², where q is the flow in L/s. Now lay a system curve over it: a lift of 10 m plus friction of 0.06q². Where the two meet, the pump gives exactly what the pipe needs, and that is the [[operating point]].[^1] So 30 − 0.04q² = 10 + 0.06q², which gives 0.1q² = 20 and q² = 200. The pump runs at about 14.1 L/s and 22 m. It is the crossing from Linear systems, with curves in place of lines. Why does it stay there? At 10 L/s the pump would give 26 m, while the pipe needs only 16 m. The spare 10 m is an unbalanced push, so the water in the pipe speeds up. Past the crossing the pipe needs more than the pump gives, and the water slows. Either way, it comes back.\n\nThe crossing has limits. Textbooks add that cool water boils if its pressure falls low enough, the other end of the boiling in Temperature and heat. The pressure is lowest where the water enters the impeller. If it boils there, vapour bubbles form, then collapse as the pressure rises further in. That is [[cavitation]], and textbooks add that it rattles like gravel and pits the impeller. A pump's [[net positive suction head]] required, NPSH for short, is the least absolute pressure it needs at its suction to avoid it.[^5] The suction must offer more. That means the atmosphere on the tank and the tank's height, less the suction pipe's friction. This is a simplified model, with sketched curves in place of a real pump's.\n\nPartly closing the discharge valve adds friction, so the system curve bends up more steeply.[^2] Double the friction, and the crossing slides up the pump's curve to about 11.2 L/s and 25 m. The outlet gauge reads higher, yet less water flows. The power the water takes is ρgQH, as in Pipes, head and pump power. It falls from about 3.13 kW to about 2.81 kW. The flow fell by about a fifth, while the head rose by only about a seventh. The pump's curve flattens near its top, so the head cannot climb far to make up. At a shut valve, the power the motor needs is at or near its least.[^3] As the valve opens and the flow rises, the motor's current rises too.[^4] So choking the flow eases the motor, because the pump moves much less water for only a little more head.\n\n**A centrifugal pump settles where its curve crosses the system curve, so closing a valve moves it to less flow and more head.**[^1]"

    "text": "**Safe first:** Don't start or run a positive displacement pump against a shut discharge valve. Textbooks add that its pressure climbs until something gives, which is why it is fitted with a relief valve. A centrifugal pump can start against a shut discharge valve, where its power is at or near its least.[^3] Don't leave it running shut, though. All its shaft power goes into the trapped water. Say 3.0 kW goes into 5 kg of seawater in the casing. That is 3,000 ÷ (5 × 3850), about 0.16 °C a second, near 9 °C a minute, before any heat escapes.\n\n**Finding where a pump will run**\n- Get the pump's curve of head against flow at its running speed.\n- Work out the system curve: the lift, plus friction growing with the flow squared.\n- Find where they cross. That flow and head are where it will run.\n- Check the efficiency there. Textbooks add that a pump is chosen to run near the top of its efficiency hump.\n- Check that NPSH available beats NPSH required at that flow, with a margin to spare.\n\n**Rules of thumb:** Close the discharge valve, and the flow falls while the outlet gauge rises. On most centrifugal pumps the motor's current falls too. Throttle on the discharge side, not the suction. A suction valve's friction comes straight off the NPSH available. Slowing the pump cuts the flow without burning head in a valve. Textbooks add that the flow goes with the speed, the head with the speed squared and the power with the speed cubed. With a lift, the flow falls faster than the speed. Below the speed where its most head matches the lift, it gives no flow at all.\n\n**Worked example:** A pump gives 30 − 0.04q² metres, with q in L/s. The pipe needs 10 + 0.06q². Set them equal: 0.1q² = 20, so q is about 14.1 L/s and the head is 22 m. The water takes 1025 × 9.81 × 0.0141 × 22, about 3.13 kW. Now close the valve until the friction doubles, to 0.12q². Then 0.16q² = 20, so q² = 125. The pump settles at about 11.2 L/s and 25 m, and the water takes about 2.81 kW.\n\n**Reading the gauges:** Outlet gauge up, with flow and current down, means something on the discharge side is choking the flow. Look for a valve left partly shut or a fouled cooler. Outlet gauge down, with flow and current up, means the discharge side has got easier. Look for a valve opened or a leak.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Expecting a centrifugal pump to give its rated flow whatever the pipe. It gives the flow at the crossing.\n- Thinking a closing valve loads a centrifugal pump's motor harder. On most, the current falls.\n- Expecting a closing valve to cut a positive displacement pump's flow. Its flow barely changes, and its pressure climbs.\n- Treating the flow as going with the speed when there is a lift.\n- Fighting cavitation with more speed or a throttled suction. Both eat the margin. Raise the suction level or cut the flow instead."
