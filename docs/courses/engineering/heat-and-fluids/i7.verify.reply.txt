   "now": "A pump has to give the water two things. It must lift it from one surface to the other. It must also push it through the pipe against friction. Both are measured as a height of liquid, called head, and the friction part grows with the flow squared. Head × flow then sets the power the pump needs, and so the current its motor draws.\n\nStart from Pressure in liquids. A column of seawater h metres tall presses ρgh on its base. Turn that round, and any pressure reads as a height: h = p ÷ ρg. In seawater, at 1025 kg/m³,[^1] 100 kPa holds up 100,000 ÷ (1025 × 9.81), about 9.9 m. That height is [[head]]. A pump's head is the height it can raise the liquid, in metres.[^3] Head is also energy. Lifting a weight of 1 N through 1 m takes 1 J. So each metre of head is 1 J given to every newton of liquid.\n\nWith both tanks open to the air, the head a pipe needs has two parts. The lift is the height between the two water surfaces. While the tanks' levels hold, it is the same at any flow, even none. The friction head is the energy lost on the way. It depends on the pipe's length and size, and on its bends and valves.[^4] In eddying flow it grows with the square of the water's speed.[^5] One way to picture why: moving water carries ½ρv² of kinetic energy in every cubic metre, as in Fluids in motion. Over each stretch of pipe, the eddies turn a share of it into heat. Twice the speed carries four times the energy, so four times as much is lost. Textbooks write it as Darcy's rule: friction head = f × (L ÷ D) × v² ÷ 2g. L is the length and D the bore, both in metres. f is a friction factor, set mostly by how rough the pipe is for its bore. Say f is 0.02. Then 50 m of 50 mm pipe at 2 m/s loses 0.02 × 1000 × 2² ÷ 19.62, about 4.1 m. At 4 m/s it loses about 16.3 m. Many expect twice the speed to lose twice the head. It loses four times. Add the lift and the friction at every flow, and you get the [[system curve]]. It starts at the lift and bends upward as the flow squared. This is a simplified model. Real pipes add bends and valves, and a real f shifts a little with speed. If the far tank is under pressure, that pressure ÷ ρg adds to the lift.\n\nNow the power. Each second the pump moves Q cubic metres of water, which weigh ρgQ newtons. It raises each newton through H metres of head. So the power it gives the water is ρgQH watts, the [[hydraulic power]]. Seawater at 10 L/s against 20 m of head takes 1025 × 9.81 × 0.01 × 20, about 2.01 kW. The pump wastes some of what its shaft gives it, so the shaft must give more. At 70% efficiency that is 2.01 ÷ 0.7, about 2.87 kW.[^2] The motor wastes some too, so it draws more again from the supply.[^6] At 90% that is about 3.19 kW. Each efficiency divides, because each stage takes in more than it passes on. On 440 V three-phase at a power factor of 0.85, that is about 4.9 A on the ammeter.\n\n**A pipe needs head for its lift plus friction that grows with the flow squared, and that head × the flow sets the pump's power and its motor's current.**"

Isolate and vent its pipework too, as for any pipe under pressure.\n\n**Working out the power a pump needs**\n- Find the lift: the height between the two water surfaces, in metres. If the far end is under pressure, add that pressure ÷ ρg.\n- Find the speed: the flow in m³/s ÷ the bore's area in m².\n- Friction head = f × (L ÷ D) × v² ÷ 2g, with L and D both in metres. Add the loss in bends and valves where you know it.\n- Total head H = lift + friction head.\n- Hydraulic power = ρ × g × Q × H, in watts, with Q in m³/s.\n- Shaft power = hydraulic power ÷ the pump's efficiency.\n- Motor input = shaft power ÷ the motor's efficiency.\n- Line current = motor input ÷ (√3 × line voltage × power factor).\n\n**Rules of thumb:** About 10 kPa of seawater pressure is 1 m of head. Twice the flow needs about four times the friction head. At the same flow, half the bore gives about 32 times the friction. A system that is mostly lift needs much the same head at any flow. A system that is mostly friction needs far more head as the flow climbs.\n\n**Worked example:** A pump lifts seawater at 10 L/s, which is 0.01 m³/s, against 20 m of head. The hydraulic power is 1025 × 9.81 × 0.01 × 20, about 2.01 kW. With the pump at 70%, the shaft must give about 2.87 kW. With the motor at 90%, it draws about 3.19 kW. On 440 V three-phase at a power factor of 0.85, the line current is 3,190 ÷ (1.732 × 440 × 0.85), about 4.9 A. To check it, pump makers often work in m³/h: kW = Q × H × relative density ÷ (367.46 × efficiency).[^2] 10 L/s is 36 m³/h, so 36 × 20 × 1.025 ÷ (367.46 × 0.7) gives about 2.87 kW again.\n\n**Common mistakes**\n- Multiplying by an efficiency instead of dividing. The motor takes in more than the shaft gives out.\n- Treating friction as growing in step with the flow.
