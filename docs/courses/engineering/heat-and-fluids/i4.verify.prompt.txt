Topic: Heat and fluids, from boiling water to pumps

This lesson: i4 "Heat into work: the first law and engines" — Heat in equals work out plus the rise in a gas's stored energy, so an engine's fuel energy splits into work, cooling and exhaust. [quantity]
Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i5 Pressure in liquids, and floating — Pressure in a liquid grows with depth alone, density × g × depth, and the extra push under an object is the upthrust that floats it.
- i6 Fluids in motion — Flow through a narrowing speeds up, and the energy for that speed comes out of the pressure, so a narrowed pipe, a venturi, measures flow.
- i7 Pipes, head and pump power — A pipe needs head, a height of liquid, for its lift plus friction that grows with flow squared, and head times flow sets pump power.
- i8 Where the pump settles, and its limits — A centrifugal pump settles where its curve of head against flow crosses the pipe's, so closing a valve moves it to less flow, more head.

Notes (claims, and the sources that support them):
- The first law defines the change in a system's internal energy as the heat transferred into it minus the work it does. [R1]
- It is impossible to convert all heat from a reservoir into work in a cyclical process that returns the system to its original state. [R2]
- In a reciprocating engine, the exhaust gas and cooling systems generally carry 60 to 70 percent of the inlet fuel energy. [R3]
- For large marine two-stroke engines, about half of the fuel energy is lost as waste heat (a 2025 marine paper). [R4]
- The energy needed to change a material's temperature depends on its mass, its specific heat capacity and the temperature change wanted. [R5]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For water, 4,200 J raises 1 kg by 1 °C. [R6]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Latent heat is energy that enters or leaves during a phase change without any temperature change, so melting or boiling happens at a steady temperature. [R7]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Water's heat of fusion is 334 J/g and its heat of vaporization is 2260 J/g, so boiling away a kilogram takes about 2,260 kJ. [R8]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- For a fixed amount of gas at constant volume, pressure is proportional to absolute (kelvin) temperature. [R9]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- At constant temperature, halving a fixed amount of gas's volume doubles its pressure. [R10]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Absolute pressure is gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure. [R11]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A larger air receiver prevents pressure fluctuation and frequent compressor motor starts. [R12]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- The atmosphere pushes with about 10^5 Pa, 100 kPa (the weight of 10 m of seawater); 1 bar is 100 kPa (i3, a cited constant). [R13]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Cited constant: the specific gas constant of air is 287 J/(kg K), for pV = mRT (i3). [R14]  (from an idea this one builds on)

- A receiver collects condensate and water from the air after the compressor, which has to be drained (i3). [R18]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Before work on a compressed air system, isolate it from all pressure sources and vent it completely to atmosphere (HSE HSG39; i3). [R19]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- A pressure gauge shows that pressure is there but does not prove it has all gone; confirm zero pressure at an open vent before breaking into a system (HSE HSG253; i3). [R20]  (from an idea this one builds on)
- Specific heat capacity is how much energy it takes to warm 1 kg by 1 degree, and water's is 4,200 J per kg per degree Celsius. [R6]  (whole topic)
