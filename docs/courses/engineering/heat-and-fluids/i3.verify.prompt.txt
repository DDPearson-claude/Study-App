  "text": "A trapped gas pushes on every wall around it. Squeeze it or warm it, and that push changes by a simple rule of proportion. The catch is where you count from. The rule only works when pressure and temperature are counted from true zero. One way to picture it: the push is the gas's molecules striking the walls. Halve the space and each one strikes twice as often. Warm them and they strike harder and more often.\n\nA pressure gauge open to the air reads zero. Yet the air is pushing on it at about 1 bar. So a gauge reads how far you are above the atmosphere. That is [[gauge pressure]]. Add the atmosphere and you get [[absolute pressure]], counted from no push at all.[^1] You met Boyle's law in Proportional reasoning. Hold a gas's temperature and halve its volume, and its pressure doubles.[^2] It is the absolute pressure that doubles. A gauge reading 1 bar means the gas pushes with 2 bar in all. The gauge just leaves out the atmosphere's share. Halve the volume and the gas pushes with 4 bar absolute. So the gauge now reads 3 bar, not 2.\n\nTemperature has the same trap. Lock the piston so the volume cannot change, then warm the gas. Its pressure climbs in a straight line. Carry that line on to colder and colder gas, and it reaches zero pressure at about −273 °C. That is absolute zero, where the kelvin scale starts. So with the volume held, the pressure is in proportion to the kelvin temperature, not the Celsius one.[^3] Warm a locked cylinder from 20 °C to 40 °C. The Celsius figure doubles, but 293 K to 313 K is only about 7% more. So the pressure rises about 7%.\n\nWhat happens also depends on what is held. Let the piston float under a steady load instead, and warm the gas from 300 K to 400 K. Now the pressure stays the same, and 3 L grows to 4 L, in step with the kelvin temperature.
