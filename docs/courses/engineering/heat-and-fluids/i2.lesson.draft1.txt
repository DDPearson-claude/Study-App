    "text": "Left to itself, heat moves from hotter to colder. A loaded cable makes heat in its copper, and that heat has to get out through the insulation into the air. The copper warms until the heat leaving each second matches the heat made. Its rise above the room is then the heat made × how hard the way out is.\n\nStart with something you have felt. Touch a steel rail and a wooden one in the same room. Both sit at room temperature, yet the steel feels colder. Heat leaves your fingers faster into the metal.[^3] Nothing called cold flows into your hand. What you feel is how fast your skin is losing heat, not the rail's temperature. So how fast heat moves is one question. How much heat it takes to warm something, from Temperature and heat, is another.\n\nNow the cable. Say it has 8 mΩ in each metre and carries 50 A. It makes I²R = 0.008 × 50² = 20 W in each metre. Textbooks add that heat moves in three ways: conduction through solids, convection as warmed air or water carries it away, and radiation. At switch-on the copper is at room temperature, so almost no heat leaves. All 20 W goes into warming it. As it warms, the gap to the room grows, and more heat flows out. So the warming slows. At some temperature the heat leaving matches the heat made,[^1] and the copper stops rising. How hard the way out is gets a number, its [[thermal resistance]]: degrees of rise for each watt flowing out of each metre, in K·m/W. For the air around a cable, it is the surface's rise above the room divided by the heat made in each metre.[^2] Textbooks add that layers in a row add, like resistors in series. Say the insulation is 0.5 and the air 1.5, so 2 K·m/W in all. The steady rise is 20 × 2 = 40 °C, so in a 30 °C room the copper settles at 70 °C. Most of that drop, 30 °C, is into the air, because the air is the harder layer. Wrap the cable in 2 K·m/W more and the rise doubles to 80 °C. To hold 70 °C, the heat made must halve. Heat made goes with current squared, so the current falls to 0.707 of 50 A, about 35 A. In a 40 °C room only 30 °C of headroom is left, so the current falls to about 43 A. That is the reason a cable in insulation or a hot room carries less.\n\nHow long it takes is a separate question. The cable's [[heat capacity]] is the energy each metre needs to warm by 1 °C, its mc. Say 300 J per °C. Thermal resistance × heat capacity is the time constant, 2 × 300 = 600 s, 10 minutes. After one time constant the copper has made 63% of its rise, about 55 °C. By about five, 50 minutes, it has all but settled. Double the heat capacity and it settles at the same 70 °C, twice as slowly. Thermal resistance sets where it settles. Heat capacity only sets how long it takes. This is a simplified model, and a real cable's losses and air resistance shift a little as it warms.\n\n**A loaded cable warms until the heat leaving matches the heat made,[^1] so its steady rise is the heat made × the thermal resistance of the way out, and its heat capacity sets only how long it takes to get there.**"
