  "text": "Water going through a narrower part of a pipe speeds up, and its pressure drops while it is there. Both follow from two things you already trust: what goes in must come out, and energy is not made from nothing. Measure that drop, and the narrowing becomes a flow meter.\n\nStart with what goes in. Water barely squeezes, so in a full pipe every litre that enters pushes a litre out further along. The flow, in litres a second, is the same at every section.[^2] The flow through a section is its area × the water's speed. Each second, a slug of water as long as the speed slides through that area. A pipe of 20 cm², which is 0.002 m², carrying water at 2 m/s passes 0.002 × 2 = 0.004 m³ each second. That is 4 L/s. Narrow it to a throat of 5 cm², a quarter of the area. The same 4 L/s must pass, so the water there moves four times as fast, at 8 m/s. That is [[continuity]]. Area × speed stays the same along the pipe, so A₁v₁ = A₂v₂.\n\nSpeeding up needs a push. Along a level pipe, what pushes the water forward is the pressure of the water around it. So, going into the throat, the pressure behind each bit of water must be higher than the pressure in front of it. That means the pressure falls as the speed rises. When water flows into a narrower part, its speed and its kinetic energy go up,[^3] and that energy comes out of the pressure. Pressure is itself energy per cubic metre.
