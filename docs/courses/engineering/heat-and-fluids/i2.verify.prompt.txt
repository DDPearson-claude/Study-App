Later ideas in the course (every claim must stay true after Dan has learned each of them):
- i3 Gases, pressure and the air receiver — Gas sums need absolute pressure and kelvin, because a gas's pressure, volume and temperature are in proportion only when measured from true zero.
- i4 Heat into work: the first law and engines — Heat in equals work out plus the rise in a gas's stored energy, so an engine's fuel energy splits into work, cooling and exhaust.
- i5 Pressure in liquids, and floating — Pressure in a liquid grows with depth alone, density × g × depth, and the extra push under an object is the upthrust that floats it.

  "text": "Left to itself, heat moves from hotter to colder. You have felt it. Touch a steel rail and a wooden one in the same room, and the steel feels colder. Both sit at room temperature. Heat simply leaves your fingers faster into the metal.[^1] Textbooks add that metal passes heat far more easily than wood. Nothing called cold flows into your hand. What you feel is how fast heat leaves your skin, not the rail's temperature. So how fast heat moves is one question. How much it takes to warm something, the mc from Temperature and heat, is another.\n\nA loaded cable makes heat in its copper, and that heat has to get out through the insulation into the air. Say the cable has 8 mΩ in each metre and carries 50 A. Its I²R losses are 0.008 × 50² = 20 W in each metre. Textbooks add that heat moves in three ways: conduction through solids, convection as warmed air or water carries it away, and radiation. At switch-on the copper is at room temperature, so no heat leaves yet. All 20 W goes into warming it. As it warms, the gap to the room grows, and more heat flows out. So the warming slows. At some temperature the heat leaving matches the losses,[^2] and the copper stops rising. How hard the way out is gets a number, its [[thermal resistance]]. For the air around a cable, it is the surface's rise above the room divided by the losses in each metre.[^3] Its unit is K·m/W, degrees of rise for each watt leaving each metre. So the heat leaving is the rise ÷ the thermal resistance. Once settled, the heat leaving is the losses. So the steady rise is losses × thermal resistance. Textbooks add that layers in a row add, like resistors in series. Say the insulation is 0.5 and the air 1.5, so 2 K·m/W in all. The rise is 20 × 2 = 40 °C, so in a 30 °C room the copper settles at 70 °C. The air takes 30 °C of that drop, because it is the harder layer.\n\nWrap the cable in 2 K·m/W more and the rise doubles to 80 °C. More insulation does not just slow the warming. It raises where the copper settles. To hold 70 °C, the losses must halve. Losses go with current squared, so the current falls to 0.707 of 50 A, about 35 A. A 40 °C room leaves only 30 °C of headroom, so the current falls to about 43 A. These are the reasons behind Ca for a hot room and Ci for thermal insulation. A fan works the other way. Moving air carries heat away faster, so it lowers the air's thermal resistance.\n\nHow long it takes is a separate question. The cable's [[heat capacity]] is the energy each metre needs to warm by 1 °C, its mc. Say 300 J per °C. Thermal resistance × heat capacity is the time constant: 2 × 300 = 600 s, 10 minutes. Its units show why it is a time, since °C per W × J per °C is J per W, which is seconds. After one time constant the copper has made 63% of its rise, so it is at about 55 °C. By about five, 50 minutes, it has all but settled. Double the heat capacity and it settles at the same 70 °C, twice as slowly. Thermal resistance sets where it settles, and the time too. Heat capacity sets only the time. This is a simplified model, and a real cable's losses and air resistance shift a little as it warms.\n\n**A loaded cable warms until the heat leaving matches the losses,[^2] so its steady rise is losses × the thermal resistance of the way out, and its heat capacity sets only how long it takes to get there.**"
