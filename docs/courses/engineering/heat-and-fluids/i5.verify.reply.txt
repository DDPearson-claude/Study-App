   "now": "Still liquid presses on everything in it, and it presses harder the deeper you go. The reason is weight. The pressure at any depth comes from the weight of the liquid above it.[^3] Picture a column of liquid standing on a patch of 1 m², reaching up to the surface. Its height is the depth, h, so its volume is h × 1 m². Its mass is that volume times the liquid's [[density]], its mass per cubic metre.[^4] Its weight is that mass times g, 9.81 newtons for each kilogram. All that weight rests on the 1 m² patch. So the pressure there is density × g × depth, written p = ρgh, where ρ, the Greek letter rho, is the density. Textbooks add that a liquid barely squeezes, so its density is near enough the same all the way down. That is why each metre adds the same pressure. Seawater's density is about 1025 kg/m³.[^2] So 10 m of it adds 1025 × 9.81 × 10, about 100.6 kPa. That is about as much again as the whole atmosphere.[^1] With the surface open to the air, a gauge reads that ρgh. Add the atmosphere's 100 kPa for the absolute pressure, as with gases.\n\nLook at what ρgh leaves out. The tank's width is not in it, and nor is its shape. Join a narrow tank, a wide one and a flaring one at the bottom, and fill them. The liquid settles at one level in all three, and at any one depth the probe reads the same in each. If one tank's bottom pressed harder, it would push liquid through the join until the levels matched. In the flaring tank, the sloping walls hold up the extra liquid's weight. At any point the pressure pushes every way at once, square to whatever surface it meets. It needs no movement either. Still water presses because it has weight.\n\nNow hold a cube 1 m on a side under seawater, its top 2 m down. The pushes on its sides match in pairs, so they cancel. Its top is pushed down by about 20.1 kPa. Its bottom is 3 m down, so it is pushed up by about 30.2 kPa. On each 1 m² face, the bottom wins by about 10.1 kN. That net push up is the [[upthrust]]. The extra pressure at the bottom is ρg × the cube's height, and it acts on the bottom's area. Height × area is the cube's volume, so the upthrust is ρg × that volume. That is the weight of the seawater the cube has pushed aside. So an object partly or wholly in a liquid feels an upthrust equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces.[^5] Push the cube deeper and both pushes grow by the same amount, so the upthrust stays the same. What the cube is made of does not change it either.\n\nWhether it floats is a contest between its weight and that upthrust. Its weight depends on its own density, and the upthrust on the liquid's. A cube less dense than the liquid rises. As it comes out of the surface it displaces less, so the upthrust falls. It settles where the upthrust equals its weight. The depth of its bottom below the surface is its [[draught]]. A 1 m cube of density 600 kg/m³ has a mass of 600 kg, so it weighs about 5.9 kN. It must push aside 600 kg of seawater, which is 600 ÷ 1025, about 0.59 m³. On its 1 m² base, that is a draught of about 0.59 m. Fresh water is less dense, about 1000 kg/m³, so the same cube sits deeper, at 0.60 m. A cube exactly as dense as the liquid hovers, and a denser one sinks. So size or weight alone does not decide it. A steel hull floats because its mass is less than the mass of water that would fill all the space its outside encloses.\n\n**In a still liquid, pressure grows with depth as ρgh, and the extra push under an object is an upthrust equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces.[^5]**"
