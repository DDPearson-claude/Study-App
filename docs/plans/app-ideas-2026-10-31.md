# New feature ideas for My University

There are 36 ideas here, numbered so you can reply with the numbers you want. Where two lenses came up with the same idea, they are merged into one and the lenses are named. The ideas are in no particular order.

## Working problems

**1. Which rule? Mixed problems without the label**: mixed problems stop telling you which course and lesson each problem comes from, so you have to spot the method yourself.
*From: Learning science and The app as built.*
What it does for you: Choosing the right rule is the hard part of any real problem, and today the label does that step for you. Mixing problems only pays off when you have to choose.
In the app: A switch on mixed problems reads "Hide where each problem is from". With it on, a problem shows only "Problem 3 of 10", and the course and lesson appear with the answer. There is also an optional row of three or four rule choices above the answer box: the right lesson or formula-sheet rule, plus look-alikes from the same mix. A wrong pick never counts against you. The answer panel says what tells the rules apart, and the end line reads "Right rule 8 of 10, right figure 7 of 10".
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**2. Problem sets that know the lesson's slips**: when your wrong figure is a slip the lesson expected, such as leaving out the 4.44, the app names it before your second go.
What it does for you: The slips each lesson names, with their hints, were written and fact-checked when the course was built. Today they only show inside the lesson. In problem sets you get a bare "check your working".
In the app: A wrong answer in a problem set or mixed problems that matches a known slip shows that slip's hint in red. You still get your one retry. With new numbers the slips are left out, because they belong to the lesson's own figures.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls. The courses are updated one at a time as their guides are re-run.

**3. Ask Claude about this problem**: after any problem, a "Why?" row opens Ask Claude, which already knows the problem, the worked answer and what you typed.
What it does for you: The moment after a second wrong answer is when you have a question, such as "why 1.732 and not 3?". Today Ask Claude cannot be reached from problem sets, mixed problems or the read-through.
In the app: Once the answer is shown, a chat row appears under the problem. The read-through gets the chat icon in its bar too. Your questions are saved and shown in the Book as now. A small fix in the same area means a list written straight after an intro line no longer runs into one paragraph.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: one Claude call per question, only when you ask.

**4. Spot the slip**: now and then a problem comes already worked "by a new starter", with one line wrong. You find the line and type the right figure.
What it does for you: It trains you to check someone else's working step by step, which you will do a lot as you move up. The wrong lines come from slips the lesson already names, so nothing is made up.
In the app: About one card in five in a problem set is marked "Find the mistake". You tap the wrong line and type the correct final figure. The answer panel then gives that slip's hint. Every line is checked by the app's calculator before it is shown.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one Claude call per lesson, once, to set out the working lines. After that, none.

**5. Fill in the gap**: after a miss, the next problem like it comes part-worked, with one line fewer filled in each time until you are doing it from scratch.
What it does for you: It bridges the gap between "I followed the working" and "I can do it alone on paper", which is too big a jump for a new kind of problem.
In the app: It only appears after a miss. The next problem from that lesson shows all but the last line done. The one after leaves two lines blank, and then the whole problem is yours. Each blank is a typed figure checked by the app. A right first-time answer ends it.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: the same one call per lesson as idea 4, so it costs less if both are chosen.

**6. What if it doubles?**: after a right answer, one quick follow-up asks what happens if one value changes, for example "the frequency drops to 40 Hz: does the flux go up or down, and by how much?"
What it does for you: Getting the figure right shows you can use a formula. Knowing how the quantities move together is what lets you sanity-check a reading on a system you have never been taught.
In the app: An optional setting, "Add what-if questions". After a correct answer you choose up, down or the same, then give a rough factor. The panel shows the reason in one line, such as "flux goes as 1 over f, so 1.5 times". One tap opens the lesson's interactive so you can watch it move. The app works out the answer from the checked formula.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: no extra Claude calls. It uses the formula the new-numbers feature already fetches.

**7. Ballpark first**: before you work a problem properly, put down a rough figure, such as about 10, 100 or 1000.
What it does for you: Many slips are size slips: mWb for Wb, peak for rms, a missing root 3. A quick estimate catches them, and it tells a slip in the working apart from a wrong idea.
In the app: An optional "Roughly?" line sits above the answer box. You can type a figure or tap a power of ten. The panel says how close you were and, if it applies, "your estimate was right; a slip crept into the working". The end line reads "Ballparks within 2x: 7 of 10".
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**8. Work it backwards**: an interactive you already know turns up with one control hidden and set to a secret value. You read the meters and work out the hidden setting.
What it does for you: Every problem today runs forwards. Fault-finding and many exam questions run the other way: here is the reading, so what must the component be?
In the app: Each finished lesson gets a "Work it backwards" row, and mixed problems get it as an option. You see the outputs, work on paper and type the hidden value. The answer is the interactive's own setting, so nothing is invented. Any control that cannot be pinned down from the readings is skipped.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**9. The job that runs across courses**: a three-to-five-step job, such as sizing a charger, then its cable, then the heat it gives off, where each answer feeds the next.
What it does for you: It is practice at carrying a figure through several steps across subjects. If you slip early, later steps are marked against your own figure, as an examiner would mark them.
In the app: Each step is a typed answer. A wrong step marks the later ones "carried forward" from your figure. The end shows which step broke and which methods were still sound. Settings are generic workshop or industrial ones. Jobs are kept so you can redo them with new numbers.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about one Claude call to write each new job. Redoing a job with new numbers costs none.

**10. Mark a whole sheet from one photo**: get six mixed problems on one page, work them on paper at your own pace, then photograph the whole sheet once.
What it does for you: You no longer pick up the phone after every question. That suits a proper sitting with pen and paper. There is no clock and no exam framing.
In the app: A "Paper sheet" option on mixed problems and on course pages shows six numbered problems, which you can save. You take one photo, and the app shows the figures it read. You confirm or correct each one, and the app marks them against the lesson's answers and slips. Any wrong one offers "check my working".
Effort: about a day. Running cost: one image call per sheet, plus one for new numbers if used.

**11. Number fluency, without a calculator**: five-minute sets of the small sums that slow everything down, such as prefixes, powers of ten, rough estimates, rearranging and percentages.
What it does for you: Exam marks are often lost on the small steps. These are drilled on their own, so they stop taking up thinking room in bigger problems.
In the app: You reach it from the Library and the calculator sheet. Ten questions like "4.7 kΩ in Ω" or "make I the subject of P = I²R" use units from your courses. A named slip shows when you go wrong, such as "out by 1000: check the prefix". You can see your own times over recent weeks. There are no targets, no streaks and no clock you have to beat.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

## Making it stick

**12. Back until right, then again cold** (reworks an earlier idea): a problem you miss comes back at the end of the same set with new numbers until you get it right once. Weeks later, the lesson's set is offered "cold".
What it does for you: It brings spaced practice back through the problem sets you already like, instead of the review box. There are no cards, no daily queue and no schedule to keep up.
In the app: Missed problems come back as twins at the end of a set. On the course page, a lesson whose set was last done about 2, then 6, then 12 weeks ago shows a quiet "Try it cold" link with your last score. Today can list up to three such lessons. There is no count, no "overdue" and no red.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: no Claude calls beyond new numbers, if you use those.

**13. Problems that remember the ones you missed** (reworks an earlier idea): each problem set keeps which problems you got wrong, not just your score.
What it does for you: The problems that beat you are the best ones to practise next, and today they are thrown away. There is no separate mistake log to read, just your own misses back in the problem set.
In the app: The course page row reads "Last time: 4 of 6 · Try the 2 you missed", with the same numbers or new ones. A problem leaves the list once you get it right first time. If you used the photo check, it can note "a slip" or "a method problem" beside the miss. Nothing shows on the Library or the tabs.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**14. Today: a short paper to work** (reworks an earlier idea): Today puts together one short paper for pen and paper. You get the lesson you left open, then five problems from missed ones and lessons you finished a while ago.
What it does for you: Spaced practice happens without you choosing courses and counts each time. It is one tap away, with nothing due and nothing counting down.
In the app: A "Today's paper" card sits under "Pick up where you left off". The same paper stays for the day if you leave and come back. "Not today" hides it until tomorrow. It does not show if nothing is finished, and it works best alongside idea 13.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about one Claude call a day for new numbers. It can fall back to the lesson's own numbers.

**15. Blank page, then the sheet**: before opening a course's formula sheet, write everything you remember on a blank page and photograph it.
What it does for you: It pulls the rules out of your own head before you see them, which is what makes them stick. Rereading a familiar sheet only feels like learning.
In the app: A first button on the formula sheet reads "Write it from memory first". Claude ticks off what you had, and the sheet opens with gaps and errors in amber and "You had 14 of 20" at the top. It shows how it read each line, so a misread is easy to spot. Next time it can say "Last time: missed the 4.44 rule". No photo is kept.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one image call each time, plus one to make the sheet if it does not exist yet.

**16. Sketch it back**: at the end of a lesson, draw its key picture from memory, such as the phasor diagram, the B-H loop or the circuit, and photograph it.
What it does for you: In electrical work, understanding often lives in a diagram. Drawing it yourself, rather than only looking, helps it stay.
In the app: An optional "Sketch it" button sits at the end of a lesson and in the read-through. Claude returns up to three points (has it, missing, the wrong way round), with the lesson's picture beside yours. It is offered only where a lesson has one key picture.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one image call each time.

**17. Say it back on paper**: at the end of a lesson, explain the idea in your own words, typed or on paper and photographed.
What it does for you: None of the ready-made lessons has a say-it-back yet, so "In your own words" in the Book is nearly empty. This fills it the way you like to work.
In the app: "Say it back" appears on the finish screens of the lesson and the read-through, as a question such as "Why does a battery give fewer ampere-hours at a faster rate?". Claude marks it only against what the lesson taught. It is saved to the Book, and "Another go" works as now. You are never prompted again.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one Claude call per explanation.

**18. Your own one-page summary**: when you finish a course, write its one-page summary by hand and photograph it.
What it does for you: Boiling a course down to one page in your own hand is what makes it yours. The app checks it, and the page doesn't get lost in a pile.
In the app: A "Write your own page" row sits on a finished course and in its dossier. Claude replies in one note: what's there, up to three things missing, and any rule written wrongly in red. A small copy of the photo becomes the dossier's front page, marked "In your hand". You can retake it, and the newest is kept.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one image call each time.

## Explaining and writing

**19. Teach the new starter**: Claude plays a keen apprentice who has one of the usual wrong ideas. You put them right, and they only get it when your explanation actually fixes the mistake.
*From: Learning science and Wildcards.*
What it does for you: It practises finding and fixing someone else's wrong idea, not just stating your own. Instructing juniors is a real part of the next steps on your path.
In the app: A button by say-it-back and in the dossier chapter. The apprentice's wrong ideas come from the lesson's own named slips. It asks, never teaches, and gives at most a follow-up or two. It ends with a short verdict on which key points you got across, saved in the Book's "In your own words". The apprentice is a generic new electrician.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about two to five Claude calls per go.

**20. Brief a new hand**: after a lesson, write a one-minute brief teaching its key idea. It has an aim, two key points, a "watch out" line and one question to check understanding.
What it does for you: It practises passing an idea on to someone who knows less, in a short fixed shape. That is the leadership side of the same knowledge.
In the app: An optional card on finished lessons has four short boxes and a counter aiming at about 150 words. Claude replies in two parts: "what a newcomer would get" and "where they would get lost". It is kept in the Book. It uses a generic structure, not any Navy briefing format.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: one Claude call per brief.

**21. Answer what the question asks**: short written questions on finished lessons, each led by an exam verb (state, describe, explain, compare, evaluate). Claude marks whether you did what the verb asks.
What it does for you: Your next assessments mark written answers, and at Level 4 just naming something where you were asked to "describe" fails the criterion. The app checks your figures well but never the depth of your writing.
In the app: A "Write it" tab sits beside a lesson's problem set. Each question shows a line on what its verb needs, such as "explain = the cause and the effect, joined by because". The marks show as ticks, never a grade, and answers are kept in the Book. The higher verbs are kept to engineering and electrical courses.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about one Claude call per answer.

**22. Write up the test**: after playing with a lesson's interactive, log a few readings and write a short test report: aim, method, results and what it shows.
What it does for you: It turns what you saw on the bench-like interactives into a written record. Job write-ups and test reports are an everyday technician skill, and report writing is a Level 4 unit.
In the app: A "Log this reading" button saves up to five snapshots of the settings and readings into a table, and every figure in it is yours. Four short boxes follow, marked against a fixed report checklist. The finished report can go into the course's dossier as "Your test report". That would be the one place the dossier prints your own words, so it needs your yes.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one Claude call per report, a bit larger than a quiz answer.

**23. Talk me through it**: pick a finished course and Claude questions you like an assessor. It asks open "why" and "what would you check" questions and follows up when an answer is thin.
*From: Career and exam path, and Wildcards.*
What it does for you: Several public routes on your path end in a viva, a professional discussion or a structured interview. Nothing in the app makes you explain under a little pressure to someone who pushes back.
In the app: A button on courses with three or more finished lessons. You get three to six questions, typed or dictated, with an optional two-minute bar that only shows time passing. At the end there are short notes on clarity, accuracy and order, a "say it better" version of one sentence, and links to the lessons to reread. There is no score. It is kept in the Book.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about 5 to 10 Claude calls per session.

**24. Assignment brief unpacker (Level 4)**: type in a Level 4 assignment's criteria and get a checklist. It shows what each verb demands and a word budget per criterion. Claude then reads your draft against each one but never writes it for you.
What it does for you: Long written assignments, met criterion by criterion within a word count, are the biggest new skill on your path, and nothing in the app touches them.
In the app: A page reached from the Book. You type the criteria, and nothing is fetched. Each criterion gets its verb, what that verb needs, a word share and one question to ask yourself. "Check against the brief" returns ticks and one question per section, never sentences to copy. A word counter tracks each section, and the page states plainly that it never drafts text.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one Claude call to unpack a brief, then one per section you check.

## Joining it up for new topics

**25. What do I already know for this?** (reworks an earlier idea): type any new topic you meet, and the app shows which of your finished lessons and formulas it builds on, and why.
*From: Learning science and Wildcards.*
What it does for you: Seeing that a new topic is mostly ratios and phasors you already know is the step people miss on their own. A full course is too costly for every new topic.
In the app: A "Starting something new?" box on the Library takes a topic name only, with no photos or pasted pages. You get up to six of your lessons, each with a one-line reason, which you can remove. A "new to you" list of terms is clearly labelled as unchecked. Buttons offer two warm-up problems or a mixed set from those lessons. The result is saved as a small toolkit page in the Book. It never teaches the new topic itself.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: one Claude call per topic, with a web search if the "new to you" list is wanted.

**26. Find where I learned it**: type a word like "Peukert" or "per-unit" into the Library look-up and go straight to the lesson and worked example that taught it.
What it does for you: Most of what 33 courses taught can't be found once you've moved on. When a term turns up somewhere new, you can find where you met it.
In the app: Look-up results show under the course rows, for example "Peukert's rule · Batteries, lesson 3 · worked example 2", and open the read-through at that page. Any amber key term in a lesson becomes tappable, showing its one-line meaning, where it was first taught and other lessons that use it. Results fold away after five.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**27. Same maths, different machine**: two problems side by side from different courses that run on the same equation, such as a capacitor discharging and a battery's self-discharge.
What it does for you: A few patterns come back in every topic: exponential decay, proportion, rate of change, energy balance. Noticing them is what carries you into new topics.
In the app: A screen reached from the Library and the Book offers a pair. You solve both on paper, then say in one line what they share. Each "family" gets a small Book page listing every place it turns up. A family is kept only if the app shows the formulas really behave alike, and you can dismiss a pair.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: a one-off Claude call to group the families, then one small call per "what's the same" answer.

**28. What my courses cover**: one page lining up your built and finished courses against public module lists for qualifications like yours.
What it does for you: New courses cost about 10M tokens each, so choosing the next one well matters. This shows gaps such as engineering drawing, fluid power and control principles at a glance.
In the app: A Library page shows each public list with a mark beside every item (built, finished or not covered) and a link to the course. Each list is headed with its source and "This is a public list for a related course, not your course". There are no percentages and no "incomplete" wording.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls in the app. The map is built once in Claude Code from public pages.

## Real jobs and real documents

**29. Call-out: find the fault**: a short fault story in a plain workshop or plant setting. You ask for readings one at a time and name the fault in as few readings as you can.
What it does for you: Your courses teach the fault-finding method, but practice is still one question, one answer. Here you choose which reading to take next.
In the app: You tap test points and readings appear from a table the app has checked against circuit rules. You name the fault and type its value. The end shows your route beside the half-split route. Readings taken count, not speed.
Effort: a week or more. Running cost: one Claude call per scenario, and none during play.

**30. Read the real datasheet**: a problem built on a real public manufacturer's datasheet, such as a cell's discharge table or a motor's catalogue data.
What it does for you: Every number you get today comes tidy. On the job, the hard part is often finding the right value in a dense table with its conditions and footnotes.
In the app: A problem shows an extract from the datasheet and the link, with every quote checked word for word against the page. You find the value, then work the answer. Good ones are kept for reuse. Only text tables from commercial, non-military sources are used.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: a web search, a page fetch and one Claude call per problem.

**31. Planning problems**: a civilian logistics puzzle with fixed speeds, times and loads. You plan it on paper, type the key times, then write two lines on why your plan works.
What it does for you: It is practice at a quick plan with numbers under time pressure, and at explaining a decision. A planning exercise like this is part of the public Admiralty Interview Board, if you go for Upper Yardman.
In the app: You type two or three figures, checked as in a problem set with one retry. Then you fill in a "Why this plan?" box marked against fixed points, and get one "what if" twist, such as a vehicle breaking down. Scenarios are deliberately civilian, and the app never claims to copy the real exercise.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: about two to three Claude calls per scenario.

## Your records and the app itself

**32. Learning record**: a dated record of what you've studied, built automatically from the app, which you can save and attach to a CPD log or portfolio.
What it does for you: Professional registration and Level 4 CPD units want evidence of learning over time. You are already doing hours of it here, but it is kept only as minutes and progress.
In the app: A page in the Book, grouped by month and course, shows the date, the lesson, its own one-line summary, rough minutes and problem-set results if you want them. An optional "one line this week" box is marked "about what you learned, not about your boat or your work", or it could be left out entirely. It saves as a tidy page.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**33. Keep Claude's answer**: when an Ask Claude explanation finally makes something click, tap "Keep this".
What it does for you: Today the answers vanish when the page closes. Asking again costs usage and gives a different answer.
In the app: A small "Keep this" sits under each reply. Kept notes appear in the Book under "Kept from Ask Claude", grouped by course and lesson. The lesson shows a quiet "1 kept note" link. They are labelled as Claude's words and never mixed into the course text.
Effort: about a day. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**34. A formula sheet you can trust and use**: each rule on a course's formula sheet is checked against that course's own worked examples and says so, for example "checked against lesson 3, example 2".
What it does for you: Today the whole sheet is labelled as Claude's summary. Checked rules are ones you can rely on, and you can use them without retyping.
In the app: Checked rules carry a tick, and the rest stay marked "Claude's summary". Tapping a checked rule opens a small form, one box per symbol with its unit, and the app works out the answer. The saved copy keeps the ticks.
Effort: a few days. Running cost: no extra Claude calls for new sheets. Sheets already made would need remaking, at one call each.

**35. Your problem sets record each problem**: in the app, this is the data side of ideas 12, 13 and 14 together.
What it does for you: It lets you choose any one of those three on its own, or all of them, knowing they share the same small change: keeping which problems you missed, not just the score.
In the app: Nothing new to see by itself. It makes the misses list, the "Try it cold" links and Today's paper possible.
Effort: about a day, and shared if more than one of 12, 13 and 14 is chosen. Running cost: no Claude calls.

**36. Shared working lines**: the build that ideas 4 and 5 share. Each lesson's worked examples are set out once as numbered lines, each line checked by the app's calculator.
What it does for you: It lets you weigh "Spot the slip" and "Fill in the gap" together. Choosing one makes the other cheaper.
In the app: Nothing new to see by itself. It feeds the "Find the mistake" cards and the part-worked problems.
Effort: a few days, counted once. Running cost: one Claude call per lesson, once.
